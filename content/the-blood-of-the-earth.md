# The Blood of the Earth

### One book, read at its own scale — a collapse with nobody standing on the far side of it, a frame with an endpoint and no occupant, and the rider the blog's telos rule now owes

*Sixteenth of the `readings`, and the first whose subject is **energy**. Every collapse this blog has described has had a holder in it: [the mechanism post](/meditation-harm/) set out a state that cannot report itself and an appraisal supplied from the far side, and the far side has always been occupied — a group, an operator, a field of persons. [The costume](/the-costume/) drew the boundary that keeps that framework honest, **levers without the mechanism are not predation**, and the boundary presumes a lever-puller. John Michael Greer's *The Blood of the Earth* is a collapse of a different kind — material, energetic, unowned — and its own answer to the question it raises is that nobody is on the far side of it at all. [The machine has no reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/) found a frame with no category for an interior event. This book is about a frame with no category for **its own ending**.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source. Every quotation is verbatim from a complete digital text of the edition, checked character for character, with the source's typographic quotes rendered as straight ASCII, its spaced dashes kept as the source sets them, its italic-spacing artefacts normalised, and elisions marked. The reading of them is an **inference**, offered as one. **The book's theses are not endorsed in either direction** — nothing here adjudicates the peak-oil argument, and nothing here claims that magic does or does not cause anything; §7 sets out what is argued, what is documented, and what would count as evidence. It is also a **polemic by a practitioner**, and §7 reports the practice-level caution it carries and the clinical referral it does not. No living author is diagnosed, and nothing here validates, disqualifies or restricts anyone's practice. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no one.

## 1. The book, and the case the blog has never had

*The Blood of the Earth: An Essay on Magic and Peak Oil* is by John Michael Greer. The edition's copyright page carries what can be quoted rather than inferred — "Published by Scarlet Imprint under the Bibliothèque Rouge banner at the Vernal Equinox 2012", ISBN 978-0-9567203-8-2, design and typography credited to At-Tariq — and its seven chapters, after an Introduction, are the argument's spine: Magical Thinking, Pluto's Republic, Strange Bright Banners, A Lesson in Practical Magic, The Path Between the Pillars, The Myth of the Machine, Begin It Now.[^bote] The pairing is named as an oddity in the book's first sentence:

> "Ceremonial magicians aren't usually known for writing books on the decline and fall of industrial society, and futurists don't normally write books on magic. It so happens that I'm both of these things and write on both topics."[^intro]

**The absence this book sits in is measurable, and it is total.** A case-insensitive search of the blog's published posts returns **0 hits** for "peak oil", **0** for "deindustrial", **0** for "fossil fuel" and **0** for "thermodynamic".[^abs] The blog's subject has all along been a collapse that arrives through a system of persons — a state misread, an appraisal installed, a holder at the exit. It has never described a collapse that arrives through **matter**: a finite planet, a falling energy return, a machine park that cannot be fuelled.

It is not a book the blog has never heard of either: the name appears **once**, in the notes to [the western column](/the-western-column/), as the revisor of a Golden Dawn edition.[^abs] The blog has cited a book he corrected without citing him — which is the ordinary way an absence looks from the inside: not a silence, an adjacency.

**The question this reading exists to answer** is about the blog's machinery rather than about oil:

> **What does the blog's mechanism do with a collapse that has no holder?**

Two answers are available and they are not the same: either the mechanism **requires** a holder, in which case a material collapse is outside its scope; or it describes the shape of a misreading, and a holder is one way to install one.

## 2. What this book claims, and what belongs to a different book

Three of the book's claims are load-bearing, and all three need their exact form, because the author's later vocabulary is more famous than this text.

**One: the non-overshoot thesis.** The prediction is not catastrophe and not rescue but decline, stated with a term that matters: "Peak oil is thus a predicament rather than a problem, since nothing anyone can do will make it go away."[^machine] A **problem** is something with a solution; a **predicament** is something to be lived in:

> "These limitations are not subject to change, or even to negotiation; they define a predicament that we will all have to live with, one way or another, for the rest of humanity's time on this planet."[^banners]

The timescale is "one to three centuries of uneven decline until the curve of overshoot and collapse bottoms out in a dark age that will endure for centuries more".[^banners] And it is worth saying what the book **does not** contain, because the shortest description of Greer's position usually comes from his later work: **"catabolic"** does not appear, **"EROEI"** does not appear — the energy case is put as "the net energy issues and energy subsidies that make the quest for a replacement for fossil fuels an exercise in futility" — and **"singularity"** does not appear at all.[^abs]

**Two: the rejection of both answers.** The book's opening move refuses scientific materialism and metaphysical rescue in the same breath:

> "Neither of these claims is particularly useful – it would be as accurate to say that neither of them is particularly true."[^intro]

The first is the claim that magic is nonsense and has nothing to say about energy; the second, that "metaphysical factors can overturn the ordinary laws of nature".[^intro]

**Three, and it is the one to hold at arm's length: the book subordinates the material to the mental.**

> "The forces that have backed the world's industrial nations into a blind corner, and are keeping them pinned there, have much less to do with strictly material realities than with the realms of mind and consciousness that are the raw materials of magic."[^intro]

That is the sentence the whole argument turns on. The physical limits are real on this account — §7 quotes the passage where magic cannot put oil in the ground — but the *binding constraint* is diagnosed as non-physical: the reason technically solved problems were never implemented.

## 3. The far side, with nobody on it

Here is the chapter of this reading the blog needs most, and it is not the one about energy.

[The mechanism post](/meditation-harm/) describes a state that cannot report itself, and the reason that is dangerous: the appraisal is supplied from **whatever stands on the far side**. In every case study since, the far side has been a party — [a group that builds a frame while the system is open](/manufacturing-the-collapse/), [an operator who arrives inside a frame the target already holds](/the-operative-master/), [a field of persons that carries the collapse between generations](/undreaming-wetiko/). *The Blood of the Earth* runs the same shape with **an empty far side**, and it does it twice.

**At the scale of a person.** The book's model of the self is Platonic and threefold: a reasoning mind, a **biological self** of appetites, and a **social self** — Plato's second horse, *thumos*, "the part that responds nonrationally to praise or blame from others, and more generally is guided by the pressures and influences of the community to which the individual belongs".[^pluto] The social self is where a culture's narratives are stored nonverbally, and the book says what it is for: "The social self is also one of the main vehicles of magic."[^pluto] That is the blog's far side with the occupant removed. The appraisal of the situation — *progress is inevitable, the universe owes us as much energy as we want* — does not arrive from a recruiter or an operator. It arrives from an apparatus the person did not choose and cannot read, doing what the book says it does:

> "Challenge it – and the concept of peak oil, if it's taken seriously, challenges it to the core – and the social self takes fright and shies away, dragging the chariot and the charioteer with it, and quite possibly spooking the other horse and sending the whole kit and caboodle careening down the nearest blind alley."[^pluto]

**At the scale of a civilisation**, the same mechanism runs on a population, and the book locates the far side in nothing more personal than a myth: "The myths you really believe, of course, are the ones you don't realize that you believe. The myth of progress is still like that for most people."[^lesson] Which is why its diagnosis of the present is not *someone is lying*. It is that the culture has an appraisal and no author: the frame is unowned, and it supplies the reading anyway.

**And here is the reading, which is this post's, and an inference from the above.** [The machine has no reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/)'s finding was that a frame with no category for an event does not remove the event, it removes the language for it — and that a vacant reading is a jurisdiction somebody will occupy. This book supplies a second kind of vacancy, and it is the harder one: not a reading that is **missing**, but one **supplied by nobody and held by everybody**.

> **The blog's mechanism does not require a holder. It requires a misreading — and this book is the demonstration that the far side can be occupied by a frame with no one in it.**

## 4. The religion of progress: a telos, and an empty seat

Now the correction, and it belongs to a specific post. [The deep inheritance](/the-deep-inheritance/) took the telos/no-telos distinction to the root of the human record and drew a rule from it: a cosmology **with an endpoint** is the precondition for a seat. *The Blood of the Earth* has an endpoint, and no seat, and it is not marginal — it is the book's central object.

> "Behind that mismatch is a set of beliefs so deeply rooted in contemporary industrial societies, and so emotionally important to most of the people who live in those societies, that it may as well be called the established religion of the modern industrial world: the belief in the inevitability and beneficence of progress."[^ch1]

It is a religion with an **eschatology**, named by its own popular forms — "God or the Space Brothers or some conveniently undefined leap of consciousness" — which the book compares to the reasoning that convinced people in 999 CE "to brick themselves up in towers, the better to ascend to heaven when Christ appeared in glory with the new year".[^intro] And it names the parallel where it diagnoses the amnesia that keeps a failed prophecy alive:

> "It's weirdly reminiscent of the acquired amnesia that enables believers in apocalyptic prophecies to forget the last half dozen times they talked themselves into believing that the Rapture or the arrival of the Space Brothers or whatever was imminent, and treat the latest prediction with the same earnest enthusiasm."[^ch1]

**So the book gives the blog a case its telos rule did not have: an endpoint, and no one standing at it.** The religion of progress was not founded by an operator, is not administered by a group, and has no membership list. It is inherited, held nonverbally by the apparatus the book calls the social self, and it supplies an appraisal of a situation it cannot see. [The deep inheritance](/the-deep-inheritance/)'s rule is that a telos is the **precondition** for a seat. This book refines it on one side only:

> **A telos is necessary for a seat, and it is not sufficient for one. A frame can name the endpoint and leave it empty — and still supply the appraisal.**

**And an unowned telos is not harmless because it is unowned. It is a vacancy** in the sense [the machine has no reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/) gave the word — a place where the claim is authoritative and unobservable at once. Greer's catalogue of what arrives to fill it is his chapter 'Strange Bright Banners' (a three-thousand-word catalogue, and not the book's longest chapter but its fullest):

> "Existing institutions fail and the collective foundations of meaning crack, there's a large demand for some new vision of destiny that will make sense of the troubles and offer a way past them to some brighter future. The economics of popular belief being what they are, that demand very quickly finds an ample supply."[^banners]

The supply has a template — a critique of the existing order, a Utopian future, and a plan of action — with the defining feature that "the plan of action can't actually deliver the goods".[^banners] The book calls these **revitalization movements** and says the present is ripe for them. The demagogue does not install that vision; he arrives inside it — which is [the operative master](/the-operative-master/)'s finding, that the operator "arrives inside a frame the target already holds, and **fits**", said of a whole society instead of one person.

**The correction, then, in one sentence:**

> **The blog's rule needs a rider: an endpoint is the precondition for a seat, but the endpoint does not have to be anybody's. The myth of progress is a frame with a telos and no owner, and it supplies the appraisal from the far side as completely as a group or an operator does — so the danger is not the telos alone, nor the seat alone, but a telos that leaves a vacancy and a culture with no check on who fills it.**

That refines [the deep inheritance](/the-deep-inheritance/) without overturning it, and it strengthens [the costume](/the-costume/)'s boundary: levers without the mechanism are still not predation, and a mechanism without a lever-puller is still a mechanism.

## 5. The myth of the machine: the frame that cannot imagine its own ending

The chapter this blog's readers will reach for first is "The Myth of the Machine", and its finding arrives from the opposite direction to [the machine frame](/the-machine-has-no-reading/)'s:

> "It's one of the most curious and least discussed features of that very openness that the only kinds of change and novelty that industrial society accepts amount to, basically, more of the same thing we've already got."[^machine]

The example is the car, and the hybrid is the tell: "That's as true of electric and hybrid cars, by the way, as it is of the usual kind – the hype surrounding the so-called hybrid revolution conveniently fails to mention that a very similar system has been used for more than sixty years in diesel-electric locomotives, and cars powered by electricity were common before a handful of major auto firms succeeded in getting a stranglehold on the industry during the last Great Depression."[^machine]

**Put that beside the blog's own finding and the two are not the same claim — they are two holes in the same wall.** [The machine has no reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/) found that a frame whose substrate is dead matter and impersonal force has no **category for an interior event**: it can call a dissolution a fault or a trick, and nothing else. Greer's finding is that the same frame has no category for **discontinuous change** — the only novelty it can generate is a variation on what it already has, which is why every proposed solution to peak oil is a machine. He argues it from first principles:

> "A machine, though, is never a subject; from a certain perspective, that's what defines the machine. Machines imitate the actions of persons, of subjects, but they have no subjectivity, no inner world; they're always and only objects, and so the only relationship you can have with them is an I-It relationship."[^machine]

That is Buber's I-It, named in the edition's notes — the blog's own blind spot seen from a different route. And the book's answer to the trap is not a lever but a noticing: the candidate advancing between two pillars, where "the pillars were the opposite poles of the binary – of any binary – and the path of wisdom lies between them", and "once you find a third option, it's very easy to find more".[^pillars] Its own account of the peak-oil argument is that it was stuck in exactly such a binary.[^pillars]

**Two blind spots, one frame, and the difference is what each does with the hole.** [The machine has no reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/)'s tell was a question: does the frame say *I cannot see this*, or does it say *there is nothing there to see*? Greer's machine frame does the second thing about endings, structurally rather than by anyone's failure of nerve. Neither is a case against the machine frame, because the frame that can be checked is the frame that made [the mechanism post](/meditation-harm/) possible.

The title is borrowed from Lewis Mumford, and the book says so, then says it wished the promise of it had been pursued "in a good deal more depth".[^machine] What the chapter gives is an argument developed from Buber — a reading of a culture, not a measurement of one.

## 6. Magic, and magical thinking — the boundary the book draws, and the one it keeps sliding

The first chapter's title is a phrase the ambient culture owns, and the book's work in it is to take the phrase back and then hand it away again.

The culture's **magical thinking** is defined by example — an investment analyst explaining why peak oil is not a problem: "[G]eology doesn't create oil; capital creates oil."[^ch1] The book's reading is that this is a symbol-system claiming "the manipulation of symbols wields occult powers that can override the laws of nature", and that "this is not what operative mages – people who actually practice magic – claim to be able to do".[^ch1] The verdict is the sentence the phrase is remembered for:

> "What our society calls magical thinking, then, is clearly not the kind of thinking that mages do, and the denunciations of magical thinking flung at mages would be much more sensibly directed at economists."[^ch1]

**So the book distinguishes the two words — and then, a page later, it does not.** The same chapter says magical thinking is what the situation needs:

> "There's a rich irony, in other words, in the insistence that magical thinking is less useful than scientific thinking, because magical thinking is exactly the form of human thought that deals with the realm of motivations, values, and goals that scientific thinking handles so poorly."[^ch1]

Read together, the chapter runs the phrase in **two senses** and does not keep them apart: a pejorative one, which is what the economists do, and a sympathetic one, which is what the situation needs. That is a real seam in the text, reported rather than tidied.

**What magic is *for* is a separate question, and the book answers it twice — once in the abstract, once in the concrete.** The abstract answer is Dion Fortune's definition, adopted and then glossed:

> "The point of magic, as Fortune's definition suggests, is bringing about change in states and contents of consciousness; it can have effects on the material world as well, but that normally involves influencing entities (for example, human beings) that in one way or another bridge the gap between mind and matter."[^ch1]

**The concrete answer is the one this reading turns on, and it comes from a nineteenth-century magician the book hauls out of obscurity to make it.** Joséphin Péladan — "the man Oscar Wilde was imitating" — wrote a book of magical theory and practice with **no magic in it**:

> "His core work of magical theory and practice, *Comment on devient mage* (*How To Become A Mage*, 1892), contains not a single magical ritual. Its theme, to borrow a typically ornate term from his writing, was ethopoeia – the making (*poesis*) of a way of living in the world (*ethos*), one that would enable individuals to stand apart from the collective consciousness of their time in order to think their own thoughts and make their own choices. Society, Péladan wrote, is an anonymous enterprise for living a life of secondhand emotions."[^lesson]

**That is what magic is for, in this book: a capacity rather than an effect.** The final chapter's three instructions are that sentence operationalised — "It's simple enough, really: learn one thing, give up one thing, save one thing."[^begin] Learn a skill the deindustrial future will need; give up one dependence on the industrial system now, while the choice is still yours; preserve one piece of the culture you could not bear to lose — because "When they make it through the dark age that follows the breakdown of a civilization, nearly always it's because someone cared enough to keep them going as living traditions."[^begin]

**One more distinction, and it is the one the blog has been using half of without knowing it.** The book's operating cut is between **theurgy** and **thaumaturgy** — the magic that trains the operator's own nonrational mind, and the magic that manipulates other people's. A case-insensitive search of the blog's published posts returns **9 hits for "theurgy"**, all of them names for the classical tradition, and **0 hits for "thaumaturgy"** anywhere.[^abs]

## 7. The counterweight: a polemic, a practitioner, and what would count as evidence

The series requires an explicit counterweight in the body, and this book needs an unusually candid one, because it is not a survey. It is a **polemic by a practising ceremonial magician who is also a peak-oil writer**, and three things follow that have to be said before anything is drawn from it.

**First, the empirical claims and the argument are different kinds of statement, and the book mostly argues.** The sourced elements are thin and checkable — Hubbert's 1956 warning, a claim that "global conventional oil production peaked in 2005 and has been in decline since then", a critique of the hundred-years-of-gas figure, Daniel Yergin's 2011 essay and his earlier price predictions.[^ch1] The **structure** of the rest is inferential: geological and thermodynamic limits, "net energy issues and energy subsidies", the diffuseness of renewables, the absence of a substitute. Each is an argument from a model, and the model is contested by serious people. **This post does not adjudicate any of it.** What would settle it is production and energy-return data measured over decades — settled by time rather than rhetoric, which is the book's own position.

**Second, its central causal claim is its least supported and most load-bearing.** §2 quotes the sentence — the forces pinning the industrial world "have much less to do with strictly material realities than with the realms of mind and consciousness". That is not a measurement; it is a diagnosis of motive, offered by a practitioner who works in that register. It may be right. Nothing here says it is wrong. But a reader who takes it as established has adopted a thesis, and this series does not do that.

**Third, and it is what this reading has to be most careful about: the book describes a magician and offers no referral.** Searched as the series searches, it carries a practice-level caution and no clinical one. "Caution" returns **0 hits**, and "safeguard" returns exactly **one** — a warning about the ambient culture, not to a reader:

> "Good intentions and idealism, it bears remembering, are not an adequate safeguard against systematic manipulation of the mass mind, especially when that manipulation moves in parallel with the desperate craving of a great many people to have the lifestyles they think they deserve and ought to get."[^begin]

A case-insensitive search returns **0 hits** for "psychiatr", "clinician", "therap", "suicide", "emergency", "psychosis", "sanity", "mental health", "premature" and "too soon"; "breakdown" occurs **once**, and it is the breakdown of a civilisation.[^abs] **So this book reports the same absence three readings have now reported, and it does not report it the same way.** Where [Undreaming Wetiko](/undreaming-wetiko/) named psychiatry as an adversary, this one never mentions it — as though the question had not arisen.

**What it *does* carry is a different kind of safeguard, internal to the practice.** Three instances:

- **The planes are not continuous.** The whole polemic against metaphysical rescue rests on one axiom: "The technical term for that mistake is confounding the planes; *the planes of existence*, an old magical axiom has it, *are discrete and not continuous* – which means in plain English that mind is mind, matter is matter".[^ch1] It is a book about magic that forbids magic from doing the thing its readers most want it to do.
- **The teachers expect the phone call.** On the first encounters with apparent spirits that serious training reliably produces: "those of us who teach magic all know to expect the panicky phone call or email that comes right after each student has his or her first encounter with an apparent spirit", and "The traditional lore offers a great deal of useful guidance in how to deal with these experiences while maintaining a state of relative mental balance".[^ch1] A frame supplied before the event — one of the provisions [the mechanism post](/meditation-harm/) found the traditions had encoded.
- **Readiness is graded, and the pace is metered.** "Initiation is very much subject to readiness factors. The competent teacher of magic knows that at any given time, some students are ready for a given grade of initiation and others simply aren't."[^pluto] On the isolation the practice requires, the book reports Péladan's advice to open "an insulating space between the individual mind and the pressures that surround it", then notes that "All this advice is aimed at the social habits of another time and has not necessarily aged well, but the basic principles still stand."[^lesson]

**And one counterweight runs the other way, which is why this book is easier to hand to a reader than some in this series.** It argues **against** the overturning of physical limits, at length, and it is the only source here that does: "You can't use incantations and rituals, for example, to put oil in the ground if it was never there in the first place, or if the oil fields have already been pumped dry."[^banners] A book whose central claim is that magic cannot do the thing a hopeful reader wants is carrying a safeguard most of this material does not. **Nothing here is a finding about the author:** that he holds the positions the text states is a fact about the text; what kind of writer he is, and whether his energy reading survives, is not decided.

## 8. What the blog owes

Two, and they are one correction and one gap.

**The correction: the mechanism's far side does not have to be a person, and this book is the proof.** [The costume](/the-costume/)'s boundary rests on an unstated assumption — that where the mechanism runs, someone is pulling something. Every case study has had one. *The Blood of the Earth* runs the same mechanism with **an ontology instead of an operator** — the myth of progress, held nonverbally by the apparatus the book calls the social self, supplying an appraisal of a situation the culture cannot read, with no founder and no frame-builder in it. And it runs the same structure a second time with **no occupant at all** — peak oil as predicament, a material process with no far side, arriving whether or not anyone reads it. Together they give the blog something it did not have:

> **The mechanism describes the shape of a misreading, and a holder is one way to install one. What it requires is an appraisal supplied from outside the state, not a person to supply it — and the far side can be a frame with nobody standing in it.**

Two consequences follow, and both are narrow. **First**, the blog's account of harm was never a theory of predation, and this book is the case that shows it: there is a way for a system to be misread that involves nobody preying on anybody, which means [the costume](/the-costume/)'s boundary can be restated as a *strength* rather than a hedge. **Second**, the vacancy finding generalises one step beyond [the machine has no reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/): there the vacancy was a missing reading, here it is a **present and unowned** one.

**The refinement: the telos rule gains a rider.** [The deep inheritance](/the-deep-inheritance/) established that a frame with an endpoint is the precondition for a seat. This book gives a frame with an endpoint and no seat, at civilisational scale, in the present tense. The rule survives in one direction and not the other:

> **A telos is necessary for a seat and not sufficient for one. What makes an unowned telos dangerous is not the endpoint: it is that the endpoint leaves a vacancy, and the book's longest chapter is a catalogue of who arrives to fill it — the revitalization movement, the three-part template, the demagogue who arrives inside a frame the target already holds.**

This does not weaken [the deep inheritance](/the-deep-inheritance/)'s finding. It completes it: the endpoint is what makes the position *available*, and the check is what decides whether anything predatory stands in it.

**And the gap, which is the same gap, in this book's own words.** [Undreaming Wetiko](/undreaming-wetiko/) left the blog with a remedy that is a first-person act and a witness who is the same person. *The Blood of the Earth* states the identical limit as a **principle of the art**, which makes it harder to argue with, not easier:

> "The wider issue here is that theurgy can't be done for, to, or by anyone else. It's up to the individual."[^pluto]

The whole remedy is addressed to one person working on their own nonrational mind, alone, on purpose, using a tradition's toolkit. And the blog's hardest structural finding — from [the mechanism post](/meditation-harm/), [the container](/the-container/), [the holy heretics](/the-holy-heretics/) and [the cosmos that refuses](/the-cosmos-that-refuses/) — is that the instrument which would audit is the instrument under audit, and that the second reader is the one thing no practitioner can supply for themselves. **This book does not supply it either; it makes the absence a doctrine.** It knows the exit is external — it is the same book that says "if the will isn't there, neither is the magic"[^banners] — and it locates the will entirely in the individual it is trying to save. That is the reading's gap: a magician's manual for a civilisational collapse, with a graded initiatory frame for the student and nobody named for the practitioner whose internal work has gone somewhere they cannot see.

## 9. What a reading is for

A reading holds one source whole — its edition, its structure, its eschatology, its omissions, its safeguards — and asks what a blog that has quoted sources all along now has to change.

**The case** this one brings is the one the blog has never had: a collapse that arrives through matter, with no one on the far side. It does not fit the framework, and the honest report is that the framework was never supposed to fit it. What the book does is narrower and more useful than a new case study: it shows which half of the mechanism was load-bearing. The appraisal, not the appraiser.

**The refinement** is small and real. The split between a frame with an endpoint and a frame without one survives contact with a book whose central frame has both an endpoint and no owner. What it gains is the rider: the endpoint makes the seat available, and the state of the seat is a separate question — which means the safeguard was never in the shape of the frame, only in whether anyone could be asked about the answer.

**And the gap is unchanged**, three readings after it first appeared, and this source states it as a rule rather than an omission, which is worse: theurgy can't be done for, to, or by anyone else. It is the individual's work, and the individual is the one instrument this series has established cannot audit itself.

Which leaves the reading's own bottom line. This book's three practical verbs — learn one thing, give up one thing, save one thing — are a program for a person whose situation cannot be reported from inside it, because the culture that would report it is the culture that cannot see it. **The check cannot come from where the state is.** This source says it in the imperative, at the end of a manual for a descent, and names nobody standing outside — which is why the correction here is about who stands on the far side, and the gap about who holds the far side of a person's own practice.

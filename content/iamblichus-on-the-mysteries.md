# Iamblichus, On the Mysteries

### Twenty-second of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the counter-text to the last reading, a polemic that takes the efficacy out of the practitioner and puts it in the act, and the first frame in this series whose uncheckable part is the practice, and says so

*Twenty-second of the `readings`, and the answer to the twenty-first. [Proclus, The Elements of
Theology](/proclus-elements-of-theology/) was the same school's system as pure deduction: two
hundred and eleven propositions, each proved from the one before it, and — on its editor's own
account — no reference to theurgy in the whole of it. That reading ended on an absence. This is
the other book of the same tradition, and it says the opposite thing in the plainest words
available to it: it is not thought that unites, it is the act. Then it names what the act is
made of, and the name it gives is one that no reader can inspect. The blog has spent this series
asking where the seat is — who or what is doing the work, and where the authority sits. Here the
seat is removed outright, and what replaces it is harder on a reader, not easier: nothing is
left to examine but an act and its symbols, and the book states, without embarrassment, that the
symbols are understood by the gods alone.*

*Status.* This post reads **two** editions of one work, and names the edition at every quotation.
The primary text is Thomas Taylor's translation, *Iamblichus on the Mysteries of the Egyptians,
Chaldeans, and Assyrians* — the 1895 reprint of the translation first printed in 1821 — cited by
Taylor's own divisions, **Section and Chapter** ("Section II, Chap. XI"), and, where a passage
comes from his front matter or his own additional notes, marked as his. The second is Emma C.
Clarke, John M. Dillon and Jackson P. Hershbell's *Iamblichus: De Mysteriis* (Society of Biblical
Literature, 2003), Greek and English facing, with an Introduction and notes; it is cited by
**Book and section** ("II.11") as its running heads print them, and its Introduction and the
editors' notes are cited as **the editor's**. Where a quotation is the editor's note rather than
Iamblichus, it is marked as such, and a cross-reference the editor gives is quoted as the
editor's. Both copies are machine readings of printed books and each is damaged in its own
places. Line-break hyphenation is undone and spacing collapsed where a quotation crosses a line;
typographic quotation marks are rendered here as straight ASCII; curly apostrophes, dashes and
ellipses are normalised, and inner quotation marks are rendered as single quotes. Three classes of
misreading in the 2003 printing are handled, and are named here: the digit-for-letter slips in
which *is* is set as **15** and the capital *I* of Iamblichus is set as a lowercase **l**, both of
which are restored; and footnote markers that the machine reading has dropped into the middle of a
sentence, which are removed. Where Taylor's reprint is corrupt — and at a few points it is — the
quotation stops, or the damaged part is elided, rather than being mended. Nothing is spliced in
from anywhere else, and elisions are marked with a bare ellipsis. The reading of this material
through the blog's model is an **inference**, offered as one. **Nothing here claims the gods are
real and nothing here claims they are not; nothing here claims theurgic efficacy is real and
nothing here claims it is not; and nothing here validates, disqualifies or restricts anyone's
practice.** Iamblichus' claims are reported as his, Taylor's as his, the editors' as theirs. No
living or dead person is diagnosed. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no one.

## 1. What kind of book this is, and what kind of answer it gives

The first thing to say about *On the Mysteries* is that it is not a treatise but a reply, and the
reply's shape is the opponent's. The Clarke/Dillon/Hersh edition names the genre exactly, and the
name is the useful thing:

> "it is essentially a series of replies to a set of *aporiai* proposed by Porphyry, about the
> nature of the gods, or of the divine realm in general, and the proper mode of our worship of
> them."

— the editor, Introduction §5.[^cIntro] The editor's name for the genre is "Problems and
Solutions" (the editors' term).[^cIntro] What set the questions was a letter of Porphyry, and the editor says what that letter
was in a sentence that ought to be kept in view for the whole of this reading:

> "The *De mysteriis*, however, is a point-by-point refutation of Porphyry's Letter to Anebo, an
> epistle which launched a vicious attack on theurgy, more than likely aimed specifically at
> Iamblichus and his beliefs."

— the editor, Introduction §2.[^cIntro2] And in a note on the same page, on why Porphyry changed
his position:

> "It was also a refutation of Porphyry's own earlier dabblings in this field, as expressed in
> the *Philosophy from Oracles*. Porphyry's personal association with Plotinus was more than
> likely the cause of his change of heart."

— the editor, Introduction §2, note.[^cN30] **So the book was written against someone, by a man
who had been that someone's pupil, on a subject where the two of them disagreed about the
efficacy of ritual in particular.** And the book opens by saying that the letter may be taken as
addressed to him: "the letter sent to my student Anebo" (the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, I.1) — the
persona's student, which the editor reads as a joke at the addressee's expense.[^cIntro3]

The speaker is not Iamblichus but a persona, and the editions name him differently. Taylor's
translation heads the work:

> "THE ANSWER OF THE PRECEPTOR ABAMMON TO THE EPISTLE OF PORPHYRY TO ANEBO, AND A SOLUTION OF THE
> DOUBTS CONTAINED IN IT."

— Taylor's translation, the heading of Section I as his volume prints it.[^tTitle] Taylor prints the
name **Abammon**, and gives the attribution in a note of his own at the foot of the first chapter,
resting on Proclus:

> "It is requisite to know that the philosopher Proclus, in his Commentary on the Enneads of the
> great Plotinus, says that it is the divine Iamblichus who answers the prefixed Epistle of
> Porphyry, and who assumes the person of a certain Egyptian of the name of Abammon, through the
> affinity and congruity of the hypothesis."

— Taylor's note to Section I, Chap. I.[^tI1n] The 2003 edition prints the same name as **Abamon**,
one *m* lighter, and its Introduction gives the same attribution in its own voice: Iamblichus, the
editors write, is "writing under the assumed guise of the Egyptian prophet" Abamon.[^cIntro] **The
one-letter difference is the smallest instance of a difference that runs through this whole
reading: the two texts are two readings of one book, and they do not agree in small things.**

What the persona is *for* is stated by the editor as a problem, and answered:

> "If Iamblichus was unconcerned with the pressures of the pagan-Christian debate, why did he
> write in the guise of the purported Egyptian prophet Abamon? The decision was an interesting
> one, and sparked by Porphyry's attack to which he is making his reply."

— the editor, Introduction §3.[^cIntro3] The book's own first chapter answers it by saying the
speaker's identity is not the point, and both renderings make that move in nearly the same words:

> "dismiss from your mind the speaker, whether he be better or worse, and consider what is said,
> whether it be true or false, rousing up your intellect to the task with a will."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, I.1.[^cI1] Taylor's:

> "dismiss the consideration whether the speaker is an inferior or a superior character, but
> direct your attention to what is said, so as readily to excite your mind to survey whether
> what is asserted is true or false."

— Taylor's translation, Section I, Chap. I.[^tI1] **So the book's first instruction is to stop
asking who is speaking** — the earliest thing in this series that resembles the blog's own
question, and it is answered by a manoeuvre rather than an answer: the authority is placed in the
argument's content, and the speaker is retired.

Then the book states its own subject, and both editions state it for it. Taylor's Introduction to
his volume is explicit about the claim the book is making:

> "it is the most copious, the clearest, and the most satisfactory defence extant of genuine
> ancient theology. This theology, the sacred operations pertaining to which called theurgy are
> here developed"

— Taylor's Introduction to his translation.[^tIntro] The editor of the modern edition says the
same thing in more neutral language, and adds what the author's motive was:

> "a masterful attempt to combine the teachings of revelation literature with those of
> Neoplatonism, and to give theurgic rites a philosophical basis."

— the editor, Introduction §3.[^cIntro3] And the thesis is stated as the book's subject, not a
topic in it:

> "Iamblichus was essentially interested in re-awakening and preserving man's contact with the
> ancestral gods, and in arguing that theurgy (or 'god-work') rather than theology (or
> 'god-talk') was the only way of achieving this."

— the editor, Introduction §3.[^cIntro3] **Theurgy against theology; the act against the talk.**
That is the book, and it is the exact inversion of the last reading, where the school's most
complete book was all talk and no rite, and its editor could say so with a straight face.

The blog should also record, at the outset, something the 2003 edition says about the *divisions*
of the book, because every citation below depends on them:

> "The present division of the work into books dates only from the Renaissance."

— the editor, Introduction §5.[^cIntro5] The editor credits the division to Scutellius' Latin of
1556, after Ficino's of 1497, and records that "this arrangement was adopted by Thomas Gale in
his *editio princeps* of 1678",[^cIntro5] and then says what the arrangement costs:

> "The division into ten 'books' (of very unequal length), while not seriously misleading (as
> being based largely on natural breaks in Iamblichus's exposition), does, however, somewhat
> obscure the original structure of Iamblichus's work."

— the editor, Introduction §5.[^cIntro5] **The addresses this post cites are a sixteenth-century
editor's, not the author's.** The same shape of problem as the last reading's — a text established
for a reader by someone else — and worse here, because the modern edition classifies the book by
its true title (*The Reply of the Master Abamon…*)[^cN110] and then cites it by a later one.

## 2. The seat is taken out

The passage at the centre of this book, and of this reading, is a sentence about what does *not*
unite. The 2003 text renders it:

> "it does not follow on this that the offerings made to the gods and divine works are invalid,
> for it is not pure thought that unites theurgists to the gods. Indeed what, then, would hinder
> those who are theoretical philosophers from enjoying a theurgic union with the gods? But the
> situation is not so: it is the accomplishment of acts not to be divulged and beyond all
> conception, and the power of the unutterable symbols, understood solely by the gods, which
> establishes theurgic union."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, II.11.[^cII11] Taylor's rendering of the same passage is why this
post quotes both editions here — it is the same claim, and it is visibly a different claim:

> "For a conception of the mind does not conjoin theurgists with the Gods … For the perfect
> efficacy of ineffable works, which are divinely performed in a way surpassing all intelligence,
> and the power of inexplicable symbols, which are known only to the Gods, impart theurgic
> union."

— Taylor's translation, Section II, Chap. XI.[^tII11] **Read it as a removal.** The claim is
negative first: thought does not unite. The rhetorical question turns that into an argument — if
thought united, the theoretical philosophers would be united, and they are not. Then the positive
claim names what does unite, and the name is not a person, not a state of mind, not a discipline
and not a virtue. It is "the accomplishment of acts" and "the power of the unutterable symbols"
(the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, II.11).[^cII11] **Nobody is standing in this sentence.**

Nor can the theurgist be re-inserted at the next step. The same section gives the mechanism and
takes the operation from both sides at once — the human and the divine:

> "Hence, we do not bring about these things by intellection alone; for thus their efficacy would
> be intellectual, and dependent upon us. But neither assumption is true. For even when we are
> not engaged in intellection, the symbols themselves, by themselves, perform their appropriate
> work, and the ineffable power of the gods, to whom these symbols relate, itself recognises the
> proper images of itself, not through being aroused by our thought."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, II.11.[^cII11] Three clauses, and each takes something from the
operator: the efficacy is *not* intellectual, *not* dependent upon us, and operates *even when we
are not engaged in intellection*. Taylor's rendering is harder on the operator still:

> "we do not perform these things through intellectual perception … the intellectual energy of
> them would be imparted by us; neither of which is true."

— Taylor's translation, Section II, Chap. XI.[^tII11] And on the symbols, Taylor leaves the Greek
word standing in his English:

> "the ineffable power of the Gods itself knows, by itself, its own images. It does not, however,
> know them, as if excited by our intelligence; for neither is it natural that things which
> comprehend should be excited by those that are comprehended, nor perfect by imperfect natures,
> nor wholes by parts."

— Taylor's translation, Section II, Chap. XI.[^tII11] The word he leaves untranslated is
*synthemata*, and his note glosses it: "The inexplicable theurgic signs or symbols."[^tII11n] **He
keeps the Greek term so that the reader sees the doing done by the thing and not by the person** —
and the modern edition does the opposite, rendering *synthemata* as "the symbols themselves"
(the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, II.11).[^cII11]

The gods are not moved by the request. Taylor, on invocation:

> "Neither do the invocations which implore the Gods to incline to us, conjoin the priests to
> them through passion; but procure for them the communion of an indissoluble connexion, through
> the friendship which binds all things together."

— Taylor's translation, Section I, Chap. XII.[^tI12] The 2003 text's rendering of what invocation
actually does is the sentence this reading leans on most, because it relocates the operation:

> "rather in virtue of the divine love which holds together all things that they provide a union
> of indissoluble involvement—not, as the name seems immediately to imply, inclining the mind of
> the gods to humans, but rather, as the truth of things itself desires to teach us, disposing
> the human mind to participation in the gods, leading it up to the gods and bringing it into
> accord with them through harmonious persuasion."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, I.12.[^cI12] **Nothing is inclined downward.** The gods are not
persuaded or brought; it is the human mind that is disposed, led, and brought into accord. The
leading is done by the names and symbols, and Taylor's rendering of the next sentence carries a
technical term into the English and glosses it:

> "such names of the Gods as are adapted to sacred concerns, and other divine symbols, are able,
> as they are of an anagogic or elevating nature, to connect invocations with the Gods
> themselves."

— Taylor's translation, Section I, Chap. XII.[^tI12]

So far, so Platonic: a hierarchy, an ascent, symbols that carry it. What makes this book the
counter-text to the *Elements* is the next move, about prayer, which denies that prayer is a
communication between two parties at all:

> "it is neither through faculties nor through organs that the gods receive into themselves our
> prayers, but rather they embrace within themselves the realisations of the words of good men,
> and in particular of those which, by virtue of the sacred liturgy, are established within the
> gods and united to them; for in that case the divine is literally united with itself, and it is
> not in the way of one person addressing another that it participates in the thought expressed
> by the prayers."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, I.15.[^cI15] Taylor's rendering ends on the same structural point:

> "The Gods, therefore, do not receive prayers in themselves, through any corporeal powers or
> organs, but rather contain in themselves the energies of pious invocations; and especially of
> such as, through sacred ceremonies, are established in, and united to, the Gods. For then, in
> reality, a divine nature is present with itself …"

— Taylor's translation, Section I, Chap. XV.[^tI15] **There is no addressee in the ordinary
sense.** The prayer is not carried to a hearer; the gods "embrace within themselves" what is
offered (the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, I.15); the divine is "united with itself" (both editions, at
I.15 and Section I, Chap. XV).[^cI15][^tI15] The blog's marker for a frame has been the seat: an
endpoint that is named, and someone or something stationed there whose authority does the work.
Here the endpoint is named and the seat holds no occupant the reader could examine — not because
the book is modest, but because it has moved the efficacy into the act, and the act's power is the
gods' own. Taylor says it outright at Section II, Chap. XI: "things pertaining to the Gods, are
moved by themselves, and do not receive from any inferior nature a certain principle in themselves
of their own proper energy."[^tII11] **The answer to where the agency sits is: nowhere a reader can
point, and everywhere the gods are — the question has been refused, not answered.**

That refusal is repeated where a reader would most expect a seat. Take Porphyry's sharpest
question — how it is that theurgists invoke the gods as superiors and then give them orders as
inferiors. The 2003 text splits the act in two:

> "the whole of theurgy presents a double aspect. On the one hand, it is performed by men, and as
> such observes our natural rank in the universe; but on the other, it controls divine symbols,
> and in virtue of them is raised up to union with the higher powers, and directs itself
> harmoniously in accordance with their dispensation, which enables it quite properly to assume
> the mantle of the gods."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, IV.2.[^cIV2] Taylor's rendering of the same split is the blog's
question in miniature:

> "in all theurgical operations the priest sustains a twofold character; one, indeed, as man, and
> which preserves the order possessed by our nature in the universe; but the other, which is
> corroborated by divine signs, and through these is conjoined to more excellent natures, and is
> elevated to their order by an elegant circumduction, this is deservedly capable of being
> surrounded with the external form of the Gods."

— Taylor's translation, Section IV, Chap. II.[^tIV2] **The modern text says the *theurgy* has a
double aspect; Taylor says the *priest* has a twofold character** — one puts the doubleness in the
rite, one in the man, and the difference decides where a reader looks for a seat. The divergence
continues in the next clause, where the authority is worn rather than owned:

> "and again, he commands these powers, because through arcane symbols, he, in a certain respect,
> is invested with the sacred form of the Gods."

— Taylor's translation, Section IV, Chap. II.[^tIV2] Taylor also appends a note making the
hierarchy explicit in his own voice:
"And these irrational spirits, so far as they contribute to wholes, are more excellent than we are,
though through being irrational they are inferior to us."[^tIV2n] **The seat, where it exists at
all here, is a borrowed costume — the hieratic role of the gods, put on by the rite, worn by
whoever is standing there.**

The book's account of the ascent has the same shape. In Book X, on the one road to happiness:

> "what other reasonable mode of ascent to it can there be apart from the gods? For if the
> essence and accomplishment of all good is encompassed by the gods and their primal power and
> authority, it is only with us and those who are similarly possessed by the greatest kinds and
> have genuinely gained union with them that the beginning and the end of all good is seriously
> practised."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, X.1.[^cX1] The editor glosses the first clause with a bare note:
"That is, other than theurgy."[^cN485] **So the only ascent is theurgy, and the theurgy is the
gods' own power and authority, which those who attain it are "possessed by".[^cX1]** And the book's
last chapter is not a technique but a prayer:

> "It remains, therefore, at the end of this discourse, for me to pray to the gods to grant both
> to me and to you the unalterable preservation of true thoughts, to implant in us the truth of
> eternal things forever, and to grant to us a participation in the more perfect conceptions of
> the gods in which the most blessed end of good things is placed before us, along with the
> sanction of the harmonious friendship between us."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, X.8.[^cX8] Taylor's closes the same way, shortening the address:

> "It remains, therefore, at the end of this discussion, that I should beseech the Gods to afford
> me an immutable guard of true conceptions, to insert in me truth eternally, and to supply me
> abundantly with the participation of more perfect conceptions of the Gods, in which the most
> blessed end of our good is posited, and the confirmation of our concordant friendship with each
> other."

— Taylor's translation, Section X, Chap. VIII.[^tX8] The modern text prays "both to me and to
you", and the editor reads the closing line as aimed: "A final put-down of Porphyry—combined,
perhaps, with something of an olive branch?"[^cN497] **The book does not end by handing the reader
a method. It ends by asking the gods for something, and the request is also the last move in an
argument with the man it was written against.**

One more place a reader would look for a seat is the *mediator* — a person standing between the
practitioner and the god. There is no such person. The book has priests, a caste, and an address
to an Egyptian prophet, and every one of those is a persona, a rank, or an addressee. What stands
between practitioner and god is a *symbol* — not a person, not an office, and not learnable the
way a technique is learnable.

## 3. And the uncheckable part is not a person or a claim. It is the practice

The blog's apparatus has been asking where the seat is, and the readings have given it several
answers. [The owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) named the endpoint and stationed someone at it.
[The cosmos that refuses](/the-cosmos-that-refuses/) kept the correspondence and dropped the
endpoint. [The Elements](/proclus-elements-of-theology/) proved a whole cosmos and stationed
nobody, and the blog corrected itself on that one: the operator was absent from the text, not from
the system, and the lesson taken was that the marker of a frame is **the claim**, and that a frame
in proof-form relocates its uncheckable part to the axioms without labelling them as axioms.

This reading supplies a third arrangement, and it is the one the instrument cannot currently see.
Here the seat is not hidden, not relocated, and not occupied by a substitute. It is **removed**,
and what replaces it is a practice whose operative part the book declares unavailable to anyone
but the gods. The declaration is not tucked away; it is inside the sentence that states the
doctrine:

> "the power of the unutterable symbols, understood solely by the gods, which establishes
> theurgic union."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, II.11.[^cII11] Taylor's rendering is the same claim in other
vocabulary: "the power of inexplicable symbols, which are known only to the Gods, impart theurgic
union."[^tII11] **Unutterable and inexplicable; understood solely by the gods, known only to the
gods.** The symbols are the efficacy, and the symbols are by construction not available for
inspection. The book does not apologise and does not explain it away. It states it as the
mechanism.

Nor is that a single sentence's slip. The book says the same about the ground of the whole system
at I.21, in a passage whose two renderings differ just enough to set side by side:

> "unutterable truths are expressed through secret symbols, beings beyond form brought under the
> control of form, things superior to all image reproduced through images, and all things brought
> to completion through one single divine cause, which itself so far transcends passions that
> reason is not even capable of grasping it."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, I.21.[^cI21] Taylor's:

> "which is in so great a degree separated from passions, that reason is not able to come into
> contact with it."

— Taylor's translation, Section I, Chap. XXI.[^tI21] One says reason cannot grasp it, the other
that reason cannot come into contact with it. **Both say the same thing about the reader.** And
the passage goes on to explain why people get the frame wrong, in a phrase that names exactly the
error the blog's instrument makes — "This, therefore, is nearly the cause of our aberration"[^tI21]
— where the aberration, on both renderings, is judging divine things by human ones. The book's
position is that the standard is not available: not that the argument is hard, but that its terms
are the gods' own, and reason is named as unable to reach them.

The same structure shows in the treatment of the sacred names, where a reader can measure it
because the text is talking about words:

> "which may indeed be called 'inexplicable' and 'barbarous,' but which are in fact wholly
> suitable for sacred rituals."

— the Clarke/Dillon/Hersh text, VII.5.[^cVII5] The claim is not that the names are difficult but
that they must not be altered, and the editor's note attaches the injunction to the Chaldaean
Oracles and to a line of ritual practice running back through the magical papyri.[^cN393] **The
book that argues the act is the efficacy also holds that the act's words are untranslatable by
design** — a pointed finding to state against the blog's own method, which is reading the argument
in English.

The modern edition's note at I.15, cross-referring to the central passage, makes the same point
from the other side, and it is the editor's sentence rather than Iamblichus', which is why it is
marked as the editor's:

> "and it is a point often reiterated by 'Abamon' (see e.g. II.11.96-97)—not even the most
> accomplished sage, so long as he maintains a purely intellectual approach (as does Porphyry,
> and as did Plotinus), can attain to the highest levels of theurgic union."

— the editor's note to I.15.[^cN88] **The editor names Porphyry and Plotinus as the two men whose
approach cannot reach the top of the practice**, and prints that as a restatement of the author's
position rather than a criticism. The editor records elsewhere that the author asserts "all
wonders or demonstrations of power are their work alone" — the gods' — and that by the editors'
summary "wonder-working by man was at best impious, at worst an example of meaningless
sorcery."[^cIntro2]

**Put those together and the finding is plain.** The seat is removed, the efficacy sits in acts
and symbols, and the operative content of the acts and symbols is declared unknowable to anyone
but the gods. The blog's instrument looks for where the authority sits; the answer here is in the
rite, and in the words of the rite, which cannot be checked by the people performing them, by the
philosophers reasoning about them, or by the reader reading them. **The uncheckable part of this
frame is not a person and not a claim. It is the practice — and the text says so on the page, in
the same sentence as the doctrine, without hedging.** That is the third case, and a more
consequential one than the first two, because a person can be watched and a claim can be tested,
and a rite whose meaning belongs to the gods has been placed beyond both by construction.

Where the last reading's correction was that a frame in proof-form relocates its uncheckable part
to the front and mislabels it, this reading's is stronger and less comfortable: **a frame can be
entirely candid about its uncheckable part and remain uncheckable.** Candour is not a safeguard.
It is at most a disclosure — and this book discloses more than the *Elements* did.

## 4. The correction the blog owes

The blog has been running one instrument through this series since [the owned
cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/): find the endpoint, find the route, and ask who is stationed at the
end doing the work. The reading of Proclus' *Elements* sharpened it into a second question — does
the frame state its premises or hide them? — and the blog's conclusion there was that the
axiomatic form relocates the uncheckable part rather than removing it.

The correction this reading owes is that **both of those questions assume the uncheckable part is
something a reader could, in principle, put a finger on.** A named seat, a stated axiom, a revealed
text, a proof: each offers the reader a *place* to look, even when what is found there turns out to
be an assumption. That is the ground of the last reading's correction — the axiomatic form
*relocates* the uncheckable part to the front. *On the Mysteries* relocates nothing. So:

> **The instrument's questions all locate the uncheckable part somewhere a reader can reach**: a
> person whose authority is borrowed, a premise that is not demonstrated. Iamblichus supplies the
> arrangement the instrument has no question for. He removes the seat, puts the efficacy into an
> act, and then declares the operative content of the act to be the gods' knowledge — so that the
> frame's uncheckable part is no longer a person and no longer a claim. **It is a practice, and
> there is nothing in it for a reader to inspect.** The absence of a seat is not a safeguard and
> not an improvement. It is a third location for the same problem, and in this book the location
> that is hardest to reach.

The consequence for the blog's test is a third question, and it is the one this reading earns.
The series has asked: *is the endpoint named, and is anyone stationed at it?* Then: *is the
frame's first principle offered as a postulate or as a fact?* The question this book adds is not
about either end of the frame but about its middle: **what is this frame's uncheckable part made
of — a person, a claim, or a practice — and does the frame say which?** The *Elements* answered
with a claim and did not label it. *On the Mysteries* answers with a practice and labels it
twice in the sentence that states the doctrine. **Both are uncheckable; only one of them is
honest about it; and honesty changes nothing about the reader's position.** That is a harder
finding than the last one, and the blog should hold it without softening it: a frame can tell you
exactly where it is uncheckable, precisely, in plain language, and be uncheckable anyway.

## 5. The counterweights

Three, and all three belong in the body, because each one cuts against the finding above rather
than decorating it.

**First: this is a polemic, and the shape of its answers is not its own.** The book is a reply,
and the reply is organised by the opponent's letter. The editor names it a "point-by-point
refutation of Porphyry's Letter to Anebo".[^cIntro2] That has two consequences a reader should
carry. One is that nothing in the book is a neutral description of practice: every position is
stated where Porphyry's question forces it, so the book's proportions are the questioner's. The
book says almost nothing, for example, about how a rite is to be performed, and a great deal
about whether it can work — because Porphyry asked the second and not the first. The other
consequence is the one this post has already recorded as a fact about its own citations: the
book's division into ten books is a Renaissance arrangement, adopted into the *editio princeps*
of 1678.[^cIntro5] **The evidence for the finding in §3 is therefore itself editorially
assembled.** That does not weaken the finding — the sentences are Iamblichus' — but it does mean
that the blog's "Book.Section" citations are citations to a shape the sixteenth century imposed,
and a reader who wants to know where a passage sat in the author's own arrangement cannot be told.

**Second: the uncheckable ground is the argument, and the cost should be stated plainly.** The cost
of the finding is that there is no version of this book from the outside. A reader cannot adopt the
frame and check it, because the terms in which it would be checked — the symbols, the names, the
efficacy of the act — are held by the book to be the gods' knowledge and not the reader's. The book
states this as its strength; from where the blog sits it is a limit that cannot be argued away. The
blog's job is to describe it, not adjudicate it: the practice is uncheckable *by construction*, the
construction is the claim, and the claim is not the sort of thing a non-participant can test. The
one thing the blog must not do is convert "uncheckable" into "false". Nothing here establishes that
theurgic efficacy does not work; what is established is that this book's account of it cannot be
verified from outside the practice, and that the book says as much. That is a statement about the
frame's structure, and about the reader's access to it, and about nothing else.

**Third: the two renderings differ measurably, and an apparatus adds what a translation cannot.**
That is the subject of §6. The short version: Taylor's English and the modern text disagree where
the doctrine is densest; the modern edition supplies book-and-section addresses, the editor's
cross-references and the editor's own notes; Taylor's volume has an Introduction and Additional
Notes keyed to his printed pages. **Neither volume is the book. Both are readings, and this post
has quoted both where they diverge.**

## 6. The two renderings, measured

This section is a comparison at named loci and makes no claim beyond them.

The central passage is the cleanest case, because both editions render the same Greek and the
difference is doctrinal. On the first clause, the modern text has "it is not pure thought that
unites theurgists to the gods";[^cII11] Taylor has "a conception of the mind does not conjoin
theurgists with the Gods".[^tII11] **Four substitutions in one clause.** *Pure thought* against *a
conception of the mind*: the modern text names a faculty in its purity, Taylor a mental event.
*Unites* against *conjoin*: the modern verb is the technical one, Taylor's plainer and slightly
more physical. *To the gods* against *with the Gods* moves the relation from an approach to an
association. And the mood changes with the construction: the modern text is a flat negative
assertion, Taylor's the same assertion carried by a noun subject doing something. **The two
sentences make the same point and would be used to make different arguments** — one by a
philosopher distinguishing faculties, the other by a Platonist describing what attaches.

The second clause is a sharper difference, because the modern text makes explicit what Taylor
leaves as a technical term. The 2003 edition has "the accomplishment of acts not to be divulged
and beyond all conception, and the power of the unutterable symbols";[^cII11] Taylor has "the
perfect efficacy of ineffable works, which are divinely performed in a way surpassing all
intelligence, and the power of inexplicable symbols".[^tII11] *Accomplishment of acts* against
*perfect efficacy of works*; *unutterable* against *inexplicable*; and, in the same Greek clause,
*not to be divulged* against *divinely performed* — two different words, the one the modern text
renders and the one Taylor does. Taylor's "divinely performed" answers a word his Greek carries,
which the modern text prints in the facing Greek but not in its English at that clause, so the
divine performer is not an agent Taylor added. **The modern text leaves the seat emptier than
Taylor's does, at the exact point where the passage is saying the seat is empty.** That is a
translation difference that bears on the finding of §3, and it is why this post has quoted both
renderings rather than choosing the cleaner one.

The third locus puts a person where the modern text puts a process. On the division of the theurgic
act: the modern text reads "the whole of theurgy presents a double aspect",[^cIV2] Taylor "in all
theurgical operations the priest sustains a twofold character".[^tIV2] **Taylor's subject is a man
and the modern text's subject is the rite**, and they assign the doubleness to different objects.
The same divergence appears in the vocabulary of authority on the next clause: the modern text has
the art "both naturally invokes the powers from the universe as superiors, inasmuch as the invoker
is a man, and yet on the other hand gives them orders, since it invests itself, by virtue of the
ineffable symbols, with the hieratic role of the gods",[^cIV2] where Taylor has a *he* who "is
invested with the sacred form of the Gods".[^tIV2] **One is a possession and one is a costume; one
has a wearer and one does not.**

There is a documented reason for the divergence on Taylor's side. His own Introduction states his
view of the work's condition and of his chief source:

> "The difficulties attending the translation of this work into English are necessarily great"

— Taylor's Introduction to his translation.[^tIntro] They arise, he says, not only from the work's
"sublimity and novelty" but also "from the defects of the original".[^tIntro] And on the annotations
he used:

> "he shows himself to be an inaccurate, impertinent, and garrulous smatterer."

— Taylor's Introduction, of Gale.[^tIntro] **Taylor is not describing a text he trusts.** His notes
record where his text was short: of one detail in the discussion of prayer, "This particular
respecting the apples of gold is added from the version of Scutellius, who appears to have
translated this work from a more perfect manuscript than that which was used by Gale."[^tV26n]
**So Taylor's English is partly a translation of a Latin translation of a source that, on his own
account, was not the fullest available** — the same class of finding the last reading made about
his Proclus, now in a second volume by the same translator. **The renderings differ measurably
because they are made from different texts by a man who said so.**

What an apparatus adds that a translation cannot is easiest to state by listing what the modern
edition supplies at these loci. It gives every passage a book-and-section address, so a reader can
find the same sentence in another edition — which is how this post caught the address problem:
Dodds cites this passage as *De mysteriis* I. 11, and the modern edition prints it at II.11. It
gives the editor's cross-references: the note at I.15 pointing back to II.11.96-97,[^cN88] the list
of the author's assertions in the Introduction, which repeats the address among several other book
references,[^cIntro2] and the gloss reading one clause of X.1 as "other than theurgy".[^cN485] It
gives the provenance of the book's divisions, and says plainly that they are a Renaissance
editor's.[^cIntro5] And it gives the editor's judgement of the closing line.[^cN497] **Taylor's
volume gives none of that: a translation, an Introduction, and Additional Notes keyed to page
numbers** — and where it does help, the help is Taylor's opinion of his source, which is not an
apparatus. A reader with only the 1895
volume has the sentences and no way to place them; a reader with only the modern one has the whole
apparatus and a rendering so fluent that the divergences from Taylor's disappear, including the one
at the central passage. **This post needed both, and the reason is not completeness. It is that the
two editions disagree where the book is arguing.**

One last difference belongs here because it is not a rendering at all. Taylor's volume carries the
publisher's note explaining that the 1895 reprint is "an almost exact facsimile of the original
edition, which was first printed in 1821"[^tReprint] — so the copy this post read descends
photographically from Taylor's own book. The modern edition carries the history of the readings
that followed, and the range is wide: the editor records Nilsson calling it a "basic book for
religion in late antiquity", "E. R. Dodds considered it 'a manifesto of irrationalism'", and Des
Places "a breviary of paganism in decline".[^cIntro3] **The same book has been read as the basic
document of a religion and as a manifesto of irrationality, by competent scholars in the last
hundred years, and the modern editor records both before turning to the more recent scholarship
that reads the book otherwise.**

---

## 7. What this reading claims

**One.** *On the Mysteries* is the counter-text to the *Elements of Theology*: the same tradition's
answer from the other side. Where the *Elements* derives a whole cosmos and performs nothing, this
book performs a rite whose efficacy it denies to thought — "it is not pure thought that unites
theurgists to the gods".[^cII11] The joint between the two books is the joint between a proof and a
practice.

**Two.** The seat is removed, not relocated. Three moves take efficacy from the practitioner — the
rite's power is not intellectual, is "not … dependent upon us", and operates "even when we are not
engaged in intellection" — and give it to acts and symbols that "perform their appropriate work" by
themselves.[^cII11] The gods are not inclined toward the worshipper; the worshipper is disposed
toward the gods.[^cI12] The divine "is literally united with itself", and prayer is not one person
addressing another.[^cI15] Where the *Elements* had an absent operator standing beside the text,
this book has an operator told, in the text, that the work is not his.

**Three.** The uncheckable part of the frame is the practice, and the book says so. The symbols that
carry the efficacy are "unutterable" and "understood solely by the gods";[^cII11] the ground of the
system is that "reason is not even capable of grasping it";[^cI21] the sacred names must not be
altered.[^cVII5] **This is the third case the instrument was missing: not a seat, not a claim, but
an act whose operative content is declared to be the gods' knowledge** — uncheckable by
construction, with the construction in the doctrine's own sentence.

**Four.** The correction: the blog's questions all locate the uncheckable part somewhere a reader
can reach — a person whose authority is borrowed, a premise that is not demonstrated — and this
frame has neither. Iamblichus removes the seat, puts the efficacy into an act, and declares the
act's operative content to be the gods' knowledge. **The test gains a third question: is the
frame's uncheckable part a person, a claim, or a practice, and does the frame say which? This book
answers "a practice", and says so, and is uncheckable anyway** — which is the harder half: candour
is not a safeguard, and it does not restore anything to the reader.

**Five.** The counterweights, in the body, are three. The book is a point-by-point refutation whose
proportions are its opponent's, and its division into books is a Renaissance arrangement, so the
addresses cited here are not the author's. The ground is uncheckable as the argument requires — a
structural cost to describe, not adjudicate: the book's account cannot be verified from outside the
practice, and nothing here claims it is false. And the renderings disagree measurably where the book
argues: on what does the uniting (*pure thought* against *a conception of the mind*), on whether the
acts have a performer (*divinely performed* against *not to be divulged*), and on whether the
doubleness belongs to the man or the rite. Neither edition is the book.

What the blog takes from the twenty-second reading is the completion of a run of four. [The Orphic
Hymns](/the-orphic-hymns/) perform a cosmology and assert nothing. [The Chaldean
Oracles](/the-chaldean-oracles/) assert one and station no one. [Proclus' Theology of
Plato](/proclus-theology-of-plato/) argues one and gives every being its place. [The
Elements](/proclus-elements-of-theology/) proves one and performs nothing. And *On the Mysteries*
performs one — and tells the reader, in the same breath as the doctrine, that the part of the
performance which does the work is the part the reader cannot have. **The frame that removes the
seat is not the frame that leaves something to check. It is the frame that puts the whole of its
efficacy in an act, and then says, in the sentence that states it, that the act is understood by
the gods alone.** The blog has spent this series asking where the authority sits. This book answers
that nobody's authority is doing the work — and that this is not the safer arrangement, because a
rite nobody owns is a rite nobody can examine either.

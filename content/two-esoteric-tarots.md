# Two Esoteric Tarots

### Seventh of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the first frame the blog has been asked to read that fits in a hand, two researchers each arguing from their own book, and a centuries-old dispute that can be audited from a library by anyone with no stake in the answer

*Seventh of the `readings`, and the first whose unit is not an argument but an **object**. The frames
series — [the frame is a variable](/the-frame-is-a-variable/) through [the cosmos that
refuses](/the-cosmos-that-refuses/) — is about cosmologies: what a culture takes reality to be, whether a
frame names an endpoint, whether it leaves **a seat**. This book is about two decks of cards, and a deck
is a frame of a different shape — a fixed table of correspondences, a set of positions, and a reader who
supplies the reading. It is a frame you carry in a pocket, lay out on a table and read aloud: the first
frame in the series that **fits in a hand**.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source. The quotations are verbatim from a complete digital text of
the edition, checked character for character against it, and cited in the notes **by part** and **by speaker** — because
this book is a **recorded conversation**, not a monograph: every claim in it belongs to the person who made
it, not to "the book". Typographic quotes are rendered here as straight ASCII, and dashes are given as em dashes except
inside numeric ranges (which keep its en dashes); the spaces its italics markup leaves around punctuation are closed; a quotation's opening
capital is normalised to its position in the sentence; and the source's
footnote numbers are dropped from quotations. The reading of the quotations is an **inference**, offered
as one. **The book's
cosmology is not endorsed** — nothing here claims the cards carry real magical power, and nothing claims
they do not. No living author or scholar is diagnosed, the Dummett-Yates dispute is described as a
structure and **not adjudicated**, and nothing here validates, disqualifies or restricts anyone's
practice.*

## 1. What the book is, and why it needs a reading rather than a quotation

*Two Esoteric Tarots* is by Peter Mark Adams and Christophe Poncet, "Convened & with a foreword by César
Pedreros", published by Scarlet Imprint in 2023.[^tet] Its front matter says what it is in the convenor's
own sentence:

> "A conversation was convened for the Sagittarius Full Moon of 3rd June 2023. This is the record of
> that meeting of minds."[^fw]

That sentence governs this reading. The book is twelve parts long and **every part opens with a question
from Pedreros** — a diffident, well-read interviewer in Santiago de Chile, who ends his foreword with a
proverb: "Tell me and I'll forget, show me and I may remember, involve me and I'll understand."[^fw] Adams
is the author of *The Game of Saturn: Decoding the Sola-Busca Tarocchi* (2017); Poncet of a forthcoming
*The Tarot of Marsilio*.[^tet] Both have brought a thesis; the convenor has brought a question.[^names]

The blog owes this one a reading because **the blog has never read the tarot**, and the tarot is a
frame. Every case study in this series has been about an environment: something a person is inside, which
supplies the meaning of a state that cannot read itself. A deck of cards is not an environment. It is a
thing, and it is used — and because it is a thing it can be exhibited, dated and checked, which is what §4
is about. Two absences belong at the top: the text carries **no page numbers**, so every reference here is
by part and by speaker; and this reading never saw the cards.[^tet]

## 2. The frame in a hand — the correspondence, the positions, the reader

Here is the post's central reading, and it is an inference from the book's descriptions.

[The owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) distilled the series into three questions: *does the frame name
the endpoint? Is it checkable by someone who does not benefit? And does it leave a seat?* A tarot deck
answers all three — by being built out of three parts that are not a cosmology at all.

**One: a correspondence system** — a fixed, finite table in which each card is an attribute. Poncet states
one with unusual precision, working from a passage in Ficino's commentary on Plato's *Timaeus* in which
the soul is "compared to a triple septenary, so a three times seven structure":

> "A three times seven grid has twenty-one cells, so one for each arcanum of the tarot, given that two of
> the trumps form a single arcanum: the one that has no name (*Arcanum XIII*) and the one that has no
> number (*The Fool*), forming together the inferior arcanum of Saturn. In this way, the grid could be
> seen as the hidden structure of the Tarot de Marseille, within which all the trumps could be
> ordered."[^x]

That is a **table**: three levels of being by seven planets, twenty-one cells, one card each — and a
closed one, since "If we think of the game as a combinatorial system, you need every part to make it work.
So, it's a complete system and each part has its function."[^ix] A complete system in which each part has a
function is a **vocabulary**. [The cosmos that refuses](/the-cosmos-that-refuses/) described "The frame with
a correspondence and no endpoint"; this one has both, and the endpoint is small.

**Two: a set of positions**, described from the far side while Adams decodes an unrelated deck:
"Considered in isolation, the cards are inherently polysemous. We therefore need to assemble a group of
cards in order to understand how they function together",[^iii] and again, "The recovery of the 'sense' —
the cards' deeper narratives — requires that these figures be grouped together in specific
combinations."[^ix] That is the spread, stated as a requirement: the positions are **not in the deck**. The
deck supplies a vocabulary, the deal supplies the grammar, and the grammar is the part nobody owns.

**Three: a reader** — and the book's most exact account of that reader is Adams's definition of the
esoteric, which he grounds not in doctrine but in an occasion: "The 'esoteric tarot' rests fully upon a
performative occasion; one in which there is a distinct experience of some degree of automatism — whether
conceived as entering a state of flow, intuition or external facilitation." And then this:

> "Therein, for a space of time, the reader and the reading appear to occupy two different worlds, the
> reading appears to have taken on a life, and a direction, of its own."[^perf]

So the deck has a table, a set of positions, and a person who speaks. **Does it name the endpoint?** Yes —
but the endpoint is an **utterance**. A cosmology names where you are going; a deck names what is the case,
now, about a question the querent brought. It is a smaller claim than any frame in the series, and a
stranger one: it is made in the first person by someone in the room.

**Is it checkable by someone who does not benefit?** Here the object does what no cosmology could: **it
splits the question in two.** The table is public, fixed and inert — anyone can check whether a card sits
where a table says it sits, and the check changes nothing. The utterance is not checkable, and is issued by
someone who benefits from its holding. [The owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) required a single check; the
deck hands the blog a checkable component and an uncheckable one welded into one object. Which of the two
has been argued over is §4's answer: the table.

**Does it leave a seat?** Yes, and this is the reading's sharpest point — because here the seat is
visible. The owned cosmos's seat is inside the frame and has to be inferred; a deck puts its mediator in
the chair opposite you with the cards in their hands. The series' finding was that "the frame is not the
harm. The closed seat is." A reading is a seat **open by default** — and it closes at one moment, which
Adams's sentence names exactly: when the reader reports that "the reading appears to have taken on a life,
and a direction, of its own." That is the structural shape of [the machine
said so](/the-machine-said-so/): an authority that cannot be argued with, because there is nobody to argue
with — except that here there *is* somebody present, and the automatism claim is what removes them from the
line of responsibility. The move which closes this seat is a claim about **who is speaking**.

**And the book supplies the blog with the adjective.** Adams describes time in the Sola-Busca's ritual
programme and reaches for the word twice: "we encounter time in its most instrumental sense";[^ix] "we find
ourselves at a very instrumental end of the spiritual spectrum, one more usually associated with goetia than
with classical theurgy."[^ix] *Instrumental* is the right word for
the whole object. A cosmology is a frame you are in; a deck is a frame you **operate**. And Adams's account
of the art of memory gives the mechanism by which an operable frame can also be durable, because a
transient experience must be encoded so it can be re-run:

> "Because of the transience of these experiences, imagistic modes of religiosity require that their core
> ritual processes, iconic imagery and visionary outcomes — rather than a body of fixed doctrine — be
> encoded in a form that allows the restitution of the ritual and verification of its efficacy and
> visionary outcomes."[^xi]

Read that as a specification and it is a specification for a **carrier**: not a doctrine but a re-runnable
apparatus — and a deck of cards is its smallest working example.

## 3. The claims, and who makes them

The book's headline sentences are not the researchers'. They are in the Foreword, and the Foreword is the
convenor's:

> "Scholarly research has firmly maintained that the tarot had no spiritual, esoteric or divinatory
> dimension before the mid-eighteenth century. However, two independent experts have shattered that
> consensus with their original research."[^fw]

(the text sets the opening in small capitals; the reading is the same). Then three claims in order, with
the second of them the sentence this book is known for:

> "The evidence he presents unequivocally challenges the notion that the tarot served as a mere game of
> chance; instead, it is an artefact imbued with a magical purpose. It is evident that this tarot, dating
> back to c.1490, predates the commonly accepted time frame of esoteric use by almost three centuries."[^fw]

and, "simultaneously, akin to a Jungian synchronicity", Poncet "had discerned unmistakable traces of
Platonic philosophy within the imagery of the Tarot de Marseille", unearthing "connections between the
Florentine aristocracy, the Medici family, Marsilio Ficino, Botticelli, and the heretical philosophical
underpinnings of the Italian Renaissance."[^fw]

**Note the address, because it is the finding.** *"An artefact imbued with a magical purpose"* is César
Pedreros, in a foreword, summarising Adams's book for a reader he is inviting in. It is not a sentence
Adams writes about his own research, and §6 reports what the two researchers write instead. The strongest
sentence in the book is written by the person with the least to lose.

**Adams's claim** is about the Sola-Busca tarocchi, an engraved fifteenth-century Ferrarese deck, whose
isolation he establishes first: it "appears to have been preserved within titled circles until its purchase
by the Italian state in 2009 — a period of some five hundred years", and "only appeared in the public
domain on four occasions".[^vii] The list prints a **first** (Cicognara, 1831), a **second** (Arthur M.
Hind's photographs at the British Museum, 1907) and a **fourth** (Wolfgang Mayer's reproduction, 1998) —
and no third. That is a whole-text observation, reported as one.[^abs] Then the claim itself:

> "At the deepest level I found that the Sola-Busca's iconographic programme was structured around two key
> rituals. The first was an extremely archaic ritual, one attested as early as 700 BCE and still practiced
> by occult groups today. It is called 'Drawing Down the Moon.'"[^iii]

the first of which, in his reading, "is creating a magical child or homunculus that will then be
'sacrificed' by immolation in the next stage of the ritual as part of an invocation of the saturnian deity
or current (whether encompassed under the name of Ammon, Kronos or Saturn)",[^iii] and the second of which
is the deck's real purpose: "It is a rite of theurgical invocation whereby the operator seeks to become
imbued with the draconian energies of the deity."[^iii] The implement on the card he can name — "known as
Hekate's Top", "described in the principal text of theurgical practice, the *Chaldean Oracles*" — and
about it he makes the strongest evidential claim in the book: "As far as I know, this is the only depiction
of a theurgical operation in the entire oeuvre of both esoteric and Renaissance art."[^iii] The summary:
"Uniquely, the Sola-Busca appears to have embodied the actual ritual processes for performing theurgy."[^vi]

Two features of the claim's **form** belong here. The first is that the designer is said to have hidden it
deliberately, and the hiding is part of the argument: "The designer has sought to hide the ritual intent
through the use of additional references that draw our attention away from the performative ritual
narrative being enacted."[^iii] A claim in which concealment is a prediction can be strong evidence; it can
also absorb counter-evidence. The second is Adams's own marking of its status — "My **suggestion** is",
"a reasonable **conjecture**" — with the date resting on an attribution he does not make: "assuming that De
Marchi is right about the engraver's identity".[^vii]

**Poncet's claim** is about the Tarot de Marseille, and it begins in a bedroom: "On my nineteenth birthday,
my girlfriend gifted me a tarot deck. A Tarot de Marseille. I was amazed by the images of the trump
cards."[^i] His method is a dating argument by costume, since playing cards are perishable and he has only
the images: the "Authorities" (Popess, Pope, Emperor, Empress, Justice) are "inspired by a single series of
etchings by the Florentine engraver Baccio Baldini, the *Prophets and Sibyls*, dated circa 1471", putting
the prototype in northern or central Italy around 1471.[^i] Then Plato — "Plato's chariot of the soul, for
instance, is obviously reflected in the *Chariot* card of the Tarot de Marseille, in a spectacular
way"[^i] — and then the intersection of the two, which produces the thesis:

> "Someone, it may have been Ficino or a member of his Academy, figured out that images can convey Ideas.
> The game of tarot was popular at that time, so they imagined using its structure and figures to embed
> philosophical images and concepts."[^iv]

with the refinement that keeps it from being stock Platonism — "I discovered that the images of the tarot
are not so much Plato's, but rather Ficino's interpretation of Plato's images, and it's noticeably
different"[^i] — and the date: "My research shows that the Tarot de Marseille was designed between 1470
and 1475."[^ix] He then declines the definition the book was convened to supply:

> "I cannot give you a definition of the esoteric or occult tarot. What I believe for certain, however,
> is that, very early in the history of the tarot, some decks had uses other than gaming."[^def]

**The shared claim**, in Poncet's words, is the sentence the two researches are joined to:

> "In this way, two different decks of the fifteenth century revealed themselves as spectacular examples
> of esotericism in tarot, more than three hundred years before the earliest date previously accepted by
> academic consensus."[^def]

One small thing worth reporting rather than smoothing: the Foreword says "almost three centuries" and
Poncet says "more than three hundred years". The gap proves nothing, and it is the kind of arithmetic a
reading should not tidy on the author's behalf.

## 4. The check: a dispute that can be audited from a library

This is the part of the book the blog has direct use for, because the blog's test arrives here as an
actual dispute between named people, in a public record, with a stated standard of evidence.

Pedreros's question frames it: "Or was Michael Dummett right when he insisted that there was no such thing
as an esoteric tarot before the French occultists 'discovered' it in the eighteenth century?"[^viii] Adams
answers by naming what the disagreement is **about**, and quoting his opponent:

> "The central issue rests on Dummett's claim that 'no occult significance was attached to the cards, nor
> any use made of them save for playing games, until the intervention of Court de Gébelin in 1781.'"[^viii]

That sentence is the whole of the negative position, and it comes with a date and a citation. So does the
dispute it belongs to: Yates's review of Dummett's *The Game of Tarot* in the *New York Review of Books*
for 19 February 1981, Dummett's reply on 14 May 1981, and at the end of it the terminal condition:

> "Rather than rehearse the intricacies of this exchange the issue rested, finally, on Dummett's appeal
> for, and openness towards, evidence overturning his statement."[^viii]

**Read that sentence twice, because it is the finding.** Dummett's claim, as the book reports it, **named
its own falsifier**: it asked for the evidence that would refute it. That is the opposite of a closed
seat, and it is the property [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) asked of every frame and almost never
found.

Then the evidence, in two kinds. The first is a document: "The first piece of evidence comes from the
records of the Inquisition. A Venetian courtesan, Isabella Bellochio, was stated to have 'worshipped an
image of the devil by kneeling before it, with her hair loose, while maintaining a lantern alight before
it day and night … a light which burned continuously in the kitchen in front of a devil and the
tarocchi …'"[^viii] Adams reads it as use rather than design, and then offers a physical observation,
**marking its ceiling in the same breath**: the card "appears to have been thumb-marked … which is not, of
course, evidence of ritual use but it certainly points towards prolonged and sustained contemplation of the
image."[^viii] The second kind is iconographic:

> "Today, the evidence for the existence of occluded subtexts deliberately hidden within the pre-Gébelin
> tarot is threefold. It comprises the detailed analyses of the iconography of the Sola-Busca (Adams);
> the early Tarot de Marseille (Poncet); and the Noblet Tarot de Marseille (Appel)."[^viii]

with Adams's conclusion and Poncet's echo: "So the answer to the question about the 'missing evidence' is
that it was never missing. Some of it existed in official records; the rest, by far the more impactful
portion, was hidden, rather artfully, in plain sight for these last five hundred years."[^viii] — "The
cards themselves were the 'missing evidence.'"[^viii]

Now the reading, and it is not a verdict on the argument.

**The strongest thing in this book is not that either side wins. It is that the dispute is checkable.**
Every item in it is a dated, third-party-readable object: Dummett's *The Game of Tarot* (1980); the review
and reply of 1981; Decker, Depaulis and Dummett's *A Wicked Pack of Cards* (1996); Martin's *Witchcraft and
the Inquisition in Venice* (1989); and the deck itself, held by the Italian state since 2009 and, since the
Brera's digitisation, available as images.[^vii] A person with no stake in either outcome can read all of
it. That is "a check that does not benefit from the answer", supplied not by a frame but by a **record** —
and one that cannot be converted into a credential, the way [the owned
cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) found the checks of a named-endpoint frame can: "rites, lineages, initiations,
proofs of access". Bibliographies do not convert, and the object cannot be argued out of the museum.

**The boundary of this reading has to be stated as plainly as the finding.** I have not read Dummett,
Depaulis or Yates. Everything above about what they held is a quotation or citation *given by this book*,
and the book's own characterisation of them — Poncet's, in Part II — is a summary this post repeats as a
summary: "For them, the occult or esoteric tarot could only be an invention of the French occultists."[^def]
What can be verified here is the **shape and the addressability of the dispute** — that its items are
named, dated and public — not its resolution, and not the fairness of this book's account of Dummett. The
post takes no side.

Two further things the book puts in the record. Poncet's characterisation of Dummett — "He was a very
intelligent man. … but he seems not to have been familiar with the culture of the Italian Renaissance"[^viii]
— is a claim about a scholar's competence made inside a conversation, quoted here **as Poncet's
characterisation**, not as a finding. And Yates's contribution is given as a word choice: "To name the
capacity of the cards to embed deep performative knowledge, Yates chose the word 'hieroglyph.'"[^viii] The
word is the argument: a Renaissance hieroglyph is an image that embeds ideas **and power**.

## 5. The lineage, and the warrant of an artwork

[The environment](/the-environment/)'s inventory has a lever for this: **"A pre-certified frame — the
warrant is issued by the *host*, not the group, and scrutiny reads as bigotry."** In the case studies the
host was a culture and the frame was a teacher's. Here the warrant is claimed for **an artwork**, by two
named living researchers, and the certifying authority is a name and a tradition. Same lever, different
carrier.

Adams lays out the lineage in Part VI. Its content is a transfer:
"Giovanni Aurispa returned from Constantinople in 1423 and again in 1427 … bringing hundreds of Hellenistic
texts with him";[^vi] Plethon is present in the Italian city states; the result is "a second Hellenistic
Renaissance", grounded in practice rather than doctrine, since "their significance — especially the works
of Proclus and Iamblichus — was the performativity that was fundamental to their approach as practicing
polytheistic Hellenists."[^vi] Then the
mechanism — the bridge that would make a *card* operative at all:

> "The fact that these beliefs threatened to undermine the institutional power of church and state meant
> that they could only be preserved by being occluded within other media. From the proactive perspective
> enjoined by theurgists, such as Proclus, the influence of higher order energies, or deities, could be
> embodied via their geometrical forms in artworks that would, henceforth, emanate their distinctive
> presence into the world."[^vi]

And the warrant's own shape, named with its Latin term: the texts "were conceived as emerging from a fount
of pristine wisdom (*prisca theologia*) at a time close to the act of creation, and were thereby thought
to be imbued with far greater depth, wisdom and verisimilitude".[^vi]

**The structural point is this: the authority being invoked is also the object of the study.** Ficino is
both the evidence and the credential. In [the certified frame](/the-certified-frame/) the warrant arrived
from a host culture that had not scrutinised the teacher; in *Star.Ships* it was self-issued in the first
person. Here the researcher's **subject** supplies the warrant — the one warrant no amount of scrolling in
the source will separate from the source.

**And there is a brake to apply immediately, which is this series' own.** The lever
[the environment](/the-environment/) named is about **predation** — the price a predator does not have to
pay. There is no predator in this book: two researchers have written about two decks, and nobody is
recruiting anyone. What the blog owes is the lever's **shape** and not its **charge** — not "is this
being used on someone" but "who issued this warrant, and would the issuer recognise the use it is being put
to". Applied here the second question has a checkable answer, because Ficino's own position on planetary
influence is in the text:

> "I don't think the planetary divinities mentioned in most of Ficino's astrological writings, and thus
> the grid I just described for the Tarot de Marseille, should be understood as referring to celestial
> bodies acting on things and people."[^x]

For Ficino, on this account, "the planets have no influence on the sublunar world", and astrological
allusions are "analogies put at the service of the description of souls."[^x] So the warrant and the claim
are **not the same claim**: Ficino certifies the *design*, not the *operation*. Adams's Sola-Busca, by
contrast, is explicitly a matter of forces and their timing — "Mars, Saturn and chthonic Mercury under the
influence of the most malefic of fixed stars, Algol", and "a technocratic, astrologically-driven vision of
the universe as a collection of manipulable forces."[^ix]

**What would count as evidence for the continuity claim** is also stated, as an absence, in the exact form
a document would have had to take: "Such writings do not state 'I have created a tarot deck that embodies
my ideas and theurgical practices,' as the followers of Dummett seem to expect, because their authors
couldn't express themselves openly."[^viii] That has a shape this series has seen: the document that cannot
exist because writing it was dangerous is the document that cannot falsify anything. It is **also** a
historically ordinary situation — Renaissance heretics were burned — and this post does not adjudicate
between the two readings of the absence. Adams then reports the modern tradition in the first person —
"Twenty-five years ago, I met a tarot teacher who enlightened me as to the existence of an initiatory
tradition in relation to the tarot … accompanied by 'signs following' that serve to confirm the validity of
the transmission"[^perf] — and the
check it names, *signs following*, is exactly the class of check [the owned
cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) found converts tests into credentials.

## 6. Counterweights, in the body

**First: the strongest language in the book is not the researchers'. It is the convenor's, and the counts
make it plain.** The Foreword is **1.8 per cent** of the text. In it are the sentences quoted at §3: "firmly maintained", "shattered
that consensus", "unequivocally challenges", "It is evident that". Across the whole text the word
"**unequivocally**" occurs **once**, and it is there.[^abs] Now the other half: in the rest of the
text, a test list of fourteen hedge markers returns **64** occurrences and **0** of them fall in the
Foreword — "**probably**" 20 times, "**seems**" 10, "**likely**" 8, "**may have**" 7, "**apparently**" 4,
"**perhaps**" 3, "**to my mind**" twice, and the singletons "**I guess**", "**I gather**", "**tentatively**",
"**My suggestion**" and "**conjecture**".[^abs] That is not deception: a foreword is a recommendation,
written in the register of recommendation. But **the paragraph with the least hedging in the book is the one
a reader is most likely to quote.**

**Second: the dating rests on named conditionals, and the book names them.** The deck's date is not read off
a document. It runs an engraver attribution (De Marchi's, with Adams adding "I personally find his
attribution convincing based upon stylistic comparisons"),[^vii] a date string "encoded" on trump XIIII
Bocho and connected to a reparations payment, and the c.1490 or 1491 window — "assuming that De Marchi is
right … a reasonable conjecture."[^vii] **The evidence for a c.1490 magical purpose is an inference from
pictures, made by the person whose book argues for it.**

**Third, and this is the central counterweight: a complete secular account of the same artefact sits inside
this book**, and the artefact cannot choose between them. On Adams's own reading of the deck's political
context, the occasion is the Salt War: "The 1482–84 Salt War with Venice had essentially bankrupted the state and destroyed a large portion of her
agricultural economy."[^vii] The arms on the cards are those of "the Venetian patrician Venier-Sanudo family"; the
initials and the date point "towards the Venetian patrician, Marin Sanudo, as their intended recipient",
who was Venice's rapporteur on Ferrara's reparations claim; and the date "also marked the date at which
Ferrara received the first tranche of reparations from Venice", about which Adams asks, "A
coincidence?"[^vii] His conclusion is a gift hypothesis: "The cards may well have served as a 'reward' for Sanudo's support
since they are precisely the kind of unique, expensive, visually engaging artefact that he, a celebrated
bibliophile, would subsequently have displayed in his house."[^vii] In terms of what a third party can check, the **political** account is the
stronger: it has dates, names and a treasury entry, and the magical one has the design.

So the same deck has, in one book, a documented diplomatic occasion with an encoded date, and a claimed
magical purpose read from its pictures — and the pictures are the evidence for both. A reading cannot
choose, and this one does not. What it can report is that the stronger of the two claims, in terms of what
a third party can check, is the **political** one, because it has dates, names and a treasury entry, and
the magical one has the design.

**Fourth: both speakers are arguing for their own prior work, and neither is an academic — one of them says
so.** Poncet: "I note in passing that none of us three is an academic".[^xii] Adams is the author of the
book under discussion and Poncet of the forthcoming one that closes the Marseille argument:
self-interested frame claims, made by interested parties, in a Scarlet Imprint edition. That is a structure
and not an accusation — a claim's interest does not make it false, and §4 is about why it does not need to
be taken on trust either. And **the evidence chain has two interpreters at the end of it, both of them the
authors of the theses being tested**: of the threefold evidence at §4, two of the three analyses are by the
two people in the conversation.[^viii] That is not a fault of the book — but it is why the blog can **read**
this book and cannot **rest** on it.

## 7. The negative finding: safeguard, metered pace, clinical referral

The series reports the presence or absence of a safeguard, a metered pace and a clinical referral as
**search results**, scoped to the text of this edition. This book's results are unambiguous, and each is a different kind
of absence.

**"Safeguard" occurs once, and it is not a safeguard of the reader.** It is the convenor's framing of a
question about transmission: "Throughout history, there has been a prevailing belief that esoteric
teachings should be **safeguarded** and transmitted orally, exclusively to those prepared to receive
them."[^xi] The word belongs to the protection of **knowledge**, held by an institution against the wrong
people. It is not a protection of a person from an experience, and the two are not the same instrument.

**There is no metered pace.** "Pace" returns **six** hits in the text and **not one of them is a rate**:
every occurrence is inside the word "space" — a space of time, a ritual space, the twenty-two spaces of the
trumps, a political space, the efficacious space of the convenor's dreams.[^abs] Nothing in the book says
how often, how much, or how fast. And here a distinction the previous six readings did not have to make:
**this is not a book with no practice.** It states that a deck was an operative ritual apparatus, describes
the operation, dates its apparatus to "as early as 700 BCE" and notes that it is "still practiced by occult
groups today".[^iii] A reader who took any of it as a manual would hold a manual's shape and none of a
manual's fences. The nearest thing to a discipline in the book is a discipline of **work** — "you would need
to be an astrologer to make all of the calculations", "highly disciplined — which is, of course, a highly
Saturnian quality."[^ix]

**There is no clinical referral at all, and the vocabulary of risk is almost wholly absent.** A
case-insensitive search returns **0** hits for "danger", "warn" and "warning", "doctor", "therapy",
"therapist", "psychiatric", "psychiatry", "psychotic", "clinical", "hospital", "crisis", "suicid", "mental
health", "breakdown", "seek help", "professional help", "self-harm", "mentor" and "dose".[^abs] Four terms
do occur and none is a referral: "**risk**" **once**, a historical risk the source names without a date; "**precaution**" **once**, and it is Ficino's; "**careful**" five times, every one about
**reading images**; and "**madness**" twice, once as Neoplatonic doctrine.[^abs] Not one is addressed to a
person's safety.

Read as a structure rather than a gap, that is a **different result** from the last two readings.
*Undreaming Wetiko* named psychiatry as an adversary; *Star.Ships* named it not at all, and had no practice
to fence. This book is the third case: it issues no instruction, and it does describe an operative ritual —
so the absence here is not merely the absence of a subject that never arose. It is a **shape without its
fences**: a book that has told the reader that a deck was built to perform a rite, and that has nothing to
say about what happens to anyone who tries. The honest reading of that is limited — a search shows only what
it indexes, and a conversation between two researchers about fifteenth-century artefacts is not the genre in
which a route to a clinician would be expected.

## 8. The correction the blog owes

One correction, stated once, and then its second clause.

> **The blog's frame inventory has been a list of environments, and it now needs an object. A tarot deck
> is not a cosmology: it is a fixed correspondence system, plus a set of positions, plus a reader who
> supplies the reading, and it is carried, dealt and read rather than lived in. It supplies the series with
> a frame type the case studies could not reach — a frame made portable and instrumental — and it splits
> the blog's single test in two, because the table is public, fixed and checkable while the utterance is
> issued in the first person by someone who benefits from it. The blog had one question for a frame. An
> object asks two, and the history has been argued on the half that can survive being checked.**

The clause that follows is about [the environment](/the-environment/)'s fourth lever — "a pre-certified
frame". That lever was written about a culture supplying the warrant for a **teacher**, and the blog has
never had to carry it into a case where the warrant is claimed by named living researchers for an
**artwork**. Here it is, and the carrier is different in a way that matters: the authority invoked is the
object of study. So the second correction is narrower than the first and sharper:

> **The warrant lever has a second carrier — a researcher claiming a warrant for an artwork, with the
> certifying authority being the very material under investigation — and the blog should carry its shape
> without importing its charge. There is no predator in this book. What the lever is good for here is a
> question, not an accusation: does the authority cited certify the claim being made with it? In this book
> the answer is checkable, and it is *no* — Ficino's planets do not influence the sublunar world, on the
> book's own account of him, and the deck that needs them to is not his.**

Neither correction touches anything the blog has argued about frames, seats or checks. Both change what
the blog may say it read: a series about cosmologies has just taken an object as a source, and a series
about warrants has just found one issued for a picture.

## 9. What a reading is for

A reading holds one source whole and asks what a blog that has quoted sources all along now has to change.
This one returns a convergence, a correction and a gap.

**The convergence is that the blog's test arrived as a real dispute, and survived it.** The series'
requirement — *a check that does not benefit from the answer* — has been found missing in six successive
readings, in every cosmology it has examined. Here the blog's question about frames is being fought over
by named people, in dated, public, third-party-readable documents, over an object that sits in a national
gallery, with one of the parties on record as asking for the evidence that would refute him. That is a
frame being checked by someone who does not benefit — not by an insider's rite, not by a lineage, not by a
credential of any kind.

**The correction is an inventory correction with a hypothesis inside it.** The series has four frames, all
cosmologies, and its rule about frames was written at cosmological scale: keep the endpoint unnamed, keep
the route plural, keep a check that does not stand to gain. A deck is the first frame the blog has met
that **names its endpoint, has a single fixed route, and sits in a hand** — and it is not a failing frame,
because it is not claiming to be an environment. It is an instrument, and an instrument's check is whether
the thing works: a different question from whether a cosmology is true, and one the blog has never asked
in this register. That does not weaken the rule. It locates it: **the rule about frames is a rule about
environments.**

**And the gap is the one the blog keeps finding.** Two researchers, each the author of the thesis he is
defending, are the only interpreters at the end of the evidence they have assembled; the strongest sentence
in the book is the convenor's; the dating is an inference from imagery, marked as such by the person making
it. This book supplies a **public record** and not a second reader of the object — and the record,
unusually, is enough for the one thing the blog needed, which is that the argument be auditable. The
blog can cite the dispute. It cannot cite either party to it.

Which leaves the reading's own bottom line, handed over almost unrepaired by the researcher who has spent the
most years with a deck bought for him on a nineteenth birthday: the Tarot de Marseille "with its
multilayered content and combinatorial structure, is a fantastic instrument to produce unique experiences
in the reader, and in this way excludes any form of literality."[^xi] The blog has been saying a version
of that about frames at every scale — that the frame supplies the reading, and that whoever supplies the
reading holds the lever. It now has it from inside the trade, in a sentence that does not know it is a
finding: **an instrument produces experiences. It does not certify them.**

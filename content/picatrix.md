# The Picatrix

### Seventeenth of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the cosmology the blog has been quoting all along, stated as equipment rather than argued, and an office whose holder the text never names

*Seventeenth of the `readings`. [The owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) named the frame this blog has spent a series documenting from below — a cosmos with a legible correspondence, a point where the worlds meet, a route that can be worked, and therefore **a seat**; because a frame that says "this is the way through" has said, in the same breath, "there is a way through that can be pointed to." [Geosophia](/geosophia/) read the operations underneath the Western material and found a hazard named in order to be refused. [Black Abbot, White Magic](/black-abbot-white-magic/) read a grimoire hidden inside a cipher. [The western column](/the-western-column/) read the West as a syllabus and found its safeguards written into the lessons. All three read material that **argues** — a thesis defended, a manuscript decoded, a curriculum taught. This book argues nothing. It is a manual, and its cosmos arrives the way a table of logarithms arrives: as something you use.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source, the complete *Picatrix* in the Greer and Warnock translation, with its Warning and Disclaimer, its introduction, its annotations and its Necronomicon section read as part of the specimen. Every quotation is verbatim, checked character for character against a digital text of the edition, and cited in the notes by Book and chapter where a note is given — the book's own chapter titles are the address used here. Its rendering is in places degraded: drop capitals are mangled, letter-spaced words appear as spaced runs, page breaks and footnote numbers interrupt mid-sentence, and the front matter sets one chapter title as "H o w you may k n o w your place". Quotations are normalised character for character across those artefacts and are given in the words the edition prints. The reading of the material is an **inference**, offered as one. **The book's cosmology is not endorsed** — nothing here claims that the correspondence holds, that the images work, that the spirits exist, or that any of it is false. No living person is diagnosed; the translators are described as a frame and not assessed as people; and nothing here validates, disqualifies or restricts anyone's practice. As everywhere in this series: describe structures, diagnose no one.*

## 1. The book the blog has been quoting, and the quotation at one remove

**The blog has named the *Picatrix* in six places, and every one of them is inside a single post.** [Geosophia](/geosophia/) lists "a whole book of translated **Picatrix** operations" among its sources; reads its operations, in its own view, as descending from "the magical papyri"; calls goetia "the original core, and the earliest designation, of Western magic" and the papyri "the ancestors of the later grimoires"; says its own nearest thing to a safety test is "quoted from the Picatrix"; and prints two passages of it — the Capricorn and then the Aquarius section of something headed "Works of the Moon in the Twelve Sign" — credited to "the translated Picatrix material".

That passage is not in this book's words. A search of this translation returns nothing for any of the phrases the blog has been quoting from it, and no chapter of it carries that title.[^oneaway] The operation the passage belongs to is here, and it reads otherwise: Book Four, chapter two, "When the Moon is in Capricorn". The honest form of the finding is this: **the blog's one direct quotation of the *Picatrix* has been a second translator's rendering, reproduced inside another author's book**, and the blog has never read the book itself. It has been carrying the *Picatrix* as a **benchmark of the operable** — the book that shows what a cosmology looks like when everything in it corresponds and everything can be worked — and has never checked whether that is what the book says.

## 2. What the book is, and what it says it is for

The edition is *Picatrix: The Occult Classic of Astrological Magic, Complete in One Volume*, translated and annotated by John Michael Greer and Christopher Warnock, Adocentyn Press, Liber Rubeus Edition. Its copyright page reads "Copyright © 2010-11 John Michael Greer & Christopher Warnock" and names Renaissance Astrology as publisher, and its own back-cover matter calls it "translated and annotated by the noted scholars, magicians and astrologers John Michael Greer and Christopher Warnock". One edition fact is not statable: the ISBN line is damaged in the rendering and cannot be quoted, so it is left as it stands rather than guessed at.[^ed]

**Its origin is stated by the translators, and stated as uncertain.** The book was "Originally written sometime in the ninth century by an anonymous Arab wizard in North Africa or Spain, and credited in the fashion of the time to the notable Sufi and scholar al-Majriti"; it "was originally titled *Ghayat al-Hakim*". It "was translated into Spanish and Latin at the court of Alfonso the Wise, King of Castile, in the year 1256, and received the new title of Picatrix. Scholars to this day are uncertain what the name means." The **Prologue** puts its own layer on top, and its layer is a translation commission rather than an author's preface:

> "To the praise and glory of the highest and omnipotent God, who reveals the secrets of sciences to those predestined for them, and also for the illumination of those learned in Latin who lack books written by the old philosophers, Alfonso, by the grace of God the most illustrious king of Spain and all of Andalusia, commanded that this book, the name of which is Picatrix, be translated from Arabic into Spanish with all zeal and diligence."[^pro]

The Prologue then gives the book's account of itself — "For the wise, noble and honored philosopher Picatrix compiled this book from two hundred books and many philosophies, and named it with his own name" — and its four-part plan, in which the third book "teaches the properties of the planets and signs ... and how to converse with the spirits of the planets and many other magical workings".

**The genre is the key to everything else.** The translators call it "the most extensive treatise on the philosophy of magic to be written anywhere west of India between Iamblichus' *On the Mysteries* of the fourth century and Cornelius Agrippa's *Three Books of Occult Philosophy* of the sixteenth" — a treatise on the philosophy of magic, and hundreds of pages of operations.

**And it never argues.** The Prologue's first premise is not a claim but a gate: this science "has been hidden by the philosophers, and they have not wished to disclose it to humanity"; they hid it "with all their might" and spoke of it "in signs and similitudes, as though they spoke of other sciences" — and the reason given is consequence, not malice: "if this science were revealed to all humanity, the universe would be thrown into confusion." Nowhere in the four books is there an argument that the correspondence exists. The correspondence is the book's **equipment**.

## 3. The cosmology, as equipment

Book One is where the frame is installed, and its chapter titles *are* the frame: "How you may know your degree in the universe"; "What magic is and what its properties are"; "What the heavens are and of their substance"; "In what degree everything exists in the universe, and how it is known that man is a lesser world and corresponds to the greater world"; and "In what degree everything in the universe exists, and many other profound things, hidden by the wise, that we intend to reveal in this book of ours."[^b1]

The first chapter is not "what the universe is"; it is **how you may know your degree in it**. And the front matter is not even self-consistent: the list at the head of Book One reads "How you may know your **place** in the universe", while the chapter itself is headed "How you may know your **degree** in the universe."

Then the content, and every piece of it is a working instruction. **Knowledge is the ladder**: "the best and most noble gift that God has given to humanity in this world is knowledge", by which we learn "how one thing corresponds to another"; the order runs both ways — "the last is raised up by similarity, step by step, until it corresponds to the first, and descends again from the first in due order until it corresponds with the last" — and it is climbable only through study, because "these secrets cannot be won except through wisdom and study in the sciences." **Magic is defined by what it does** and by its limit, since "this science is too deep and strong for the intellect."

**The image is the instrument, and the instrument is force.** Talismans are called "'violators', because whoever makes an image does so by violence, and makes it by conquering the substance of which it is made." The mechanism is two sentences and they are the whole theory: "these images are made from their proper substances in order that they might receive the aforementioned influence, and this is done at appropriate times. By suffumigation they are strengthened, and spirits are drawn into these images." Which makes *timing* the operative variable — "the virtue of images consists wholly in the election of hours and times of the proper constellations" — and the prerequisite hard: "No one is able to attain this science except by astrology."

**And the heavens are one machine with one output.** The rays from its centre to its circumference "signify the rays that the stars cast upon the earth"; the sphere "has no excess or lack", and "from it all the powers of spirits are summoned" — because "the heavens are the cause of all operations below them."

**And the operator's part is belief.** Chapter Four is the passage the frame turns on, because it makes faith a component of the mechanism:

> "they ought to believe firmly in the works that they do with images, so that what they do will be true and without doubt, nor should they harbor doubts concerning its effectiveness, since this work is not done in order to test or prove whether it be true or not; rather, they should have faith in their mind that it be true."[^b1c4]

The belief is what lets the image "have the power to call upon the cooperation of the intelligence, and of all the primary and principal intelligences, and all the intermediate intelligences"; and the operator's place is stated without flattery — "Among all these intelligences, the human soul or intellect is clearly the lowest that can call upon the cooperation of others." Chapter Six answers its own title in a line, that man "is a little world similar to the great world"; Chapter Seven gives the ladder whole — "All things in the world are ordered in grades according to the things that govern them. The first of all things of this world, nobler, higher, and more perfect than anything found in the world, is God Himself" — and the ascent and descent meet in humanity, "which is nobler than any other thing existing under the sphere of the Moon."

**And the correspondence is a table.** Book Three, chapter one is the equipment's heart and it is a list: "Saturn is the source of the retentive virtue. He rules profound sciences ... Among languages he rules Hebrew and Chaldean; among the external organs the right ear; among internal organs, the spleen ... among professions, those that work with the earth, plowing, digging, extracting minerals, builders, and architects ... among stones, onyx and all black stones; among metals lead, iron and all metals that are black and smell bad." And so on for the seven.

**Which is the section's finding.** This is not a description of the world that happens to permit magic. It is a description of the world **written as a working index**: grades, correspondences, times, substances, intelligences, and an operator at the bottom of a ladder with a rule for climbing it. This is the frame as **infrastructure** — [the differential](/the-differential/)'s word for a frame that operates as plant rather than as claim, arriving here as a chapter list.

## 4. The operations

Books Three and Four are what the equipment does. Reported as claims, at their own scale, without adopting them.

**What an image is, defined.** Book Two, chapter six: "a magical image is nothing other than the power of celestial bodies in the material bodies that they influence", and the proof of a well-made one is that "the spirit that has been placed in the magical image will be apparent in its effects."[^b2c6] Book One, chapter five opens with the operation the blog's Western material has always gestured at:

> "When you wish to make images to cause love between two people, and make love and delight strong and firm between them, make images of the two in their likenesses. Let this be done in the hour of Jupiter or Venus, with Caput Draconis rising, and with the Moon making a good aspect to Venus ..."[^b1c5]

The two figures are joined and buried in the place of the person who is to love. What the chapter does next is more revealing than the operation: it **defines the aspects as feelings** — "the trine is an aspect of perfect love ... The opposition is an aspect of perfect hatred" — the geometry of the sky read as a grammar of relationship.

**How you address a planet.** Book Three, chapter seven is the general procedure, and it opens with a condition rather than a technique:

> "When you desire to speak with any planet or ask it for anything that you need, first and above all else, purify your will and your faith in God, and beware especially that you believe in no other; then cleanse your body and your garments of all dirt."[^b3c7]

Then the method — "dress in clothing dyed the color of that planet, and suffumigate yourself with its suffumigation, and pray its prayer" — and then a long division of the world's business among the seven, governed by a rule narrower than the frame suggests: "you should not seek anything from any planet unless it belongs to his dominion", since Saturn's petitions concern "old people", Mars's "soldiers" and "thieves", and Venus's "women, boys, and girls". And the election has a hard side: "Beware of his detriment and unfortunate aspects ... and do not let him be cadent."[^b3c7b]

**The suffumigations.** Book Four, chapter six gives one per planet, and the scale is the point: compounding recipes measured in tens of ounces, combining plants, minerals and animal parts — "the dried brains of black crows and cranes" — ground together and formed into pills, because they are "the act of exposing a talisman to the smoke of incense, so the magical qualities of the incense enter into the talisman."

**The times.** Book Four, chapter two is the chapter of times — the Moon in each of the twelve signs in turn — and one of them is the operation the blog has been quoting at second hand. In this translation the Capricorn working runs: a house covered for seven days with fresh boughs and suffumigated daily; seven circuits of the house; a sacrifice; and then, on re-entering, "you will find a man sitting, to whom you must say three times" a conjuration, and "Speak with him then, and ask him for whatever you wish, and it will be effectually accomplished."[^b4c2] The chapter then closes the operation with its *foundation* rather than its rules — and this is the sentence the other translation renders as a bar and this one as a condition of character:

> "The foundation of this working, however, and others of the same kind, is that the one who performs them be of good deeds and a follower of natural effects and far from all evil deeds; also let him be clean and clear from all voluptuousness and as secret as he possibly can be."[^found]

Now set the **Aquarius** section — the one the blog has been quoting at second hand — in the two renderings side by side. Both **bar**. The earlier rendering reads: "The simple test with which to guard yourself is that only the **strong of soul and noble of substance** should venture on these works." This one reads: "for no one should dare to meddle in this work unless they are of **good memory and good quality**." The bar is the same and the *grounds* are not: one admits by a state of the soul, the other by a working competence. That is the cleanest single demonstration available of how much of the *Picatrix* as the blog knows it is a translation’s — not whether a line bars, but **on what it is made to bar**.

**And the operations run all the way down.** Book Four, chapter two invokes the seven planets' angels by name — "You, Anzil, who are the angel of Saturn!" — and then the operator works "with a calcitarat for what you will"; Book Four, chapter nine offers images "found in a book that was discovered in the church of Coredib", of a different order entirely.[^b4c9]

**The scale is the report.** Hundreds of pages of this: elections, substances, images, prayers, angels, confections, poisons and their remedies, an aphorism list drawn from a book of aphorisms "called *The Secret of Secrets*", and, recurring through it, a rule the book states as a foundation — "You should make many suffumigations, have perfect faith, fast often, utter many prayers, choose a place appropriate to the work, and observe the aspects of the planets. These are the foundations of magical workings." Beside it, the aphorism on what the spirits do: "Spirits have the power to strengthen the effects of a planet and ameliorate its qualities, or to the contrary, to lessen and worsen its effects and scatter its powers."[^b4c4] The operations always claim a specific, timed, material channel from a claim about the sky to a result on earth. The equipment's business is the timing.

## 5. The degree, and the office the text never fills

This is the post's structural question. [The owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) argued that a frame which names an endpoint and makes the connection operable leaves **a seat**, and that the seat will be taken. The test here is whether this book names anyone to stand at it.

**The first chapter is about the operator's position, and it is not a claim of rank.** The degree in question is knowledge, and the climb is study. The ladder is an order of being, not a hierarchy of persons, and the operator's place on it is low: humanity is "nobler than any other thing existing under the sphere of the Moon", and the human intellect is "clearly the lowest that can call upon the cooperation of others".

**And the qualification is a curriculum.** The translators note it plainly — "The author of *Picatrix* conveniently lists the qualifications for a sapiens in Book IV, chapter 5" — and the chapter's own words are: "there are ten arts you must know, of which five are considered necessary to the student of law, and five to the student of philosophy". The list runs agriculture, seafaring and government; the military arts; grammar, languages, law and writing; "the four traditional branches of mathematics — arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music"; logic; medicine; and metaphysics. Book Two, chapter one puts the same requirement as a wall: no one "can realize and understand the virtues and powers of the heavens in this little world unless they have studied the natural and mathematical quadrivium".

**And a second condition, which is not a curriculum at all.** Book Three, chapter six:

> "the wise concealed according to their degree, so that no one except a philosopher would be able to uncover it. They taught all knowledge and the subtleties of philosophy to their disciples before revealing to them the work of the spirits of Perfect Nature."[^b3c6]

So far there is transmission, and there are disciples. Then the text defines the teacher, and does something the blog did not expect. Asked what Perfect Nature is, Hermes answers: "Perfect Nature is the spirit of the philosopher or sage linked to the planet that governs him. This is that which opens the closed places of knowledge." And its mode of teaching is a relation between two states of one person: "Perfect Nature acts in the sage or philosopher as a teacher toward a student, teaching the latter first in simple and easy matters, and then proceeding step by step to greater and more difficult ones." The last qualification decides: "it is impossible for anybody to attain this science except those who are naturally inclined to it, both by their own virtue and by the disposition of the planet ruling in their nativity."

**So: does the text state a qualification?** Yes — and the hypothesis that it states none is not fair to it. It states three, and they are heavy: a curriculum, a transmission, and an election by nativity. What it does not do, and this is the finding, is **install a person at the route**. There is no order, no lineage, no office of initiation, no named holder whose permission is the way through. The Prologue's gate — "no one would be able to obtain it unless this science was first revealed to them" — is a condition on the reader, not a chain of custody. And where the book makes the route personal it makes it *interior*: the teacher is the operator's own spirit, and the warrant is his nativity.

**The secrecy is addressed to the operator and administered by no one.** "You, then, ought to be the guardian of the aforesaid workings, and never reveal them to the unworthy." And Book Three, chapter twelve gives the sharpest line in the book about who is at risk:

> "[I pray] that you will not reveal any of your secrets to the ignorant, because it is thus that they become the murderers of holy men and prophets."[^b3c12]

The *Picatrix* therefore has a closed frame and no gatekeeper. Its closures are horizontal — not to the ignorant, not to the unworthy — and its key is a competence anyone can acquire and a nativity everyone has or has not. That is the structure the correction in §8 states.

## 6. The warning, and its kind

Three strata, and they are not the same kind of thing. The series has reported this vocabulary before: *Holy Daimon* with none, *Holy Heretics* with a dose, Quareia with a clinical triage, Fromm with a survey, the Black Abbot's manuscripts with operational cautions. This book's results — reported as search results scoped to the text of this edition — are different again, and the difference is the finding.

**First: the publisher's and translators' Warning and Disclaimer, and its kind is legal.**

> "The material in this book includes descriptions of processes and activities that may be hazardous, illegal, or both. Readers who choose to pursue any of these activities do so entirely at their own risk, and are urged to use discretion, to be aware of the potential risks involved in these processes and activities, and to consult with appropriate licensed professionals before making any attempt to perform any such processes or activities. The translators and publisher of this book shall have no liability nor responsibility with respect to any loss or damage caused or alleged to be caused by reading or following any of the instructions in this book."[^wd]

Two things in it are worth naming. It is addressed to a **reader in a jurisdiction** — "illegal" is a legal category and not a magical one — and it names a **professional route**, "consult with appropriate licensed professionals", which is the closest thing to a referral the book contains and sits in a liability notice rather than in the text. It is therefore **not a statement about the work at all**: it tells a reader what the publisher will not answer for, which is a different question from what the practice does to a person.

**Second: the translators' own warning in the introduction, and its kind is designed obstacle plus poison notice.**

> "the book before you is deliberately designed to hide its innermost secrets from anyone who is not willing to put in the work necessary to unravel them. This is not a user-friendly book ... he was offering a warning to the innocent and incautious. Given the common modern carelessness toward magic, this warning needs to be repeated in strong terms."[^user]

Then: "The teachings in this book are designed to protect themselves from casual use, and there are deliberate traps in certain places that have been set for those who are not willing to take the time to think things through". Then a physical hazard: "Some of the recipes in this book produce lethal poisons. Not all of them are listed as such; in fact, some of them are described as healthful cures". Then the summary judgement: "Glance through it passively, and it will teach you little and deceive you whenever it can". Set beside the other sources in this series, this is the first warning that describes the hazard as a **feature of the text's design** — traps set for the careless — and the first that treats difficulty as a protective device rather than an obstacle to be cleared. It is also the only one whose remedy is stated as study: the poison notice's answer is to "do the necessary research yourself and be sure that you know the health and safety issues surrounding every ingredient". It is a gate, and the gate is the reader's competence.

**Third: the cautions inside the text itself, and every one of them is operative.** Three kinds, and no fourth. **Technical**: "Beware of his detriment and unfortunate aspects ... and do not let him be cadent"; and, on writing the names, "Beware that you do not make a mistake in these names, neither in their form nor in their figure, that you not fall into error". **Toxicological**: "Be careful that you do not touch it with your hands, nor get your clothing near it, and keep your face turned away from its odor, for it is a deadly poison in all its qualities". And where the hazard is a spirit rather than a substance the remedy is still a substance: the text records that "spirits sometimes attack the workmen who perform the foregoing, destroying their natures", and supplies a compound to bring those natures back, with the assurance that keeping it to hand means "you will be able to work safely and without danger." The book answers every hazard with a recipe. **Moral and preparatory**: "From unlawful foods and pleasures you should stay as far as you possibly can ... because desire and love attract spirits". And the strongest caution in the book is a death:

> "the greatest peril to body and spirit results, and when those who perform these workings do not know how to do them, there occur the greatest and most terrible dangers, which are frightful even to describe. This, I say to you, is to be taught and reproved: for no one should dare to meddle in this work unless they are of good memory and good quality."[^peril]

The paragraph before it is the caution's evidence, and it is an anecdote with a corpse in it: a man who performed the Moon operation while "living in a manner contradictory to this operation" was visited by "a man who had something in his hand, which he put in the mouth of the would-be magician; and at once his mouth was sealed up ... at the end of which he perished utterly."

**The count, and the negative finding.** "safeguard" **0**; "therapist", "psychiatry", "psychiatric", "clinical", "mental health", "breakdown", "self-harm", "seek help", "professional help" — **0 each**. "Caution" occurs once, as a running head. "Physician" occurs 13 times, every one inside the book's own world — including an image "to make a physician successful" — and "poison" 32 times, every one an operation or its remedy. **The vocabulary of danger in this book is entirely internal to the equipment.** Every peril named is a peril of doing the work wrong, and every answer to one is another piece of the work.[^abs]

**And the two kinds of warning do not touch.** The publisher's disclaimer tells a reader what the publisher will not be liable for; the text tells an operator what happens when the rules are not added to the working. Neither answers the other's question — the first is about a court, the second about a spirit — and neither is a safety rule in the sense this blog has been tracking. That is not a failure of either. It is the frame's shape: a book that takes a cosmos as given has nowhere to stand while telling you the cosmos might be wrong about you.

## 7. The frame inside the frame

The translators are not a neutral channel, and the edition says so itself: its own cover matter calls them "the noted scholars, magicians and astrologers". [Black Abbot, White Magic](/black-abbot-white-magic/) drew this reflection one register down — a modern practitioner's frame around a Renaissance one. Here there are two frames between the reader and the text, and a press and a course sitting in the middle of them. Describe the structure; diagnose no one.

**The apparatus is an operator's, not a philologist's.** The introduction is also, in part, a syllabus and an advertisement: "Modern astrology is emphatically not an adequate preparation ... close study of a standard text such as William Lilly's *Christian Astrology* ... will provide the necessary background. The distance learning courses offered by Renaissance Astrology ... provide another and, for many people, a more congenial way to gain the same working knowledge of the ancient science of the stars." The bibliography carries the same press as publisher and as author: "Warnock, Christopher, *Secrets of Planetary Magic* (Iowa City, Renaissance Astrology Press, 2009)". One of the two translators has, in the same volume, a school, a press and a shelf.

**And the introduction argues.** One thesis is about where magical power lives: "the sources of magical power are in the macrocosm rather than the microcosm; **power is native to the universe, not to the mage**" — with the consequence that "The magician is the one who knows how to catch these currents at the moments of their greatest power, store their energies in material objects appropriate to them, and direct those energies to carefully chosen ends." Another is a dating claim with a polemical edge: will-based magic "appears nowhere in *Picatrix*", because "the concept in question was introduced to the magical traditions by Eliphas Levi in 1854". The annotations do the same work at smaller scale: a sentence in Book Three, chapter twelve draws a note that is a position rather than a gloss — "This sentence all by itself does much to resove the ongoing debate about the descent of modern science from Renaissance magical traditions" — and the same voice corrects the text where the text is wrong: "Note that *Picatrix* here is no longer the name of the compiler of the book, but that of one of his sources."

**And one of the two is the author of this blog's previous reading.** [The blood of the earth](/the-blood-of-the-earth/) — the sixteenth post in this series — is by the same hand as the introduction and annotations here. The blog has been reading a modern essay and a medieval manual by one author, in the same weeks, from the same shelf. That is not a coincidence to be resolved; it is the frame inside the frame, occurring in two texts side by side. And the annotations are **good**: careful, explicit about the Latin, and willing to name what is not known — "neither of us is sufficiently fluent in classical Arabic to manage an accurate translation", so the base text is Latin, and "It has not been possible to interpret all of these satisfactorily, those that resisted understanding have been left untranslated, in the hope that readers with a background in medieval magical literature may be able to help us identify them in a future edition." A translation that stops at the edge of what it can say, and prints the edge, is doing something the frame's enthusiasts rarely do.

## 8. The correction the blog owes

**The blog's model of the seat has a claimant built into it, and the *Picatrix* is the same frame with the claimant missing.**

[The owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) reached its structure from the case studies: a cosmos with a legible correspondence and a point where the worlds meet is **operable**, and an operable route can be held, so the frame leaves a seat and "the seat will be occupied". Every instance in that post has a person in it — the guru who alone can read your state, the *doutrinador*, the certified master, the container with its officers. The seat was described as a **place**, and what filled it was an **office-holder**.

The *Picatrix* is that frame entire, and there is no office-holder anywhere in it. Its cosmos has a correspondence, its correspondence is a table, its table is a curriculum, and its curriculum is open to anyone with the nativity and the patience. Its teacher is defined as the operator's own spirit. Its closures are addressed to the operator and administered by no one. It is the purest case in this series of the operable cosmology, and the thing the blog's model predicts, a claimant at the route, is simply not there.

So the correction is not that the *Picatrix* refutes the seat. It is that **the blog has been treating a person standing in the seat as the seat itself** — and a frame can be entirely operable and entirely unstaffed. **The desk is the seat.** The position is filled by whoever sits down with the tables, it is empty the rest of the time, and it is filled by no one else, ever, because the qualification is a body of knowledge and a nativity rather than an appointment. Which is why the book never argues: a frame that is all equipment has nothing to defend and nobody to defend it. And which is why the *Picatrix*, of all the sources this series has read, is the one that reads most like a technology.

**The consequence for the blog's test is a fourth clause, and it is the uncomfortable one.** The test [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) arrived at was: *Does the frame name the endpoint? Is it checkable by someone who does not benefit? And does it leave a seat?* The *Picatrix* names God at the top of the ladder and nobody at the route; its checkable half is astronomy, which is public, while the half that carries the claim is checkable by no one; and it leaves a seat that needs no holder. So the fourth question is:

> **Does the seat need a holder at all?**

Because the third question was doing more work than the series noticed. "Does it leave a seat?" finds the seat; "does the seat need a holder?" finds whether there is **anybody to ask**. A frame with a named mediator can be asked, and that is what makes [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/)'s distinction usable at all — **open against closed mediation**. A frame with no mediator anywhere cannot be asked: its accountability is distributed into a technique, and a technique cannot be held responsible, only performed well or badly. The *Picatrix* is therefore not the *safer* frame the absence of a claimant might predict. It is the frame the blog's safeguard **cannot reach**.

Two smaller corrections, stated once each.

> **The blog's one direct *Picatrix* quotation has been a second translator's rendering reproduced inside another author's book, and the operation it belongs to reads differently in the book itself. The blog now owes the citation to the *Picatrix*, and the note that the wording it has been using is one translation's.**

> **The western column's second addition, "a curriculum is a safeguard", needs its scope tightened a third time. Here is a curriculum — ten sciences, the quadrivium, an order — and it is not a safeguard, because of what it grades. A curriculum that grades a person's state (Quareia's staged Void, the Golden Dawn's gate) is a protection; a curriculum that grades a competence (astrology, geometry, music) is a gate on knowledge, and a gate is not a safeguard. The row stands for the lineages that grade the person. It must not be generalised to curricula as such.**

## 9. The counterweight, in the body

**The text is a translation of a text with a contested history, and the translators say so.** This translation is not made from the Arabic: the base is the Latin, and the Latin from a Spanish version made at Alfonso's court, resting on David Pingree's critical edition with a seventeenth-century manuscript consulted beside it. The name's meaning is unknown; the Latin makes it worse by claiming the book is named after its author, while the text elsewhere uses "Picatrix" as the name of one of that author's sources. The attribution to al-Majriti is, in the translators' phrase, "in the fashion of the time"; the ninth-century date is an estimate, and the Prologue's date of 1256 belongs to the *translation*; and the chapter list disagrees with the chapter headings about whether chapter one concerns your "place" or your "degree". **The upshot is not that the book is unreliable. It is that the blog cannot quote "the Picatrix" as a single voice.** There is a ninth-century Arabic *Ghayat al-Hakim*, a thirteenth-century Latin *Picatrix*, a 1962 German rendering, a 1986 critical edition of the Latin, two modern English translations — and the blog's earlier quotation came from the other one.

**The magical claims are asserted, and the text forbids the test that would settle them.** This is the gap, and the book states it itself, in Book One, chapter four: "this work is not done in order to test or prove whether it be true or not; rather, they should have faith in their mind that it be true." Everything follows from that. The evidence the book offers is of four kinds and none of them can fail: first-person report ("I then made a figure of the same sort at the prescribed time, with which I sealed and suffumigated seals like the ones he described, and with them worked wonders at which everyone marveled"); second-hand report ("which I learned from a certain sage who labored in this science"); attributed authority (Aristotle, Hermes, "the wise of India"); and the reader's own eventual experience, which is exactly what the rule protects from disconfirmation. **There is no case in the book that could have come out otherwise.** That is not a defect relative to the genre — it *is* the genre — but it is the gap, and the blog must state it wherever it uses the material.

**And the book was written for a world that already had the cosmology.** The translators' own account of the obstacles is the cleanest statement of it: "three significant obstacles must be confronted by anyone who hopes to learn the magic of *Picatrix* and put it to work in today's world" — the distance between the medieval magical philosophy and the modern one, the distance between the medieval social world and the industrial one, and a difficulty the text builds in deliberately. On the second: "you may be sure that *Picatrix* offends today's sensibilities on that issue". On the third, practically: some ingredients are "illegal for private citizens to possess in the United States", and "how many people can afford, say, to buy several ounces of pure gold for a talisman of the Sun?"

**And the checkable part of the equipment is the part that is not the magic.** The astronomical half of the *Picatrix* is public, dated and reproducible: an ephemeris can be checked by anyone, and the operations' timings are stated to the degree. The half that carries the claim — that this substance, at that moment, produces that effect — is not checkable at all, and the book's own rule is what stops it from being tested. So the frame is not opaque through and through. It is transparent in exactly the register where nothing is claimed. [Two esoteric tarots](/two-esoteric-tarots/) had the same shape: a public, fixed, checkable table, and a private claim riding on top of it.

**What this post does not do, and will not.** Nothing here adjudicates whether the images work; nothing here says the correspondence is real, and nothing says it is not; nothing here assesses the translators, the author, or anyone who works this material; and nothing here validates or disqualifies any practice. The book's cosmology is a specimen, described at its own scale.

---

## 10. What a reading is for

**The source is the operable cosmology in its purest form, and the blog now has it.** Not a description of the frame and not an argument for it — the frame itself, at book length: the chapter list that names the operator's degree, the planets' tables, the ten sciences, the suffumigations, the elections, the angels, the aphorisms from a "Secret of Secrets", the remedies for the poisons. Where [Geosophia](/geosophia/) printed the operations *underneath* the Western material, this book is the material the Western column's tables came from — arguing nothing, and assuming the reader is already inside. That is what "the frame as infrastructure" looks like when it is not a metaphor.

**The correction is about where the blog has been looking for the seat.** It looked for a person at the endpoint, because every case study had one. The *Picatrix* is the operable frame with the person removed: a curriculum, an election by nativity, a teacher defined as the operator's own spirit, and closures addressed to the operator rather than administered by an office. **The desk is the seat** — and the blog's test needed a fourth question to see it, because a seat nobody holds is still a seat, and it is one you cannot ask anything of.

**And the gap is that a manual cannot argue, and this one does not try.** Its evidence is what practitioners say they did; its rule forbids the trial that would settle it; its reader was never the unconvinced. It arrives in English through a chain — Arabic, Castilian, Latin, a German rendering of the Arabic, a critical edition of the Latin, and two modern translations which disagree about whether the *Picatrix*'s conditions read as a bar or as a discipline.

The *Picatrix* turns out to be a manual: an inventory of a world in which knowledge is a ladder, a table is a route, a time is a mechanism, and the operator's qualification is to have studied and to have been born. The blog quoted that world before it visited it. What the visit adds is the one thing a quotation cannot carry — the flatness of the register. There is no argument in the book, and therefore no boundary in it either. That is not a fault. It is what equipment is.

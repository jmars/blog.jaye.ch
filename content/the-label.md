# The Label

### "Cult" is a category that does harm — including to the people it is meant to protect. Why this series names mechanisms instead.

*Seventh in the series. [The first](/meditation-harm/) is the mechanism;
[the third](/manufacturing-the-collapse/) describes the groups that engineer it. This one is
about the **word** — and about a debt the third post owes, because the sources it relies on
were used to justify a great deal of harm.*

*Status.* This post is mostly documented history and a named scholarly critique; the argument
comes at the end. It names one group — MOVE — because its case is the clearest on record and
because the harm done to it is the point. It diagnoses no group, and it is not a checklist.

---

## 1. The question the series has been dodging

Six posts have described a structure: a collapse, engineered, held by someone standing on the
far side. The third post listed its mechanisms — exclusivity, an owned frame, an accelerated
pace — and called the picture "coercive control."

But there is a word for all of this, and it is **cult**. The series has been conspicuously not
using it. That was deliberate, and this post is the reason, said out loud:

> **What happens when a description of harm becomes a verdict about a group?**

Because it does something. The label is not a neutral summary of the mechanisms. It is a move
in a game with a long, documented, and ugly history — one in which the people applying it have
repeatedly done harm of their own, and in which some of the series' own sources are
implicated.

---

## 2. The label tells you about the labeller

The sharpest formulation is Richard Kent Evans', in *MOVE: An American Religion*:

> **"There is, to be clear, no such thing as a cult."** The category "cult" "does not reflect
> an empirical reality. There are no charges that can be leveled at 'cults' that cannot also be
> leveled at things we call 'religions.'"[^evans]

And he names what it is instead: a **"nomenclature of containment"** — following Robert Orsi —
"an implicit argument about what the observer imagines 'true religion' to be."

Judith Weisenfeld puts the same point from the other side: **"The label 'cult' tells us less
about a group's theology or members' self-understandings than about the commitments of those
who use the label."**[^weisenfeld]

This is not academic nicety. It is a **testable claim about the word's behaviour.** Check it
against any group you know: the things that brand something a cult — closedness, a
charismatic founder, demands on members' time and money, strong in-group norms, a claim to
special truth — are all present, to some degree, in ordinary churches, firms, families,
armies, universities, and political parties. The word is not sorting by *what the group does*.
It is sorting by *how much the group resembles the labeller's idea of legitimate religion* —
which, as Evans says plainly, tends to mean "religion done right looks like moderate white
Christianity."

---

## 3. And it falls unequally

That has a consequence that is not theoretical.

Evans documents that critics **attempted to discredit MOVE by accusing founder John Africa and
his community of being abusive, disingenuous, irrational** — that is, the abuse accusation
functioned as a weapon in the lead-up to state violence. And the violence is among the
worst on record: in May 1985 Philadelphia police **dropped a bomb** on the home of the
Black-liberation community, let the fire burn, and killed **eleven people including five
children**, destroying 61 houses and displacing 250 people.[^move]

Weisenfeld's point about the pattern is the general form of it: **Black-led religious
innovations, women-led innovations, and especially Black women-led innovations** are
disproportionately criticised as cults — and members of those groups "are often seen as less
rational, weaker, and thus more susceptible to religious, as well as economic and sexual,
manipulation." The label arrives pre-loaded with an account of who is credulous.

So the word does not merely fail to describe. **It has been an instrument** — of
surveillance, of civil-rights violation, of discrediting. Whatever your intentions, picking it
up means picking up that history.

---

## 4. The debt: the series' own sources were used to justify kidnapping

Here is the part that this series has to say about itself.

There was a movement in the 1970s and 80s — the **anti-cult movement (ACM)** — built on
"rescuing" young adults from groups it called cults. Its central doctrine was
**"brainwashing."** Its practice was **deprogramming**: the forcible removal of adults from
their communities, described in the literature as **extra-legal abduction**, defended
initially as "a family problem involving unruly juveniles," and eventually reined in by
civil-liberties and NRM litigation — culminating in the collapse of the Cult Awareness
Network in 1996.

Now the parts that are uncomfortable:

- **The word "brainwashing" was not science.** It was popularised in the 1950s by Edward
  Hunter, an **undercover CIA propaganda and psychological-warfare specialist**, as part of a
  documented **disinformation campaign**; and the history is blunt: "There is no reliable
  scientific evidence to convincingly prove the brainwashing theory's legitimacy. There is,
  however, a great deal of research against it."
- **The ACM's stated model of the "victim" used this series' vocabulary.** Deprogramming was
  justified on the premise that the person had been **"stripped of their freedom of thought"**
  and rendered **"abnormally depersonalised, dissociated and regressed to psychological
  infancy."** Depersonalized. Dissociated. That is the language of post 1 — used as grounds for
  abducting adults.
- **And the ACM drew on the exact sources the third post cites.** Its conceptual apparatus
  came from **Robert Lifton's** criteria for totalistic communities and from **Margaret
  Singer**, who testified in support of the brainwashing defence at Patty Hearst's trial.[^acm]

So the third post's authorities — Lifton, Singer, and the thought-reform tradition — are also
the intellectual supply of a movement that kidnapped people. That does not make the third
post wrong. Lifton's *description* of milieu control and confession can be accurate while the
*use* made of it was harmful; the same lifeguard can be misused as a weapon. But a series that
leans on these sources owes the reader this paragraph, and here it is.

---

## 5. The other error

The obvious reply is: *so never say it.* Avoid the label entirely, stick to description, and
the harm goes away.

That does not work either, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of harm. If "cult" is
unsayable, then **abuse becomes unchallengeable** — because the accusation "you are calling us
a cult" is available to any group as a shield. The No True Scotsman runs in both directions:
"No real religion abuses its members" protects abusers, and "cult is just religion you don't
like" protects them too. The academic literature is clear that **abuse in high-demand groups
is real**, that **coercive control is a studied structure**, and that people are genuinely
harmed — sometimes severely, sometimes permanently.[^control]

There are two failure modes, and they are symmetric:

| error | what it protects |
|---|---|
| **Label freely** | the labeller's comfort; the state's power; the reputation of the "respectable" |
| **Never label** | the abuser, who gains a shield from the vocabulary of tolerance |

Both of them leave the person inside the situation with no language, no warning, and no way
out.

---

## 6. The resolution: mechanisms, not labels

So the answer is not a better word. It is to **stop sorting groups and start describing
mechanisms** — which is what the third post was already doing, and why it went out of its way
to avoid naming anyone.

Look at what it actually offers. Not membership criteria or a vibe. **Specific, checkable
mechanisms:**

- Does the group supply the frame *during* the destabilisation — and does that frame
  conveniently explain why the group is essential?
- Is the community becoming **exclusive** — do outside relationships quietly degrade?
- Is the pace being **accelerated** — food, sleep, contact all pressed, intensity recoded as
  commitment?
- Is the teacher present at onset **and** the only available response to the result?

Every one of those is **a question about behaviour**, answerable by an outsider, and none of
them requires calling anything a cult. That is the whole point. A mechanism can be described,
observed, and acted on. A label can only be agreed with or rejected — and rejecting it is
cheap, because the group's lawyers, defenders, and sincere members all get to say *"we're not
a cult."* Nobody can say *"we do not have an exclusive frame"* if they do.

**Mechanism-first preserves the ability to warn without the harms of labelling.** It is more
useful and less dangerous at the same time, which is rare, and it is why the third post reads
the way it does.

---

## 7. How to talk about a group you are worried about

The practical version, and it is short:

**Describe what you have observed, specifically. Ask. Do not diagnose.** "I've noticed you've
stopped seeing X," "your sleep has changed," "what happens if you ask a hard question
there?" — these are things a person can evaluate. "You're in a cult" is not; it recruits the
whole apparatus of the label, and it hands them a defensible denial.

**Let the person decide.** The mechanism has one structural result that governs this: the
state cannot initiate its own exit, so recovery comes from outside — but **"from outside"
means a relationship, not a verdict.** Someone who needs an exit cannot use your diagnosis.
They can use the fact that you're still there, still asking, still not conceding that the
situation is normal.

**And sometimes the ethical choice is to withhold a name you have.** There are situations where
the evidence is real, the harm is documented, and naming the group publicly would cost more
than it buys — litigation, retaliation, the exposure of people who cannot afford to be
identified, or simply the conversion of a live situation into a public position. Holding a
name back is not cowardice or complicity. It is a legitimate judgement that the description is
more useful than the verdict. This series makes that choice throughout, and it is a choice,
defended here rather than left implicit.

---

## 8. The reflexivity: this framework is not exempt

The last move is the one the third post's §7 already began, and it belongs here too, in full.

**Every mechanism this series describes is available to be turned into a label.** "Exclusive
community," "owned frame," "accelerated pace," "a teacher present at onset" — a checklist of
those is a cult-detector, and cult-detectors are exactly what the anti-cult movement ran on.
Give someone four boxes and a suspicious neighbour and you have reproduced the machine.

The safeguard is not a better checklist. It is a **discipline**, and it has to be re-applied
every time:

- **Describe, do not diagnose.** Behaviour is observable; membership is a verdict.
- **The person inside decides**, and their account outranks yours. The ACM's central error was
  overriding exactly this — deciding on people's behalf that they lacked agency.
- **Never let the description become an identity.** "This group does X" is a claim you can be
  wrong about. "These people are cultists" is one you cannot, because it has stopped being
  about anything observable.
- **Notice who benefits from the naming.** Sometimes it is the person being helped. Often it
  is the labeller, the state, the family, the platform, or the enemy.

That the vocabulary of *depersonalization and dissociation* was used to justify abducting
adults is the sharpest possible warning, because it is this series' own vocabulary. The
lesson is not to stop describing. It is that **a description is not a licence** — and the
distance between them is maintained by discipline, not by having the right words.

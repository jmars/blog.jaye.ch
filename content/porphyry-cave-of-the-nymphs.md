# Porphyry, On the Cave of the Nymphs

### Twenty-fourth of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the direct answer to the last reading: the founder's route given a location, a cave in the Odyssey read as the cosmos, two gates, and a frame whose uncheckable part is a reading of verses

*Twenty-fourth of the `readings`, and it answers the twenty-third. [Plotinus](/plotinus-collected-writings/) ended the last reading with a finding about location: his ascent has none. There is no seat, no practice, no stated axiom for the uncheckable part of the route to occupy, and the blog's instrument was corrected to ask whether the route terminates in the practitioner's own state, and whether there is anything at all that someone other than the practitioner could inspect. Porphyry is Plotinus' own pupil and the editor of his papers, and this is the first time the series has read him as a book rather than cited him. What he does with the same subject is give the route a **place**: a cave in Ithaca, in the thirteenth book of the Odyssey, with two gates turned north and south, water, honey, a loom on stone beams, and an olive at the mouth. The descent into generation is drawn as geography. So the route is located — and what is located turns out to be a reading of fourteen lines of Homer, which is the finding this post has to report carefully, because it is not quite the finding the series went looking for. The same succession, one generation apart, produces a frame with no location and a frame whose whole content is a location. **The answer to "is the route located?" is not a property of the tradition. It is a property of the individual text.***

*Status.* This post reads one short treatise at its own scale, and it is shorter than the recent readings because the book is short: Porphyry's *On the Cave of the Nymphs* is a single treatise in eighteen numbered sections, a commentary on one passage of one poem, and reading it at the length of the *Enneads* or the *Elements* would be a different kind of dishonesty. Two printings of one translation are used, and every quotation says which printing it came from. The first is ***On the Cave of the Nymphs in the Thirteenth Book of the Odyssey, From the Greek of Porphyry, Translated by Thomas Taylor*** (London: John M. Watkins, 1917) — the **1917 printing** below, cited by Porphyry's own section number in it. The second is the same translator's ***Select Works of Porphyry*** (London: Thomas Rodd, 1823), which reprints the treatise alongside the four books *On Abstinence from Animal Food* and the *Auxiliaries to the Perception of Intelligible Natures*, and adds Taylor's own Introduction and an Appendix of his own; quotations from it are marked as coming from the **1823 printing**, and Taylor's prose is marked as Taylor's. Every quotation is checked against the printing named. Straight ASCII quotation marks are used throughout; the printers' curly apostrophes and quotation marks are normalised, spaces the printing leaves before its punctuation and before a closing quotation mark are closed up, a word split across a line — with or without a hyphen — is rejoined so it reads whole, and two words the printing runs together are separated. **Both printings are damaged in places, and differently in each** — the 1917 title page is one of the damaged places — so where a quotation's printing is broken the quotation stops at the break rather than being mended, elisions are marked with a bare ellipsis, and where the 1917 printing spoils a passage the 1823 printing carries cleanly the quotation is taken from the 1823 and said to be. Nothing is spliced, and no word is inserted into a quotation. The reading of this material through the blog's frame is an **inference**, offered as one. **Nothing here claims that the cosmos is as Porphyry reads it, that souls descend through a gate, that the nymphs preside over generation, or that Homer meant what Porphyry says he meant; nothing here claims the contrary of any of these either.** Porphyry's claims are reported as his, Taylor's as his, and the later Platonists quoted in Taylor's notes as theirs. No living or dead person is diagnosed, and nothing here validates or invalidates anyone's practice. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no one.*

## 1. What kind of book this is

The book is a short treatise doing one thing: explaining a passage of Homer. It opens with its question and never leaves it.

> "What does Homer obscurely signify by the cave in Ithaca, which he describes in the following verses ?"

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^s1q] What follows is not a treatise on the soul that happens to use Homer as an illustration. It is an exegesis of fourteen lines of the *Odyssey*, book thirteen, and every sentence of it is accountable to those lines. This is also the blog's first **direct** Porphyry. He has been a figure on the edge of the series all along: the pupil who arranged and edited [Plotinus](/plotinus-collected-writings/), and the author of the *Letter to Anebo* that [Iamblichus, On the Mysteries](/iamblichus-on-the-mysteries/) answers. Here he is read as a book, and the edition matters. Taylor published the treatise in 1823 in the *Select Works*, where the title page advertises, after the translations, "AN APPENDIX, EXPLAINING THE ALLEGORY OF THE WANDERINGS OF ULYSSES. BY THE TRANSLATOR."[^tp] So the 1823 volume does two things the 1917 reissue does not: it carries an Introduction in which Taylor frames the treatise, and an Appendix in which he extends its method over the whole *Odyssey*. Taylor's Introduction is where he says what he thinks the treatise is for:

> "Porphyry, in his treatise On the Cave of the Nymphs, having informed us, that Numenius, the Pythagorean, considered the person of Ulysses, in the Odyssey, as the image of a man who passes in a regular manner over the stormy sea of generation, or a sensible life, and thus at length arrives at a region where tempest and seas are unknown, and finds a nation …"

— Thomas Taylor, Introduction to the 1823 *Select Works*, page xii.[^tintro] Two things there are Taylor's and not Porphyry's: the double gloss "the stormy sea of generation, or a sensible life", and the claim that Porphyry "informed us" of the Ulysses reading. Porphyry does give that reading — but to Numenius and his followers, not to himself. The post comes back to that.

The 1917 printing is a cheap reissue of the same translation, read first here because it prints the treatise alone at section numbers that run unbroken from one to eighteen, and because its title page advertises the treatise as "From the Greeh of Porphyry"[^title] — the condition of both printings, and the reason each quotation below says which one it came from.

## 2. The verses, and the objection the treatise starts from

The passage is the cave at the head of the harbour of Phorcys, where the Phaeacians leave the sleeping Odysseus. Taylor's version of it, as Porphyry quotes it:

> "High at the head a branching olive grows
> And crowns the pointed cliffs with shady boughs.
> A cavern pleasant, though involved in night,
> Beneath it lies, the Naiades' delight :
> Where bowls and urns of workmanship divine
> And massy beams in native marble shine ;
> On which the Nymphs amazing webs display,
> Of purple hue and exquisite array.
> The busy bees within the urns secure
> Honey delicious, and like nectar pure.
> Perpetual waters through the grotto glide,
> A lofty gate unfolds on either side ;
> That to the north is pervious to mankind :
> The sacred south t'immortals is consign'd."

— Porphyry, section 1, quoting Homer; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^verses]

Everything in the treatise hangs on those lines, and the first move is not interpretation but a refutation of the obvious reading. Porphyry denies that Homer reports a real cave, with a borrowed argument — that the poet

> "under the veil of allegory, conceals some mysterious signification"

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^veil] The argument is Cronius', and Porphyry marks it as his from the start: those who have described the island have, as Cronius says, "made no mention of such a cave being found in it",[^cronius1] and Cronius insists the story cannot be history. Porphyry's own sentence making the point states the treatise's premise:

> "And it is equally absurd to suppose, that nature herself should point out, in this place, one path for the descent of all mankind, and again another path for all the Gods."

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^absurd]

From there it builds a question list out of the physical details, and the list is what makes it a commentary rather than an essay: why the cave is of the nymphs called Naiades and not of nymphs simply; why there are bowls and jars when nothing is said to fill them, but bees deposit honey in them as in hives; why the weaving beams are stone rather than wood; why the gate of men is turned north and the gate of the gods south.[^qlist] Then a concession, and a passage of geography: Porphyry allows that something like the cave may exist, quoting Artemidorus the Ephesian on the island, and concludes, "This cave, therefore, will not be entirely an Homeric fiction." But whether the poet describes it as it is or adds something of his own, "the same inquiries remain".[^artemidorus] The treatise will not settle whether the cave is real. It will settle what it means.

## 3. The cave is the world

The first identification is the large one, and Porphyry gives it in the second section: the cave is the cosmos, consecrated to the cosmos "collectively, according to the whole of itself, or separately, according to its parts". The reasoning runs through matter, which is what a cave is made of:

> "through the cave indicating the world, which was generated from matter"

— Porphyry, section 2; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^worldmatter] Caves are spontaneous productions, connascent with the earth and hollow inside; and the world, produced from no external cause and self-adherent, is allied to matter. Matter, on this account, is "denominated a stone and a rock" for its sluggish and repercussive nature with respect to form.[^matter] Then the sentence that makes the allegory turn:

> "through the connecting power, and orderly distribution of form, from which also it is called world, it is beautiful and delightful."

— Porphyry, section 2; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^connect] Hence the cave is properly denominated a cave: lovely to him who first enters it through its participation of forms, obscure to him who surveys its foundation with an intellectual eye, its exterior parts pleasant and its interior parts dark.

That is the frame, and the kind of claim it makes should be marked. It is not that the cave is *like* the world: a cave **is** a symbol of the world, on the grounds that the two share matter, darkness and a hollow shape. The treatise then establishes that caves were dedicated in fact. In the second section the Persians perform their initiation "in a place which they denominate a cavern", and Eubulus reports that Zoroaster first consecrated a cave in the mountains of Persia "in honour of Mithra, the maker and father of all things".[^persiancave] And in the third section, in Porphyry's own summary sentence, "to the world they dedicated caves and dens; as likewise to Nymphs … on account of the water which trickles, or is diffused in caverns, over which the Naiades, as we shall shortly observe, preside" — the ellipsis standing for a cross-reference the printing carries there.[^cavesdens]

The fourth section separates two senses, and it is where the treatise is most careful. A cave can be a symbol of the intelligible world too — invisible to sense, of a firm and stable essence — and Porphyry says the ancients used both senses "by different and not the same conceptions". This cave has a twofold entrance and contains flowing water, so it is an image of the sensible essence, not the intelligible, and it is sacred to the Naiades, the nymphs of streams, rather than to the mountain nymphs. And the identification that does the work:

> "For the ancients thought that these souls are incumbent on water which is inspired by divinity, as Numenius says"

— Porphyry, section 4; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^numeniuswater] The thesis follows in one clause:

> "for souls descending into generation fly to moisture"

— Porphyry, section 4; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^moisture] The witnesses are stacked behind it: Heraclitus, that "moisture appears delightful and not deadly to souls", and again that "We live their death, and we die their life";[^heraclitus] and the Egyptians, for whom the planets sail rather than stand. Water is the substance of the descent, and the fifth section is a short physiology of it: souls, corporeal or incorporeal, while they attract body, are "drenched in moisture" and so verge to humidity, the dead being evoked by the effusion of bile and blood; Heraclitus gives the maxim in the other direction — "a dry soul is the wisest" — while Porphyry gives the mechanism: the spirit becomes moist through the desire of generation, the soul attracting a humid vapour from verging to generation.[^mistsoul] And then the identification that ties the water to the nymphs, in five words:

> "…into generation are the nymphs called naiades"

— Porphyry, section 5; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^naiades] The nymphs of the water are the souls on their way in; marriage is called being a nymph, because it is being joined to generation.

## 4. The two gates, and which is for whom

The gates are the sharpest part of the treatise and the part the blog came for. Porphyry builds to them through cult: caves were consecrated to gods before temples were built, and wherever Mithra was known "they propitiated the God in a cavern".[^mithra] Then he returns to the verse he quoted at the start:

> "Homer was not satisfied with saying that it had two gates"

— Porphyry, section 9; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^twogates] The rest of that sentence the 1917 printing breaks and the 1823 printing carries: the poet adds that one of the gates "was turned towards the north, but the other, which was more divine, to the south".[^twogatesb] And Porphyry marks a piece of authorial care: the poet calls the northern gate pervious to descent, but says nothing of the southern gate's perviousness at all, only that it "is inaccessible to men, but it is the path of the immortals".[^inaccessible]

The identification of the gates is where the cosmology becomes astronomy. Porphyry states the premise as received, and attributes it:

> "as Numenius and his familiar Cronius assert"

— Porphyry, section 10; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^numcron] That is the attribution he gives for the claim that a cavern is an image and symbol of the world; the two caps of the heaven are his own mapping on top of it. The summer tropic is in Cancer and the winter tropic in Capricorn, Cancer being nearest to us and attributed to the Moon, Capricorn invisible from here and given to Saturn.[^tropics] Then the gates are named:

> "that Cancer is the gate through which souls descend; but Capricorn that through which they ascend."

— Porphyry, section 11; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^cancer] Cancer is northern and suited to descent, Capricorn southern and suited to ascent, so the verse's two gates are the two solstitial signs and the direction of travel in the poem's geography is the direction of travel in the cosmos: **north is down, south is up**.[^cancerb]

Porphyry then does something worth marking, because it is the treatise's most exact piece of reading. Homer gives the southern gate to "the immortals", not to "the gods", and Porphyry insists on the difference:

> "but the southern gates are not the avenues of the Gods, but of souls ascending to the Gods."

— Porphyry, section 11; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^avenues] The reason he gives is a claim about us: the name "immortals" is also common to our souls, "which are per se, or essentially, immortal".[^appellation] The poem's word is honoured exactly, and the honouring changes what the gate is for: if the southern gate were the gods' own avenue, the cave would be a picture of divine traffic, whereas with the word "immortals" it is a picture of souls going home. **A reader can check that move against the verse**, and it is one of the two things in this treatise a second person can check.

The winds follow the gates, the north wind suiting souls falling into generation, the south wind dissolving life,[^winds] and in the fourteenth section the general principle is stated: every twofold entrance is a symbol of a nature that runs in two directions, so that one gate pertains "to Gods and good (daemons)" and the other "to mortals and depraved natures".[^pertains] Parmenides, Porphyry adds, "mentions these two gates" in a treatise on the nature of things, and Pherecydes Syrus "also mentions recesses and trenches, caverns, doors, and gates".[^witnesses]

## 5. The nymphs, the vessels, and the loom

The sixth section brings the allegory down to objects, and there the treatise is at once most like a diagram and most like an argument. The cave is consecrated in common to souls and to the nymphs who preside over waters, and the question is which symbol belongs to which. The stony bowls and jars are for the aquatic nymphs, the stone suiting nymphs "who preside over the water that flows from rocks", while the jars of baked earth are symbols of Bacchus.[^bowls] And to souls coming in:

> "what symbol can be more appropriate than those instruments pertaining to weaving?"

— Porphyry, section 6; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^weaving] The answer to the stone beams is anatomy: the formation of the flesh is on and about the bones, "which in the bodies of animals resemble stones", so the beams of the loom are stone because the frame of a living body is bone, and the purple web is flesh woven from blood.[^flesh] The body then gets the treatise's second large figure:

> "the body is a garment with which the soul is invested, a thing wonderful to the sight"

— Porphyry, section 6; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^garment] The figure is not Porphyry's coinage, and he says so: Proserpine is represented by Orpheus as weaving a web, and the heavens are called a veil by the ancients.[^proserpine]

The seventh and eighth sections are about honey, and they are the best example in the treatise of how far one physical detail stretches. The jars hold honeycombs and not water; honey is the nutriment of bees, and also what is offered to the dead, since the poet pours nectar and ambrosia into the nostrils of corpses to prevent putrefaction. Being both cathartic and preservative, it is "the symbol of a preserving and defending power" when the Persians offer it to the guardian of fruits.[^persians] And then the reading on which the section turns, because it is a reading of a *myth*:

> "the sweetness of honey signifies, with theologists, the same thing as the pleasure arising from generation, by which Saturn, being ensnared, was castrated."

— Porphyry, section 7; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^honey] In Orpheus, Saturn is caught by Jupiter through honey, is intoxicated, and sleeps, so the sweetness of honey means the pleasure that draws souls down. Bees are read the same way — the priestesses of Ceres were called bees, Proserpine was called honied, the moon was called a bee — and honey is at the same time a symbol of death, which is why libations of honey go to the terrestrial gods. Even the boundary is policed: all souls proceeding into generation are not simply called bees, Porphyry says, "but those who will live in it justly and who, after having performed such things as are acceptable to the Gods".[^bees]

## 6. The olive, and Ulysses as the soul

The last stretch turns to the olive at the head of the cave, where the allegory becomes advice. Porphyry starts by refusing the trivial reading:

> "But the growth of the olive in such a situation is not fortuitous, as some one may suspect, but contains the enigma of the cavern."

— Porphyry, section 15; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^olive] The olive is the plant of Minerva, and Minerva is wisdom — the 1917 printing breaks that sentence and the 1823 printing carries it:

> "hence an olive, the symbol of this wisdom, flourishes near the present cavern, which is an image of the world."

— Porphyry, section 15; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^olive2] The olive is ever-flourishing, its leaves turning white end upward in summer and downward in winter, which is why suppliants extend olive branches, exchanging "the sorrowful darkness of danger for the fair light of security and peace"; the Demiurgus "invigorates miserable and suppliant souls".[^demiurgus]

Then the sixteenth section, which is the treatise's nearest thing to an instruction, and the sentence to read twice:

> "it is requisite to sit at the foot of the olive, and consult with Minerva by what means we may most effectually destroy that hostile rout of passions which insidiously lurk in the secret recesses of the soul."

— Porphyry, section 16; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing. The 1917 printing carries the same sentence with two words broken.[^sit] That is the treatise telling someone to do something — and the someone is Ulysses, inside the allegory. Porphyry's preamble makes that clear: "Here, naked, and assuming a suppliant habit", afflicted in body, averse to the energies of sense, one sits at the foot of the olive.[^sit2] The person who sits is the figure in the poem, read as the soul.

And then the identification the series went looking for, which Porphyry attributes rather than claims:

> "it was not without reason that Numenius and his followers thought the person of Ulysses in the Odyssey represented to us a man, who passes in a regular manner over the dark and stormy sea of generation, and thus at length arrives at that region where tempests and seas are unknown, and finds a nation
> "Who ne'er knew salt, or heard the billows roar.""

— Porphyry, section 16; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^ulysses] So Odysseus is the soul that comes in through the water and goes out through it. The port of the cave is named Phorcys, which Porphyry reads as a statement about matter — "It is the port of the ancient marine Phorcys"[^phorcys] — and the seat under the olive belongs to Ulysses precisely as a suppliant, "as to one who implores divinity, and would appease his natal demon with a suppliant branch".[^suppliant] The story ends at the oar mistaken for a winnowing fan; then the eighteenth section defends the method against the charge that interpretations of this kind are "forced, and nothing more than the conjectures of ingenious men".[^forced] The defence is not a rule for reading but a claim about the poet — that it "must not be denied that he has obscurely … indicated the images of things of a more divine nature in the fiction of a fable", the ellipsis standing for the printing's page-break — and its ground is Homer's wisdom:

> "it would not have been possible to devise the whole of this hypothesis unless the figment had been transferred (to an appropriate meaning) from certain established truths."

— Porphyry, section 18; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^figment] Then the treatise stops: "But reserving the discussion of this for another treatise, we shall here finish our explanation of the present Cave of the Nymphs."[^finish]

## 7. The correction the blog owes

The last reading ended on a question, and the question was the blog's own instrument being corrected. Plotinus' frame, the twenty-third reading found, has no location for the part of it that cannot be checked: no seat, no practice, no stated axiom, nothing outside the thinker at all. The route terminates in the practitioner's own state, so the instrument gained a fourth question: does the route terminate there, and is there anything at all that someone other than the practitioner could inspect? The blog came to Porphyry expecting the answer to be yes, because he is Plotinus' pupil and because his treatise is a location — a cave, a map, two gates.

The answer is yes, and it is not the answer the blog was expecting.

**First, the route is located, and it can be pointed at by anyone.** The verses exist. A second person can read book thirteen and see that there is a cave with two gates and that one of them is north; can see that Homer gives the south gate to "immortals" and not to "gods", and that Porphyry's reading of it turns on exactly that word; can check the identification of the gates with Cancer and Capricorn against the verse's directions; and can find Cronius and Numenius named and see what Porphyry takes from each. **The uncheckable part of this frame occupies a location, and the location is a text.** That is a real answer to the fourth question, and a different kind of answer from anything else in the series.

**Second, and this is the part the blog owes honestly: what the location locates is a reading, and nothing in the treatise adjudicates it.** The instrument had been asking about the uncheckable part as though all uncheckable parts were the same shape. They are not, and one school, one generation apart, shows the difference. In **Plotinus** the uncheckable part is a **state** — the soul turned inward, unshareable in principle, which someone else cannot inspect and could not if they tried. In **Porphyry** it is a **reading** — a public object, quotable and arguable and checkable for consistency, and still undecided. Someone else can inspect it perfectly well; what they cannot do is decide it, because nothing in the treatise and nothing in Homer settles why a cave should be a cosmos rather than a sanctuary, or why water should mean generation rather than purification.

So the correction is not the one the blog expected. **The instrument had been treating "location" as a property of a frame, and Plotinus' answer as a property of the school. Porphyry shows both are properties of the individual text** — the pupil of the man whose route terminates in a private state writes a route that terminates in a public poem.

**Third, the treatise does not station a practitioner, and its form says so.** Its only imperative sits inside the allegory: the sentence that puts someone under the olive to consult Minerva, whose subject is the figure in the poem, read as the soul. There is no initiation prescribed, no object to be used correctly, no office. The comparison with the neighbours is exact, because the series has just read them. Iamblichus' frame puts the efficacy in an act performed on symbols, so its uncheckable part is a practice, with the gods' knowledge as its operative content. [Proclus'](/proclus-elements-of-theology/) frame is two hundred and eleven propositions, so its uncheckable part is the first principle, offered at the head of a proof. Porphyry's treatise has neither: no act whose performance could be checked, no proposition to inspect. **Its whole external content is fourteen lines of Homer and a claim about what they mean**, and Porphyry grounds that claim in Homer's wisdom rather than in a criterion.

That is why the fourth question needs one more cut: **a reading is public and arguable, and being public and arguable is not the same as being decidable.** The instrument can now ask whether a frame's uncheckable part is a state or a reading, because it has one of each, in one line of succession, from the hand that arranged the *Enneads*.

## 8. The counterweight: three gaps, in the body

**First, the allegorical warrant, which is the structural cost, and the treatise states it itself.** Porphyry's method is total: every physical detail in the passage gets a meaning, and the meanings are supplied from outside the poem. His defence answers the charge of forcing with a premise about Homer rather than a rule — the figment could not have been devised at all "unless the figment had been transferred (to an appropriate meaning) from certain established truths". That is not a criterion of correct reading but a claim about the poet's competence, and **the cost is that the treatise's interpretations cannot be wrong in a way a reader could detect, because there is no test outside the reading itself.** A reader is free to say that Homer simply described a cave, and nothing in the treatise can defeat that, because its defence of allegory is itself allegorical.  The objection is available; he reports it, and answers it with the standing of Homer. That is a real and unsolved structural cost, and the treatise is not naive about it.

**Second, the method is named, and this is the checkability the treatise actually offers.** Its argument is dense with attributions, and they can be sorted. From **Cronius**, the claim that the cave is not historical; from **Numenius**, the identification of souls with water — "as Numenius says" — and, with Cronius, the assertion that a cavern is an image and symbol of the world; from **Numenius and his followers**, the Ulysses reading, said in the opening of the sentence, which is why Taylor's Introduction's "informed us" is Taylor's summary and not Porphyry's claim. From **Artemidorus the Ephesian** the geography of the island; from **Eubulus** the report about Zoroaster and Mithra; from **Parmenides** and **Pherecydes Syrus** the two gates and another mention of doors and gates; from **Heraclitus** the moisture maxims; from **Empedocles** the secret cavern; from **Plato** the cave of the *Republic* and the tubs of the *Gorgias*; from **Pythagoras** the people of dreams in the galaxy; from **Orpheus** the honey and the weaving of Proserpine; from **Sophocles** the bees. What is Porphyry's own is the mapping of the two tropic signs onto the two gates, the argument from bone to stone beam, and the insistence that the verse's word "immortals" rather than "gods" gives the southern gate its passengers. **A reader can see whose reading each one is, and can check the attribution** — which does not make the readings decidable but makes them attributable, and is why this post can report the allegory as the treatise gives it rather than as Porphyry alone gives it.

**Third, the translation, which is a Romantic rendering of a late-antique Greek text and should be said to be.** Both printings read here are Thomas Taylor's, made in 1823 and reissued in 1917; this reading had no critical English edition to work from and no second translator to set against him. Two consequences matter. **Taylor's own frame is printed with the text, and he annotates and criticises the author.** He introduces the treatise in 1823, and the same volume carries an Appendix extending the method over the whole *Odyssey*, its sources named as plainly as Porphyry names his: "from the hints afforded by Porphyry … to unfold the latent meaning of the wanderings of Ulysses, as narrated by Homer."[^appendix] His note on the castration of Saturn is a direct charge:

> "Porphyry, though he excelled in philosophical, was deficient in theological knowledge; of which what he now says of the castrations of Saturn and Heaven is a remarkable instance."

— Thomas Taylor, note to Porphyry, section 7; 1917 printing.[^tnote10] So the apparatus is a second reading with its own standing, and it brings in Hermias and Proclus — a later Platonic reading of Porphyry's reading, in which the nymphs are "Goddesses who preside over regeneration, and are ministrant to Bacchus, the offspring of Semele".[^hermias] **And the two printings are damaged, and differently, so a reader has no clean copy of the treatise to fall back on.** Where one is broken the quotations here come from the other and say so, which is a way of working, not a critical text. Nothing in this post rests on a word that only one of the two printings carries.

## 9. What this reading claims

**One.** Porphyry's *On the Cave of the Nymphs* is a commentary on fourteen lines of Odyssey 13, in eighteen numbered sections, and a whole book at that size: it opens with its question — what Homer obscurely signifies by the cave in Ithaca — and closes by announcing that it has finished.

**Two.** The method is total allegory, built on a premise stated at the outset: the story cannot be history, because nature does not put one path in one cave for all humanity and another for all the gods. Every physical detail then receives a meaning drawn from outside the poem — stone is matter, water the descent of souls, bone the stone beam of a loom, purple flesh, honey the pleasure that draws souls down.

**Three.** The two gates are the sharpest part of it. The northern gate is the descent of souls and the southern the path of the immortals, and the two are identified with the tropic signs, Cancer and Capricorn. The treatise's most exact reading — that Homer writes "immortals" and not "gods", so that the southern gate is the way home for souls rather than an avenue of the gods — is checkable against the verse.

**Four.** The correction. The last reading found a frame with no location for its uncheckable part, and asked whether the route always terminates in the practitioner's own state. Porphyry, Plotinus' own pupil, gives the route a place. So **"is the route located?" is not a property of the tradition but of the individual text.** But what the location locates is a reading, and a reading is public and arguable without being decidable. The instrument therefore gains a fifth cut: **ask whether the uncheckable part of a frame is a state or a reading.**

**Five.** The counterweights, in the body. **The allegorical warrant is the structural cost**: the treatise answers the charge of forcing with a claim about Homer's wisdom rather than a criterion of reading, so no interpretation in it can be shown wrong by anything outside the interpretation. **The method is named, and that is the real checkability**: Cronius, Numenius, Artemidorus, Eubulus, Parmenides, Pherecydes, Heraclitus, Plato, Orpheus and the rest — a reader can see whose reading each one is, which makes them attributable without making them decidable. **And the translation is a Romantic rendering with no critical English edition behind it**, printed with its translator's own Introduction, Appendix and notes — the notes criticising the author whose text he is translating.

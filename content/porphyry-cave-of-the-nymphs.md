# Porphyry, On the Cave of the Nymphs

### Twenty-fourth of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the direct answer to the last reading: the founder's route given a location, a cave in the Odyssey read as the cosmos, two gates, and a frame whose uncheckable part is a reading of verses

*Twenty-fourth of the `readings`, and it answers the twenty-third. [Plotinus](/plotinus-collected-writings/) ended the last reading with a finding about location: his ascent has none. There is no seat, no practice, no stated axiom for the uncheckable part of the route to occupy, and the blog's instrument was corrected to ask whether the route terminates in the practitioner's own state, and whether there is anything at all that someone other than the practitioner could inspect. Porphyry is Plotinus' own pupil and the editor of his papers, and this is the first time the series has read him as a book rather than cited him. What he does with the same subject is give the route a **place**. There is a cave in Ithaca, described in the thirteenth book of the Odyssey; there are two gates, one turned north for the descent of souls and one turned south for the ascent of the immortals; there is water, honey, a loom on stone beams, and an olive at the mouth. The descent into generation is drawn as geography. So the route is located — and what is located turns out to be a reading of fourteen lines of Homer, which is the finding this post has to report carefully, because it is not quite the finding the series went looking for. The same school, one generation apart, produces a frame with no location and a frame whose whole content is a location. **The answer to "is the route located?" is not a property of the tradition. It is a property of the individual text.***

*Status.* This post reads one short treatise at its own scale, and it is shorter than the recent readings because the book is short: Porphyry's *On the Cave of the Nymphs* is a single treatise in eighteen numbered sections, a commentary on one passage of one poem, and reading it at the length of the *Enneads* or the *Elements* would be a different kind of dishonesty. Two printings of one translation are used, and every quotation says which printing it came from. The first is ***On the Cave of the Nymphs in the Thirteenth Book of the Odyssey, From the Greek of Porphyry, Translated by Thomas Taylor*** (London: John M. Watkins, 1917) — called the **1917 printing** below, and cited by Porphyry's own section number in it. The second is the same translator's ***Select Works of Porphyry*** (London: Thomas Rodd, 1823), which reprints the treatise alongside the four books *On Abstinence from Animal Food* and the *Auxiliaries to the Perception of Intelligible Natures*, and adds Taylor's own Introduction and an Appendix of his own; quotations from it are marked as coming from the **1823 printing**, and quotations of Taylor's prose are marked as Taylor's. Every quotation is checked against the printing named. Straight ASCII quotation marks are used throughout; the printers' curly apostrophes and quotation marks are normalised, spaces the printing leaves before its punctuation and before a closing quotation mark are closed up, a word split across a line — with or without a hyphen — is rejoined so it reads whole, and two words the printing runs together are separated. **Both printings are damaged in places, and differently in each** — the 1917 title page is one of the damaged places — so where a quotation's printing is broken the quotation stops at the break rather than being mended, elisions are marked with a bare ellipsis, and where the 1917 printing spoils a passage the 1823 printing carries cleanly the quotation is taken from the 1823 and said to be. Nothing is spliced, and no word is inserted into a quotation. The reading of this material through the blog's frame is an **inference**, offered as one. **Nothing here claims that the cosmos is as Porphyry reads it, that souls descend through a gate, that the nymphs preside over generation, or that Homer meant what Porphyry says he meant; nothing here claims the contrary of any of these either.** Porphyry's claims are reported as his, Taylor's as his, and the later Platonists quoted in Taylor's notes as theirs. No living or dead person is diagnosed, and nothing here validates or invalidates anyone's practice. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no one.*

## 1. What kind of book this is

The book is thirty-odd pages of ancient Greek philosophy doing one thing: explaining a passage of Homer. It opens with its question and never leaves it.

> "What does Homer obscurely signify by the cave in Ithaca, which he describes in the following verses ?"

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^s1q] What follows is not a treatise on the soul that happens to use Homer as an illustration. It is an exegesis of fourteen lines of the *Odyssey*, book thirteen, and every sentence of it is accountable to those lines. That is worth stating at the start, because it fixes what kind of claim the treatise makes and what kind it cannot make.

The treatise is also the blog's first **direct** Porphyry. He has been in the series all along as a figure on the edge of it: he is the pupil who arranged and edited [Plotinus](/plotinus-collected-writings/), and it is his *Letter to Anebo* that [Iamblichus, On the Mysteries](/iamblichus-on-the-mysteries/) answers. Here he is read as a book, and the book is small and self-contained — the last thing the author does is announce that he has finished.

The edition it arrives in matters, and it arrives twice. Taylor published the treatise in 1823 in the *Select Works*, where it sits between the books on abstinence and the *Auxiliaries*, and where the volume's own title page advertises, after the translations, "AN APPENDIX, EXPLAINING THE ALLEGORY OF THE WANDERINGS OF ULYSSES. BY THE TRANSLATOR."[^tp] So the 1823 volume does two things the 1917 reissue does not: it carries an Introduction in which Taylor frames the treatise for a reader, and it carries Taylor's own essay in which the method of the treatise is extended over the whole *Odyssey*. Taylor's Introduction is where he says what he thinks the treatise is for:

> "Porphyry, in his treatise On the Cave of the Nymphs, having informed us, that Numenius, the Pythagorean, considered the person of Ulysses, in the Odyssey, as the image of a man who passes in a regular manner over the stormy sea of generation, or a sensible life, and thus at length arrives at a region where tempest and seas are unknown, and finds a nation …"

— Thomas Taylor, Introduction to the 1823 *Select Works*, page xii.[^tintro] Two things in that sentence are Taylor's and not Porphyry's: the double gloss "the stormy sea of generation, or a sensible life", and the claim that Porphyry "informed us" of the Ulysses reading. Porphyry does attribute it — but to Numenius and his followers, not to himself, and the post comes back to that.

The 1917 printing is a cheap reissue of the same translation, and it is the printing read first here because it prints the treatise alone, at section numbers that are stable throughout: one through eighteen. It is also a printing on which a reader's habits matter, because the type is broken in places, and the title page advertises the treatise itself as "From the Greeh of Porphyry".[^title] That is the general condition of the two printings and the reason the Status note above says what it says: a quotation is taken from whichever printing carries it cleanly, and says which.

## 2. The verses, and the objection the treatise starts from

The passage under discussion is the description of the cave at the head of the harbour of Phorcys, where the Phaeacians leave the sleeping Odysseus. Taylor's version of it, as Porphyry quotes it:

> "High at the head a branching olive grows
> And crowns the pointed cliffs with shady boughs.
> A cavern pleasant, though involved in night,
> Beneath it lies, the Naiades' delight :
> Where bowls and urns of workmanship divine
> And massy beams in native marble shine ;
> On which the Nymphs amazing webs display,
> Of purple hue and exquisite array.
> The busy bees within the urns secure
> Honey delicious, and like nectar pure.
> Perpetual waters through the grotto glide,
> A lofty gate unfolds on either side ;
> That to the north is pervious to mankind :
> The sacred south t'immortals is consign'd."

— Porphyry, section 1, quoting Homer; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^verses]

Fourteen lines, and everything in the treatise hangs on them. The first move is not interpretation but a refutation of the obvious reading. Porphyry begins by denying that Homer is reporting a real cave, and he does it by borrowing an argument:

> "that the poet, under the veil of allegory, conceals some mysterious signification"

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^veil] The argument around that clause is Cronius', and Porphyry marks it as his from the beginning: those who have described the island have, as Cronius says, "made no mention of such a cave being found in it";[^cronius1] and Cronius is the one who insists that the story cannot be history, because

> "it is impossible to be persuaded, that in the Ithacensian cave men descend, and Gods ascend."

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^ithaca] The reasoning is blunt and it is worth seeing whole: nature does not put one path in one cave for all humanity and another for all the gods, so a poet who says she does is saying something else. Porphyry's own sentence making the same point is the cleanest statement of the treatise's premise:

> "And it is equally absurd to suppose, that nature herself should point out, in this place, one path for the descent of all mankind, and again another path for all the Gods."

— Porphyry, section 1; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^absurd]

From there the treatise builds a question list out of the physical details, and it is the list that makes it a commentary rather than an essay. Why is it not simply the cave of the nymphs, but of the nymphs called Naiades? Why are there bowls and jars when nothing is said to fill them, but bees deposit honey in them as in hives? Why are the weaving beams made of stone rather than wood? Why is the gate of men turned to the north and the gate of the gods to the south, when nearly every temple faces east?[^qlist] Then a concession, and a passage of geography: Porphyry allows that something like the cave may exist, quoting Artemidorus the Ephesian on the island's dimensions and its harbour named Phorcys, and then says: "This cave, therefore, will not be entirely an Homeric fiction." But whether the poet describes it as it is or adds something of his own, "the same inquiries remain".[^artemidorus] The treatise is not going to settle whether the cave is real. It is going to settle what it means.

## 3. The cave is the world

The first identification is the large one, and Porphyry gives it in the second section: the cave is the cosmos, and it is consecrated to the cosmos, "collectively, according to the whole of itself, or separately, according to its parts". The reasoning runs through matter, which is what a cave is made of:

> "through the cave indicating the world, which was generated from matter"

— Porphyry, section 2; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^worldmatter] Caves are, for the most part, spontaneous productions, connascent with the earth, one uniform mass of stone that is hollow inside and spread over an indefinite extent outside; and the world, being produced from no external cause and being self-adherent, is allied to matter. Matter, on this account, "is denominated a stone and a rock, on account of its sluggish and repercussive nature" with respect to form.[^matter] And then the sentence that makes the whole allegory turn, and the one a reader should hold onto:

> "through the connecting power, and orderly distribution of form, from which also it is called world, it is beautiful and delightful."

— Porphyry, section 2; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^connect] The cave is dark and it is pleasant, and the two are not a contradiction to be resolved but the two aspects of the same thing:

> "Hence it may very properly be denominated a cave; as being lovely, indeed, to him who first enters into it, through its participation of forms, but obscure to him who surveys its foundation and examines it with an intellectual eye."

— Porphyry, section 2; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^lovely] Porphyry says the same in his own summary: the cave's exterior and superficial parts are pleasant, its interior and profound parts obscure.

That is the frame, and it is worth noting what kind of a claim it is. It is not that the cave is *like* the world. It is that a cave is a symbol of the world, on the grounds that the two share matter, darkness and a hollow shape — a claim about what a cave **is**. The treatise then establishes the practice of dedicating caves, which is a fact about cult: the Persians, "mystically signifying the descent of the soul into the sublunary regions, and its regression from it, initiate the mystic … in a place which they denominate a cavern"; and Eubulus is quoted for the report that Zoroaster first consecrated a cave in the mountains of Persia, "in honour of Mithra, the maker and father of all things".[^persiancave] Caves were dedicated to the world and to nymphs:

> "so to the world they dedicated caves and dens; as likewise to Nymphs … on account of the water which trickles, or is diffused in caverns, over which the Naiades, as we shall shortly observe, preside."

— Porphyry, section 3; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing. The ellipsis stands for a note-reference the printing carries there.[^cavesdens]

The fourth section separates two senses and is where the treatise does its most careful work. Caves can be symbols of the intelligible world too — invisible to sense, of a firm and stable essence — and Porphyry says the ancients used both senses "by different and not the same conceptions". The Homeric cave is not spherical, has a twofold entrance, and contains perpetually flowing water; therefore it is an image not of the intelligible but of the sensible essence, and it is sacred to the Naiades, the nymphs of streams, and not to mountain or rural nymphs. Then the identification that does the work:

> "For the ancients thought that these souls are incumbent on water which is inspired by divinity, as Numenius says"

— Porphyry, section 4; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^numeniuswater] The sentence continues, and the continuation is the treatise's thesis in one clause:

> "for souls descending into generation fly to moisture"

— Porphyry, section 4; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^moisture] The witnesses are stacked up behind it: Heraclitus, that

> "that moisture appears delightful and not deadly to souls"

— quoted in Porphyry, section 4; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing;[^heraclitus1] Heraclitus again, in another place, "We live their death, and we die their life";[^heraclitus2] the Egyptians, who represent the daemons and the planets standing not on anything solid but on a sailing vessel, because souls descending fly to moisture; and the Stoics, who say the sun is nourished from the sea:

> "Some likewise are of opinion, that the bodies in the air, and in the heavens, are nourished by vapours from fountains and rivers, and other exhalations."

— Porphyry, section 4; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^stoics]

Water is therefore the substance of the descent, and the fifth section is a short physiology of it. Souls, corporeal or incorporeal, while they attract body — "and especially such as are about to be bound to blood and moist bodies" — verge to humidity:

> "It is necessary, therefore, that souls, whether they are corporeal or incorporeal, while they attract to themselves body, and especially such as are about to be bound to blood and moist bodies, should verge to humidity, and be corporalised, in consequence of being drenched in moisture."

— Porphyry, section 5; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^humidity] The dead are evoked by the effusion of bile and blood, and souls that love body condense a humid vehicle like a cloud. Heraclitus supplies the maxim in the other direction — "a dry soul is the wisest" — and Porphyry's own sentence states the mechanism:

> "Hence, here also the spirit becomes moist and more aqueous through the desire of generation, the soul thus attracting a humid vapour from verging to generation."

— Porphyry, section 5; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^mistsoul] And then the identification that ties the water to the nymphs, in five words:

> "into generation are the nymphs called naiades"

— Porphyry, section 5; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^naiades] The nymphs of the water are the souls on their way in. Marriage is called being a nymph, because it is being joined to generation; the pouring of water into baths is the same figure.

## 4. The two gates, and which is for whom

The gates are the sharpest part of the treatise and the part the blog came for, and Porphyry builds to them slowly. Sections nine and ten establish that caves were consecrated to gods before temples were built — the Curetes in Crete to Jupiter, a cave in Arcadia to the Moon and to Lycean Pan, Naxus to Bacchus — and that wherever Mithra was known, "they propitiated the God in a cavern".[^mithra] Then he returns to the verse he quoted at the start:

> "Homer was not satisfied with saying that it had two gates"

— Porphyry, section 9; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^twogates] The rest of that sentence the 1917 printing breaks, and the 1823 printing carries it: the poet adds that one of the gates "was turned towards the north, but the other, which was more divine, to the south".[^twogatesb] And Porphyry notices a piece of authorial care in the next section: Homer says the northern gate is pervious to descent, but does not say the southern gate is pervious at all — of the southern gate alone, Porphyry writes, the poet says only "It is inaccessible to men, but it is the path of the immortals."[^inaccessible]

The identification of the gates is where the cosmology becomes astronomy. Porphyry states the premise as a received one, and attributes it:

> "as Numenius and his familiar Cronius assert"

— Porphyry, section 10; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^numcron] That is the attribution he gives for "a cavern is an image and symbol of the world"; the two caps of the heaven are then his own mapping, working from the received identification of the cavern with the cosmos:

> "the winter tropic, than which nothing is more southern, and the summer tropic, than which nothing is more northern."

— Porphyry, section 10; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^tropics] The summer tropic is in Cancer and the winter tropic in Capricorn; Cancer, nearest to us, is attributed to the Moon, and Capricorn, invisible from here, to Saturn. And then the gates are named:

> "that Cancer is the gate through which souls descend; but Capricorn that through which they ascend."

— Porphyry, section 11; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^cancer] Cancer is northern and suited to descent, Capricorn southern and suited to ascent:

> "Cancer is indeed northern, and adapted to descent; but Capricorn is southern, and adapted to ascent"

— Porphyry, section 11; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^cancerb]

So the verse's two gates are the two solstitial signs, and the direction of travel in the poem's geography is the direction of travel in the cosmos: north is down, south is up. Porphyry then does something a reviewer of this post should mark, because it is the treatise's most exact piece of reading and it is entirely checkable against the verse. Homer says the southern gate belongs to "the immortals", not to "the gods", and Porphyry insists on the difference:

> "but the southern gates are not the avenues of the Gods, but of souls ascending to the Gods."

— Porphyry, section 11; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^avenues] The reason he gives is a claim about us:

> "this appellation being also common to our souls, which are per se, or essentially, immortal."

— Porphyry, section 11; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^appellation] The poem's word is honoured exactly, and the honouring changes what the gate is for. If the south gate were the gods' own avenue, the cave would be a picture of divine traffic; because the word is "immortals", it is a picture of souls going home. **A reader can check that move against the verse.** That matters, and it is one of only two things in this treatise a second person can check.

The rest of the gate doctrine runs out in the same direction. The northern parts belong to souls descending, and the winds follow the gates: the north wind suits souls falling into generation, its blasts refresh the dying "and when they can scarcely draw their breath", while "the south wind, being hot, dissolves this life, and sends it upward to the heat of a divine nature".[^winds] Caves and gates were consecrated before temples, and the gates of the heaven are kept by the Hours. Then the general principle, which is the treatise's most portable sentence:

> "Since, therefore, every twofold entrance is a symbol of nature, this Homeric cavern has, very properly, not one portal only, but two gates, which differ from each other conformably to things themselves"

— Porphyry, section 14; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^twofold] A twofold entrance is a symbol of a nature that runs in two directions, and the two gates are not one door with two names:

> "of which one pertains to Gods and good (daemons), but the other to mortals and depraved natures."

— Porphyry, section 14; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^pertains] The treatise's remaining witnesses on this point are Parmenides, who "mentions these two gates" in a treatise on the nature of things; the Romans, who free slaves at the Saturnalia when the sun is in Capricorn; the Egyptians, whose year begins at Cancer; Pythagoras, for whom the souls collected in the galaxy are "the people of dreams"; and Pherecydes Syrus, who "also mentions recesses and trenches, caverns, doors, and gates" — a place where the 1823 printing carries more of the sentence than the 1917 printing does.[^witnesses]

## 5. The nymphs, the vessels, and the loom

The sixth section brings the allegory down to objects, and this is where the treatise is most like a diagram and most like an argument. The cave is consecrated in common to souls and to the nymphs who preside over waters; the question is which symbol is for which. The stony bowls and jars are for the aquatic nymphs — they are, Porphyry concedes, symbols of Bacchus too, but of baked earth and friendly to the vine, whereas the stone ones suit nymphs "who preside over the water that flows from rocks".[^bowls] And to souls coming in:

> "what symbol can be more appropriate than those instruments pertaining to weaving?"

— Porphyry, section 6; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^weaving] The answer to the stone beams is anatomy:

> "For the formation of the flesh is on and about the bones, which in the bodies of animals resemble stones."

— Porphyry, section 6; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^flesh] So the beams of the loom are stone because the frame of a living body is bone, and the purple web is flesh, woven from blood. The body is then given the treatise's second large figure:

> "the body is a garment with which the soul is invested, a thing wonderful to the sight"

— Porphyry, section 6; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^garment] The figure is not Porphyry's coinage and he says so: Proserpine, "who is the inspective guardian of everything produced from seed, is represented by Orpheus as weaving a web", and the heavens are called a veil by the ancients, being as it were the vestment of the celestial gods.[^proserpine]

The seventh and eighth sections are about honey, and they are the best example in the treatise of how far a single physical detail will stretch. The jars are said to hold honeycombs, not water; honey is the nutriment of bees, and it is also the nutriment offered to the dead, since the poet pours nectar and ambrosia into the nostrils of corpses to prevent putrefaction. Honey is both cathartic and preservative — so both a purification and a preservative power, "the symbol of a preserving and defending power" when the Persians offer it to the guardian of fruits.[^persians] And then the reading on which the whole section turns, because it is the reading of a *myth*:

> "the sweetness of honey signifies, with theologists, the same thing as the pleasure arising from generation, by which Saturn, being ensnared, was castrated."

— Porphyry, section 7; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^honey] In Orpheus, Saturn is caught by Jupiter through honey, is intoxicated, and sleeps; the sweetness of honey therefore means the pleasure that draws souls down. Bees are then read the same way — the priestesses of Ceres were called bees, Proserpine was called honied, the moon was called a bee and also a bull — and honey is at the same time a symbol of death, which is why libations of honey go to the terrestrial gods. There is even a caveat at the boundary:

> "All souls, however, proceeding into generation, are not simply called bees, but those who will live in it justly and who, after having performed such things as are acceptable to the Gods"

— Porphyry, section 8; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^bees] Bees do not sit on beans, and Porphyry reports the reason: the bean was a symbol of generation proceeding in a straight line without flexure, because its stalk is perforated throughout without knots.[^beans]

Two things should be said about this section as a section, and both are structural rather than evaluative. The first is that **every detail has been assigned a meaning, and none has been left over.** Water, stone, bone, flesh, wool, purple, honey, bees, beans, the direction of a door: all of them are read, and the reading is total. The second is that the assignment is not uniform in kind: the nymphs-water link is asserted as something the ancients thought; the honey link is attributed to "theologists" and to Orpheus; the flesh-and-bones link is Porphyry's own inference, from anatomy to a loom. A reader who wants to know which readings are whose can get it — because the treatise names its sources. That is the checkability this text actually offers, and the counterweight section below comes back to it.

## 6. The olive, and Ulysses as the soul

The last stretch of the treatise turns to the olive at the head of the cave, and it is where the allegory becomes advice. Porphyry starts by refusing the trivial reading:

> "But the growth of the olive in such a situation is not fortuitous, as some one may suspect, but contains the enigma of the cavern."

— Porphyry, section 15; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^olive] The olive is the plant of Minerva, and Minerva is wisdom — the 1917 printing breaks that sentence and the 1823 printing carries it:

> "hence an olive, the symbol of this wisdom, flourishes near the present cavern, which is an image of the world."

— Porphyry, section 15; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^olive2] The olive is ever-flourishing, its leaves turning white end upward in summer and downward in winter, and that is why suppliants extend olive branches: they shall exchange "the sorrowful darkness of danger for the fair light of security and peace".[^branch] So the world is governed by an intellectual nature, and the Demiurgus "invigorates miserable and suppliant souls".[^demiurgus]

Then the sixteenth section, which is the treatise's nearest thing to an instruction, and the sentence the blog has to read twice:

> "it is requisite to sit at the foot of the olive, and consult with Minerva by what means we may most effectually destroy that hostile rout of passions which insidiously lurk in the secret recesses of the soul."

— Porphyry, section 16; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing. The 1917 printing carries the same sentence with two words broken.[^sit] That is the treatise telling someone to do something — and it is telling **Ulysses**, inside the allegory. Porphyry's preamble makes that clear: "Here, naked, and assuming a suppliant habit", afflicted in body, having cast aside everything superfluous and being averse to the energies of sense, one sits at the foot of the olive. The person who sits is the figure in the poem, read as the soul.

And then the identification the treatise has been building to, and the one Porphyry attributes to his predecessors rather than claims for himself:

> "it was not without reason that Numenius and his followers thought the person of Ulysses in the Odyssey represented to us a man, who passes in a regular manner over the dark and stormy sea of generation, and thus at length arrives at that region where tempests and seas are unknown, and finds a nation
> "Who ne'er knew salt, or heard the billows roar.""

— Porphyry, section 16; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^ulysses] So Odysseus is the soul that comes in through the water and goes out through it. The port of the cave is named Phorcys, and Porphyry reads the name as a statement about matter: "It is the port of the ancient marine Phorcys";[^phorcys] the Cyclops, born of Thoosa and blinded by Ulysses, is the natal daemon whose blinding brings a pursuit of anger from the marine and material gods. And the seat under the olive belongs to Ulysses precisely as a suppliant,

> "as to one who implores divinity, and would appease his natal demon with a suppliant branch."

— Porphyry, section 17; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^suppliant] The treatise ends the story at the oar mistaken for a winnowing fan, and then, in the eighteenth section, turns to defend itself:

> "It must not, however, be thought, that interpretations of this kind are forced, and nothing more than the conjectures of ingenious men"

— Porphyry, section 18; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^forced] The defence is not a rule for reading. It is a claim about the poet:

> "it must not be denied that he has obscurely … indicated the images of things of a more divine nature in the fiction of a fable"

— Porphyry, section 18; Taylor's translation, 1823 printing.[^fable] And the ground of that claim is Homer's wisdom:

> "it would not have been possible to devise the whole of this hypothesis unless the figment had been transferred (to an appropriate meaning) from certain established truths."

— Porphyry, section 18; Taylor's translation, 1917 printing.[^figment] Then the treatise stops: "But reserving the discussion of this for another treatise, we shall here finish our explanation of the present Cave of the Nymphs."[^finish]

## 7. The correction the blog owes

The last reading ended on a question, and the question was the blog's own instrument being corrected. Plotinus' frame, the twenty-third reading found, has no location for the part of it that cannot be checked: there is no seat, no practice, no stated axiom — nothing outside the thinker at all — and the route terminates in the practitioner's own state. So the instrument gained a fourth question: does the route terminate in the practitioner's own state, and is there anything at all that someone other than the practitioner could inspect? The blog came to Porphyry expecting the answer to be yes, on the grounds that he is Plotinus' pupil and that his treatise is a location — a cave, a map, two gates.

The answer is yes, and it is not the answer the blog was expecting, and the difference is the finding.

**First, the route is located, and it can be pointed at.** Not by the practitioner — by anyone. The verses exist; a second person can read Odyssey 13 and see that there is a cave with two gates and one is north; they can see that Homer gives the south gate to "immortals" and not to "gods", and see that Porphyry's reading of the southern gate turns on exactly that word. They can see the identification of the gates with Cancer and Capricorn, and its consistency with the verse's directions. They can go and find Cronius and Artemidorus and Numenius named and see what Porphyry takes from each. **The uncheckable part of this frame occupies a location, and the location is a text.** That is a genuine answer to the fourth question, and it is a different kind of answer from anything else in the series.

**Second, and this is the part the blog owes honestly: what the location locates is a reading, and nothing in the treatise adjudicates it.** The blog's instrument asked about the uncheckable part as though all uncheckable parts were the same shape. They are not, and Porphyry's text makes the difference visible. Compare the two cases inside one school, one generation apart:

- In Plotinus, the uncheckable part is a **state** — the soul turned inward, which is unshareable in principle. Someone else cannot inspect it, and could not if they tried.
- In Porphyry, the uncheckable part is a **reading** — a public object, quotable, arguable, checkable for consistency, and still undecided. Someone else can inspect it perfectly well. What they cannot do is decide it, because nothing in the treatise, and nothing in Homer, settles why a cave should be a cosmos rather than a sanctuary, or why water should mean generation rather than, say, purification.

So the correction is not the one the blog expected. **The blog's instrument had been treating "location" as a property of a frame, and Plotinus' answer as a property of the school. Porphyry shows both are properties of the individual text.** The pupil of the man whose route terminates in a private state writes a route that terminates in a public poem.

**Third, the treatise does not station a practitioner, and it says so by its form.** The treatise's only imperative is inside the allegory — the sentence that puts someone under the olive to consult Minerva, whose subject is the figure in the poem, read as the soul. There is no initiation prescribed, no object to be used correctly, no office. And there is a comparison worth making precisely, because the series has read the neighbours. Iamblichus' frame takes the efficacy out of the thinker and puts it into an act performed on symbols, so its uncheckable part is a practice, with the gods' knowledge as its operative content. Proclus' *Elements* puts the frame into two hundred and eleven propositions, so its uncheckable part is the first principle, offered as a postulate at the head of a proof. Porphyry's treatise has neither: no act whose correct performance could be checked, and no proposition to inspect. **Its whole external content is fourteen lines of Homer and a claim about what they mean** — and Porphyry himself locates the ground of that claim in Homer's wisdom, not in a criterion.

That is the sense in which the last reading's fourth question needs one more cut, and this book is what makes it askable: **a reading is public and arguable, and being public and arguable is not the same as being decidable.** The blog's instrument can now ask whether a frame's uncheckable part is a state or a reading, because it has one of each, in one line of succession, from the same hand that arranged the *Enneads*.

## 8. The counterweight: three gaps, in the body

**First, the allegorical warrant, which is the structural cost, and the treatise states it itself.** Porphyry's method is total: every physical detail in the passage gets a meaning, and the meanings are supplied from outside the poem. The treatise's own defence of the method — quoted in section 6 above — answers the charge of forcing with a premise about Homer rather than a rule: the figment could not have been devised at all "unless the figment had been transferred (to an appropriate meaning) from certain established truths". That is not a criterion of correct reading; it is a claim about the poet's competence. And it is the honest shape of the cost: **the treatise's interpretations cannot be wrong in a way a reader could detect, because there is no test outside the reading itself.** A modern reader is free to say that Homer simply described a cave, and nothing in the treatise can defeat that, because the treatise's defence of allegory is itself allegorical. Porphyry knows an objection is available; he reports the objection, and he answers it with the standing of Homer. The blog should say plainly that this is a real and unsolved structural cost, and that the treatise is not naive about it.

**Second, the method is named, and this is the checkability the treatise actually offers.** The treatise's argument is dense with attributions, and they can be sorted. From **Cronius** Porphyry takes the whole claim that the cave is not historical and the reading of the gates as a starting question. From **Numenius** he takes the identification of souls with water — "as Numenius says" — and, with Cronius, the assertion that a cavern is an image and symbol of the world. From **Numenius and his followers** he takes the Ulysses reading, and he says so in the opening of the sentence, which is why Taylor's Introduction — which says Porphyry "informed us" of it — is Taylor's summary and not Porphyry's claim. From **Artemidorus the Ephesian**, in the fifth book of his work, he takes the geography of the island. From **Eubulus**, the report about Zoroaster and the cave of Mithra. From **Parmenides**, the two gates in his treatise *On the Nature of Things*; from **Pherecydes Syrus**, another mention of doors and gates. From **Heraclitus** the moisture maxims; from **Empedocles**, "Now at this secret cavern we're arrived"; from **Plato**, the cave of the *Republic* and the tubs of the *Gorgias*; from **Pythagoras**, the people of dreams in the galaxy; from **theologists** and **Orpheus**, the honey and the weaving of Proserpine; from **Sophocles**, the bees. What is Porphyry's own is the mapping of the two tropic signs onto the two gates, the argument from bone to stone beam, and — the sharpest instance — the insistence that the verse's word "immortals" rather than "gods" gives the southern gate its passengers. **This is checkable in a way a state is not: a reader can see whose reading each one is, and can go and check the attribution.** It does not make the readings decidable. It makes them attributable, which is a different and considerable thing, and it is the reason this post can report the allegory as the treatise gives it rather than as Porphyry alone gives it.

**Third, the translation, which is a Romantic rendering of a late-antique Greek text and should be said to be.** Both printings read here are Thomas Taylor's, made in 1823 and reissued in 1917; this reading had no critical English edition to work from, and no second translator to set against him. That has three consequences a reader should hold. **The first is that Taylor's own frame is printed with the text.** He introduces the treatise in 1823, and the same volume carries an Appendix in which he extends the method over the whole *Odyssey* — and Taylor there names his own sources as plainly as Porphyry does: "from the hints afforded by Porphyry, and the work of an anonymous Greek writer, De Ulyxis Erroribus, to unfold the latent meaning of the wanderings of Ulysses, as narrated by Homer."[^appendix] So the 1823 volume offers two readings of Homer, the second in the same method as the first. **The second is that Taylor annotates Porphyry, and criticises him.** His note on the castration of Saturn is a direct charge:

> "Porphyry, though he excelled in philosophical, was deficient in theological knowledge; of which what he now says of the castrations of Saturn and Heaven is a remarkable instance."

— Thomas Taylor, note to Porphyry, section 7; 1917 printing.[^tnote10] So the translator's apparatus is a second reading with its own standing, and Taylor's notes also bring in Hermias, Proclus, Olympiodorus, Simplicius, Macrobius and Martianus Capella — a later Platonic reading of Porphyry's reading. Where those notes quote, they are marked here as whose words they are; the note on the nymphs is a case in point, since it gives the frame's title-role in another hand: "are Goddesses who preside over regeneration, and are ministrant to Bacchus, the offspring of Semele".[^hermias] **The third consequence is that the two printings are damaged, and differently, so a reader has no clean copy of the treatise to fall back on.** Where one printing is broken the quotations here come from the other and say so, which is a way of working, not a critical text. Nothing in this post rests on a word that only one of the two printings carries.

---

## 9. What this reading claims

**One.** Porphyry's *On the Cave of the Nymphs* is a commentary on fourteen lines of Odyssey 13, in eighteen numbered sections, and it is a whole book at that size. It opens with its question — what Homer obscurely signifies by the cave in Ithaca — and closes by announcing that it has finished, reserving the rest for another treatise.

**Two.** The method is total allegory built on a premise stated at the outset: the story cannot be history, because nature does not put one path in one cave for all humanity and another for all the gods. Every physical detail in the passage then receives a meaning, and the meanings are drawn from outside the poem — stone is matter, water is the descent of souls, bone is the stone beam of a loom, purple is flesh, honey is the pleasure that draws souls down.

**Three.** The two gates are the sharpest part of it. The northern gate is the descent of souls, the southern the path of the immortals, and the two are identified with the two tropic signs, Cancer and Capricorn. The treatise's most exact reading — that Homer writes "immortals" and not "gods", and that the difference makes the southern gate the way home for souls rather than an avenue of the gods — is checkable against the verse, and it is one of the two things in this text a second person can check.

**Four.** The correction. The last reading found a frame with no location for its uncheckable part and asked whether the route always terminates in the practitioner's own state. Porphyry, Plotinus' own pupil and the editor of his papers, gives the route a place: a cave in Ithaca, two gates, geography. So **"is the route located?" is not a property of the tradition but of the individual text** — the same succession produces a route with no location and a route that is nothing but a location. But what the location locates is a reading, and a reading is public and arguable without being decidable. The blog's instrument therefore gains a fifth cut it did not have before: **ask whether the uncheckable part of a frame is a state or a reading.** This treatise has one of the second kind, and it is the first the series has read.

**Five.** The counterweights, in the body, and there are three. **The allegorical warrant is the structural cost**: the treatise's own defence of its method answers the charge of forcing with a claim about Homer's wisdom rather than a criterion of reading, so no interpretation in it can be shown wrong by anything outside the interpretation, and Porphyry does not pretend otherwise. **The method is named, and that is the real checkability**: Cronius, Numenius, Artemidorus, Eubulus, Parmenides, Pherecydes, Heraclitus, Empedocles, Plato, Pythagoras, Orpheus, theologists — a reader can see whose reading each one is, which makes them attributable without making them decidable. **And the translation is a Romantic rendering with no critical English edition behind it**, printed with its translator's own Introduction and Appendix, and with his notes criticising the author whose text he is translating.

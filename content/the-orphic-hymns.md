# The Orphic Hymns

### Nineteenth of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — a liturgy rather than a doctrine, a frame performed instead of argued, and the one thing it never builds, with the scholars' own account of what the silence was for

*Nineteenth of the `readings`. Three readings running, the series has taken an **assertion**: [the
Picatrix](/picatrix/) gave a cosmology as equipment, laid out as operations and correspondences; [the Chaldean
Oracles](/the-chaldean-oracles/) gave the same shape as a metaphysics in fragments, a revelation the later
Platonists argued over; [Black Abbot, White Magic](/black-abbot-white-magic/) gave it as text inside a text, a
doctrine held in reserve. Each of the three tells a reader what is the case. This one tells a reader almost
nothing. Eighty-seven short poems, each addressed to a single power, each ending in a request, all but four
carrying a line at the top naming a substance to burn. No argument, no thesis, no defence, no system — [a frame one
does](/the-frame-is-a-variable/) rather than a frame one holds.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source: the Athanassakis and Wolkow translation of *The Orphic Hymns*
(Johns Hopkins University Press, 2013), from a complete digital text of the edition, all eighty-seven hymns with
the Introduction and notes. Every quotation below is checked character for character against that text and cited
by **hymn number and line**, using the edition's own numbering — the opening address is cited as its own piece,
since it is unnumbered and printed before hymn 1. The text as it reaches this post is not clean, and what is
deformed in it is stated rather than repaired. Its typographic quotation marks are rendered here as straight
ASCII and its curly apostrophes, dashes and ellipses are normalised; a quotation set inside another quotation
keeps the source's inner double quotes as single quotes. Its Greek is not quoted at all. Three artefacts are
repaired silently, and they are worth naming: the text as it reaches this post frequently **carries the print
edition's line numbers inside the running line** — "Hear, O blessed goddess, 3 jet-black and starlit" — and
those stray numerals are relocated to the verse breaks the edition prints them at, so that a quoted line reads
as the printed line; where a digit in a heading has been corrupted ("$7. To Death" for "87. To Death") I read
what the edition's contents list requires the heading to be; and the era abbreviation, which the text mangles
into stray glyphs, is decoded here as CE. Nothing else is silently changed. Elisions are marked with a bare
ellipsis. The reading of the material through this blog's model is an **inference**, offered as one.
**Nothing here claims the gods of these hymns exist and nothing here claims they do not.** The Introduction's
historical judgements are attributed to the scholars throughout, and to nobody else — they are their claims
about the text, not the text's claims about itself. No living or dead person is diagnosed. As everywhere:
describe structures, diagnose no one.

---

## 1. The assertion that is missing

Eighteen readings in, the series has had one reliable method. Take a book whole — its frame, its omissions, its
own account of its authority — and ask what a blog that has quoted sources all along now has to change. It has
worked because the books **assert**. The Picatrix argues by tabulating — its four-book plan runs the material
into images, planetary petitions and elections, and if you doubt the frame the table itself is the reply. The
Chaldean Oracles assert more violently: the fragment collection opens with an
imperative addressed to the reader's mind — "You must not perceive it intently, but keeping the pure eye of your
soul turned away …"[^oc1] — and every line after that is a claim about how reality is arranged.

**These eighty-seven poems assert nothing.** They are liturgy. Each one names a power in the vocative, recites
what that power is, and asks it to come; all but four state what to burn. There is no argument for the existence
of Hekate; there is a hymn that begins by addressing her, and the address is the whole of the case. The scholars
describe what this means on the page, and their description is an editorial observation rather than a
theological one:

> "The Orphic Hymns, as well as similar hymns, have neither plot nor action."[^intro]

And in the same place, the sentence that is the hinge of this entire reading:

> "None of our hymns shows even a trace of humor, rhetorical conceit, or for that matter a desire to delight or
> shock the initiates."[^intro]

That is a description of **absence**, and absence is hard to show. What the scholars are pointing at is that
the collection contains no persuasion addressed to a reader. A hymn does not argue that its addressee exists,
or that the correspondence is real, or that the practitioner will benefit. It **performs** all three and leaves
no trace of having done so.

So this reading cannot be "what does the book claim," because the book makes no claims to examine. It has to
be the harder question: what does a frame look like when it is **enacted** instead of argued? The answer is
already in the text's shape — the epithets are the theology, and the incense is the correspondence — and the
rest of this post is that shape. What changes for the blog is not a doctrine it must now accept or refuse. It is
that the thing the last three books theorised has been sitting here the whole time in a form that theorises
nothing.

## 2. What the collection is, in its own voice

A hymn collection is an odd object to read at book scale, because its unit is not the book. It is one poem, and
the poem is complete in twelve lines. The collection is eighty-seven of those, and its own words for itself are
thinner than any of the texts this series has taken so far.

The heading of the first item is the whole of its frame:

> "Orpheus to Mousaios"[^toc]

That is it — a title, and then a poem. The title names a speaker and a hearer, and the poem is the speaker's
instruction to the hearer, in the second person. It opens with a greeting and, at once, the instruction itself:

> "Friend, use it to good fortune. / Learn now, Mousaios, / a mystical and most holy rite, / a prayer which surely
> excels all others."[^proem]

What follows is a catalogue of powers, some forty-four lines long, longer than almost any single hymn in the
collection and structurally unlike all of them: it invokes Zeus and Earth together, the Sun, the Moon, the
Stars, Poseidon, Persephone, Demeter, Artemis, Phoibos, Dionysos, Ares, Hephaistos, Aphrodite, "king of the
underworld," Hebe, Eileithyia, Herakles, Justice, Piety, the Nymphs, Pan, Hera, Mnemosyne, the Muses, the
Graces, the Seasons, the Year, Leto, Dione, the Kouretes, the Korybantes, the Kabeiroi, the Idaian gods, Hermes,
Themis, Night, Day, Faith, Dike, Thesmodoteira, Rhea, Kronos, Tethys, Okeanos, Atlas, Aion, Time, the water of
the Styx. The list does not end there, and with a good deal of the catalogue still to come it states its own
method:

> "I call on all these gentle gods, / and I call on Pronoia, / and on the holy Daimon, / I also call on Daimon
> baneful to mortals,"[^proem]

**The baneful daimon is invoked in the same breath as the gentle gods, and the catalogue does not stop to
apologise.** In the notes, the scholars give the reason and it is a practical one:

> "Inclusivity is a characteristic of Greek polytheism, and it can be dangerous to omit the wrong divinity."[^noteO]

That sentence is doing more work than it looks. It is not a claim about the pantheon's structure. It is a claim
about **risk** — and its remedy is comprehensiveness, which is exactly what the proem delivers. The catalogue
still has far to run: past those lines it invokes "divinities dwelling / in the sky, in the air, in the water, /
on earth, under the earth, / and in the fiery element," and then Ino, Leukothea, Palaimon, Nike, Adrasteia,
Asklepios, Pallas, the winds and Thunder, the Mother of the immortals, Attis and Men, Ourania, Adonis, and
"Beginning and End, too". The list is the answer to the risk, and it is long on purpose.

So the collection's own words for itself are: a rite, a prayer, and a list long enough that nothing is left out.
The poem closes on its own performance:

> "and I ask them to come / in a spirit of joyous mercy / to this holy rite, / to this reverent libation."[^proem]

**"To this holy rite, to this reverent libation."** The frame is the ceremony. The text is not a book about the
ceremony; it is the part of the ceremony that consists of words. Everything the blog has read since its first
readings has been a description of something. This is the thing, in the form of an address.

**What the scholars say about the proem is a further and stranger fact, and it belongs here rather than in a
footnote.** They report that its position in the collection is contested:

> "There is a question whether this poem belongs with our collection or was added at some later date after the
> Hymns had been made part of a greater compilation of ancient hymns."[^noteO]

And they give the specific reason a reader can check: "the order of divinities in this poem does not, for the
most part, match the order of the hymns to individual deities."[^noteO] Some names appear only in the proem and
have no hymn; Hestia has a hymn and does not appear in the proem. Their three options are stated as options —
that it was a separate poem, that it was the *Thuépolikon* (Sacrifice or Mystic Rites) which the Souda attributes
to Orpheus, or that "the poem was an independent entity that our composer somewhat inartfully grafted onto his
efforts."[^noteO]

**The frame of the collection may be later than its contents.** That is not a defect to be corrected; it is the
shape of the object, and it is the first place this reading will find that the blog's usual questions about
authority do not fit. There is no author to locate and no date to fix at the level of the frame either.

## 3. The formula, and what the epithets carry

Take four hymns and lay them side by side. The form is stable enough that it can be described as a form, and the
stability is the point: the collection is eighty-seven instances of one pattern, and the pattern is not a
doctrine but a procedure.

**First, hymn 1, "To Hekate."** It begins with the vocative:

> "Lovely Hekate of the roads / and of the crossroads I invoke."[^oh1]

Then the realm — three of them, in a line that the scholars gloss as the same triple division Hekate has in
Hesiod:

> "In heaven, on earth, / then in the sea, saffron-cloaked,"[^oh1]

Then the epithet chain, and it is not flattering. It runs: tomb spirit reveling in the souls of the dead,
daughter of Perses, haunting deserted places, delighting in deer, nocturnal, dog-loving, monstrous queen,
devouring wild beasts, ungirt and repulsive. Then the elevation, in the same breath and without a change of
tone:

> "Herder of bulls, / queen and mistress of the whole world, / leader, nymph, / mountain-roaming nurturer of
> youths, / maiden, I beseech you to come / to these holy rites, / ever with joyous heart, / ever favoring the
> oxherd."[^oh1]

**That closing petition is not for knowledge, power, or ascent.** The hymn asks for a disposition and names, as
its beneficiary, a cult official. The scholars gloss the word: "This refers to some official of the cult. The
figure is found in other Dionysian mystery cults as well."[^note1] They note, in the Introduction, that the
title *boukolos* — oxherd — "occurs only twice in the Orphic Hymns: at hymn 1.10 and hymn 31.7," and that "it is
reasonable to assume that the underlying idea was that of the mediator who would help the initiates into a
Dionysian transformation."[^intro]

**So here is the first structural fact that matters for the blog:** the collection has an operator. There is a
person who runs this, who is named in the hymns' own closing lines, and who the scholars think functioned as a
mediator. This is not a text with no human in it. It is a text with a human in it and **no office built out of
him** — the oxherd is asked for favour in a closing line and is otherwise absent from eighty-five of the
eighty-seven poems.

**Second, a chthonic hymn: 26, "To Earth."** Its first move is the direct address of a natural power:

> "Divine Earth, mother of men / and of the blessed gods, / you nourish all, you give all, / you bring all to
> fruition, you destroy all."[^oh26]

And its central line is a cosmological claim made as an epithet, not as an assertion:

> "seat of the immortal cosmos,"[^oh26]

The scholars trace where the phrase comes from and what it does: Hesiod calls Earth "the firm seat of all/the
immortals who hold the peaks of snowy Olympos," and the same phrase, differently distributed, appears of Sky in
Hesiod.[^note26] **Here the word that carries this blog's entire structural vocabulary is being used of a
place** — Earth as the seating of the cosmos — and it recurs that way everywhere in the collection. Hymn 18, to
Plouton, has "earth, queen of all, / seat of the gods";[^oh18] hymn 19 has Zeus shaking "the seat of the gods"
with his thunderbolts;[^oh19] hymn 48, to Sabazios, has the power's arrival "to noble Tmolos / by the side of
fair-cheeked Hipta."[^oh48]
And in hymn 62, Dike is not seated *as* a mediatory office but on one: "I sing of the all-seeing eye / of comely
and radiant Dike, / who is seated upon the sacred throne / of lord Zeus."[^oh62] §6 returns to this, because the
line between an office and a seat is the line this reading has to draw, and the collection draws it differently
from every text so far.

**Third, the celestial run, and here the cosmology is.** Hymns 3 to 10 are, in order: Night, Sky, Ether,
Protogonos, the Stars, the Sun, Selene, Physis. Read as a sequence rather than separately, they are a theogony
performed. Night is addressed as the origin — "I shall sing of Night, / mother of gods and men;"[^oh3] — and
carries the sentence the scholars single out as the one that must have resonated for the initiate:

> "for dreadful Necessity / governs all things."[^oh3]

They explain why: "Necessity mated with Time to give birth to Ether, Khaos, and Erebos."[^intro] Sky follows, and
his hymn is a compressed cosmogony in ten lines: "Sky, father of all, / eternal cosmic element, / primeval
beginning of all, / end of all," and then the claim that a frame is being sung rather than described — "You are
home of the blessed gods, / your motion is a roaring whirl."[^oh4] Ether is next: "Yours are Zeus' lofty
dwelling, / endless power, too; / of the stars, of the sun, / and of the moon you claim a share."[^oh5] Then
Protogonos, the Orphic first-born, and this is where the cosmogony is at its most explicit in the whole
collection:

> "Upon two-natured Protogonos, / great and ether-tossed, I call; / born of the egg, / delighting in his golden
> wings,"[^oh6]

**"Born of the egg" is the Orphic cosmogony, stated inside a vocative.** It is not defended, dated, or
attributed. The hymn says it because the hymn is the way the statement exists. And it continues into a line the
scholars read as the cosmos being formed:

> "you scattered the dark mist, / the mist that lay before your eyes. / Flapping your wings, you whirled about /
> throughout this world, / you brought pure light."[^oh6]

The scholars give the identifications as the text's own: "Protogonos is equated with Phanes and is addressed as
'born of the egg, delighting in his golden wings' (hymn 6.2)," and "Kronos and Time (Greek *khronos*) are
characteristically equated at hymn 13.5 where Kronos is called 'begetter of time.'"[^intro] In the same
paragraph they lay out what the collection does and does not carry, and the sentence is worth quoting whole
because it is the load-bearing scholarly judgement of this entire reading:

> "Bits and pieces of Orphism are evident throughout the collection, but there is very little about Orphic
> theogony, anthropogony, or eschatology."[^intro]

Then the heavens proper. The Stars are addressed as agents of fate — "you do reveal fate, / everyone's fate, /
you determine the divine path / for mortals"[^oh7] — and asked, in a closing that shows what the collection
thinks a hymn is *for*, to attend a competition:

> "Coruscating and gleaming, / kingly and nocturnal, / visit the learned contests / of this sacred rite, / finish
> a noble race / for works of renown."[^oh7]

The Sun's hymn is where the cosmic identification is fullest — "Hear me, O blessed one, / eternal eye that sees
all," and then, near the end, the line that fuses hymn and astronomy:

> "Piping lord of the world, / fiery circle of motion,"[^oh8]

The scholars note that hymn 34, to Apollon, "contains some specific musical technical terms" and that a passage
in it "strongly suggests that the composer was at least vaguely familiar with Ptolemy's *Harmonics*," which they
offer as a dating anchor: "This would provide a terminus post quem of about 200 CE."[^intro] **That sentence is
the scholars' inference, drawn from a conjecture about what a text resembles, and it is included here as
theirs.** Where the collection's own cosmology is concerned, the scholars say something firmer and more
useful, and it is the sentence that should govern this whole section: "Their composer was not interested in
creating a religious or philosophical system."[^intro]

**Fourth, hymn 87, "To Death," the last item.** It begins by addressing the power directly:

> "Hear me, you who steer / the path of all mortals / and give sacred time to all / from whom you are distant. /
> Your sleep tears the soul / free from the body's hold,"[^oh87]

and it contains the collection's most famous doctrinal line, which the scholars flag as the capstone:

> "The idea held by the Orphics that the body was a prison for the soul is expressed in the very last hymn (hymn
> 87.3-4)."[^intro]

**And then the hymn asks Death for a long life.** The last five lines are:

> "In you alone is executed / the verdict common to all, / for to entreaties and to prayers / you alone are deaf.
> / O blessed one, / with pious vows, with sacrifices, / I beg you, I pray to you, / grant me long life, / that
> old age might be / a noble prize among men."[^oh87]

The same closing formula appears elsewhere, and it names the collection's actual hope without metaphysics: "and
bring a good end to a life / of industry and prosperity."[^oh25]

**Put the four together and the formula is visible:** vocative, epithets, a request to come, a request for
something ordinary — offspring, safety in labour, a good end, a noble old age — and, above all of it, the
incense line. The collection's theology is carried by the epithets, and the epithets are addressed to the god,
not to the reader. That is the whole difference from the Picatrix, whose tables are addressed to the operator,
and from the Oracles, whose fragments are addressed to the mind. Here the cosmology is in the second person.
The frame is spoken **to** something, and that is its entire mode of existence.

## 4. The incense, where the correspondence is performed

Every hymn but four carries one line at its head, and the line is not a rubric or an editorial note. It
is a material instruction.

> "incense—storax"[^oh1b]

That is hymn 2's. Hymn 1 is one of the four without one; hymn 3's is "incense—firebrands"; hymn 4's is
"incense—frankincense"; hymn 5's, "incense —crocus"; hymn 6's, "incense—myrrh"; hymn 8's,
"incense—pounded frankincense." The scholars count and classify them, and
the classification is the most concrete thing in the Introduction:

> "Storax was imported from Phoenicia, while incense and myrrh came from the Arabian Peninsula. There seems to
> have been a certain protocol, and a preference for this or that kind of aromatic substance. Incense for Sun,
> frankincense for Nike, Apollon, Artemis, Dionysos Liknites, and several other divinities. Myrrh was offered to
> Protogonos, Poseidon, Nereus, and Leto; storax to Prothyraia, Kronos, Zeus, Proteus, Dionysos, Demeter,
> Chthonic Hermes, etc. Crocus was offered to Ether, opium to Sleep, and simply perfumes for Moon, Physis, Rhea,
> Athene, etc."[^intro]

**This is the correspondence doctrine of the last three readings, and here it has stopped being a doctrine.** In
the Picatrix, the table of substances and planets is an argument: it is the shape of the cosmos, written out,
and one learns it in order to work. The Chaldean Oracles make it a metaphysics of tokens — *synthēmata*,
*symbola*, the material thing that is the link. In both cases the correspondence is a claim about reality. Here
it is a line of the poem, performed at the moment the poem is performed, and the scholars' own verdict on its
systematicity is a refusal to find one:

> "As has been observed it is not easy to see the connection between specific aromatic substances and
> ritualistic practices."[^intro]

And then the sentence that ends the question:

> "The distribution of perfumes and aromatic substances as they appear in our Hymns is such as to make it
> impossible for us to offer a rational explanation of any systematic organization."[^intro]

**The scholars then say where the choices probably came from, and it is not cosmology.** "The variety might be
attributed to the preference of persons who contributed the specific perfume or other material. Perhaps also the
season of the year played a role."[^intro]

That is a modest and slightly deflating finding, and this blog should hold it rather than smooth it over. The
correspondence in this collection is *thin*: it is a protocol with a preference, a supply chain from Phoenicia
and Arabia and a practical sense of what to burn. What makes it strong is not its completeness but the fact
that it is **done**. The Picatrix's correspondences have to be true to be worth anything. The Orphic Hymns'
incense only has to be burnt. **The frame here is not a picture of the world; it is an act, repeated, with a
material in it.**

Two more incense lines carry the range, and both are informative:

> "incense, any grain save beans, and aromatic herbs"[^oh26b]

That is Earth's, and it is one of only two incense lines in the collection that **withholds** something: hymn
53's leaves frankincense out of its offering. The notes to hymn 26 do not comment on the exclusion, so the line
stands as the edition prints it: a permission and a single prohibition, in a hymn to the earth. And Death's,
which brings the book full circle:

> "incense—powdered frankincense"[^oh87b]

**The last hymn in the collection is burnt with the same substance as the fourth and the eighth** — 4, 8 and 87
are the frankincense hymns. The frame opens on Night and closes on Death, and the two ends do not match: Night
takes firebrands, Death the powdered frankincense. If the blog wants a single image for what an enacted
correspondence looks like, it is that: a substance that means nothing in itself, named at the head of a poem,
and used to close the book.

## 5. The authorship is a fiction, stated plainly

Here the reading must state a structural fact with no softening, because it is the one thing about this
collection that everybody knows and nobody treats as a scandal.

**The hymns are attributed to Orpheus, and the scholars say outright that the attribution is a fiction.** Their
note on the opening poem is the cleanest statement of it:

> "The opening poem of the collection portrays the fiction of Orpheus instructing Mousaios in a rite."[^noteO]

And in the same note, they say what the fiction does for the people who used it:

> "the fiction presented here of the master Orpheus passing on wisdom to his disciple Mousaios would have had
> great significance for the initiates," and "attributing the rite to the august figure Orpheus lends the whole
> affair an aura of prestige and legitimacy …"[^noteO]

They add that Mousaios himself is "a semi-legendary figure of questionable antiquity who was thought to be both
a poet (his name means 'of the Muse') and a founder of religious rites,"[^noteO] and that the address "friend"
suggests he "seems to have been originally independent of Orpheus but later was made his son and/or disciple."[^noteO]

**So the chain is: a legendary singer instructs a semi-legendary founder, in a rite, in a text that neither
wrote.** This is the veil of pseudepigraphy over a real liturgy, and the scholars' own handling of it is
practical rather than moral — they are interested in what the fiction was *for*. One of their suggestions is
that the fiction was enacted rather than merely claimed:

> "One wonders if it was not the group but a figure of authority (the 'oxherd'?) who opened the ceremony by
> chanting these verses in the guise of Orpheus."[^noteO]

And they place that inside a known religious idiom: "There might be an element of play-acting here, of taking on
another role, something that is prevalent in Dionysian worship."[^noteO]

**That sentence is worth pausing on, because it is not a debunking.** If the founder's authority is a role that
an officiant takes on in performance, then the fiction is not a lie told about the text. It is part of the
procedure. The name is inherited, the inheritance is understood as inheritance by everyone present, and the
performance goes ahead anyway. **The text's authority comes from a name its authors did not have, and the
collection's own structure says so.**

**The dating, in the scholars' words, and it is a refusal to date.** They begin with the evidence rather than the
conclusion: the oldest reference to the Hymns is "a scholium on Hesiod's *Theogony* by Ioannes Galenos (first
half of the twelfth century CE)," who "refers to them three times in the same scholium but says nothing about
their authorship or literary value."[^intro] Then:

> "A date of composition cannot be assigned to the Hymns with any certainty."[^intro]

Their own best guess has a shape and a bracket, and both matter:

> "Those scholars who place the composition of the Orphic Hymns within the first four centuries of our era are
> probably closer to the truth. The relative purity of the language and the nearly flawless hexameter would
> argue for the earlier part of this period. So would also the remarkable absence of anything faintly
> Christian."[^intro]

And then they land, with the register of a measured guess:

> "Perhaps then a date in the middle of the third century CE is as good a guess as any."[^intro]

**The place is the same kind of answer.** "The place in which the Hymns were composed and originally used is a
mystery."[^intro] The evidence they adduce is specific and inferential: "The appearance in the Hymns of
divinities hardly known or totally unknown to mainland Greece should turn our attention eastward to Asia
Minor, where, in fact, the names of some of these less known Asiatic gods have been consistently turning up in
inscriptions. The three most telling names are Mise, Hipta, and Melinoe, all three previously known only from
the Hymns until the soil of western Anatolia revealed their existence in inscriptions."[^intro]

The Pergamon hypothesis is reported as a hypothesis with a history, not as a finding:

> "Otto Kern in 1911 was the first to suggest that Pergamum was the birthplace of the Hymns and that they were
> used in mystic Dionysian ceremonies in the temenos of Demeter in the city. His suggestion has found both
> sympathizers and critics among scholars. Until epigraphic and other evidence points to some other place we
> might consider Kern's theory a distinct possibility."[^intro]

**Single authorship is refused too.** "There is little to force us to the assumption of a single authorship for
all the Hymns. Indeed, we can scarcely assume that the collection was composed at once. The opening to Mousaios,
for example, has come under suspicion."[^intro] Their description of the composer is deliberately not a person:
"the Hymns do give the impression of being the work of a religious antiquary who had ready access to some sort
of concordance from which he marshaled forth hosts of epithets that he then linked together in hexameters."[^intro]

**On the word "Orphic," they say plainly that it does not describe a movement:**

> "the term 'Orphic' is somewhat misleading. It implies something unified, stable, and definable— something
> like a movement, a genre, a type of phenomenon. But in actuality 'Orphic' is a sort of catch-all term that is
> used to designate anything and everything directly or indirectly connected to Orpheus. And who this figure
> was leads to even more ambiguity. We do not know if he ever existed."[^intro]

And they set the Hymns against the other poems in circulation under that name: "Poems such as the Rhapsodies,
*Argonautika*, and *Lithika* were written in late antiquity. Falsely attributed to Orpheus, they are a fantasy
literature whose connection with Orphism ranges from non-existent to tenuous."[^intro] **The Hymns are not in
that class, and the distinction matters:** the scholars treat this collection as a real liturgy whose attribution
is false, and they treat the other poems as literature whose attribution is also false.

**The cult setting is stated as coming from the collection itself.** "It seems quite clear from the collection
itself that the Hymns were used by such a religious association (*thiasos*) of people who called themselves
*mustai*, *mustipoloi*, or *orgiophantai* (mystic initiates) and who through prayer (*eukhé*), libation
(*loibé*), sacrifice (*thusia*), and, presumably, secret ceremonies (*orgia*, *teletai*) invoked a series of
divinities and asked for their presence or for the gift of some blessing, such as wealth, peace, health, and not
infrequently for a blameless end to a good life."[^intro] **And the commissioning is given the same status as
anything else in the book — a reasonable inference, so marked:** "It is equally reasonable to assume that a newly
founded *thiasos* commissioned one or more able poets to provide them with religious poetry that should be as
authentic and comprehensive as possible."[^intro]

**Notice what this all amounts to, because the blog's whole machinery of suspicion has nothing to grip here.**
Nobody is being deceived about Orpheus. The scholars call the attribution a fiction, they suggest the role was
being played, and the collection was made by named-nothing poets for a real association that used it.
The pseudepigraphy is not a cover story for a doctrine. **It is how a liturgy takes on a founder it never had.**
The blog has read texts where the authority claim was load-bearing — where a lineage had to be real for the
operation to work. Here the claim is a costume, and the operation works regardless. §7 is about what that does
to the blog's most-used marker.

---

## 6. The thread the blog has been standing on

This reading was owed before it was written, and the shape of the debt is measurable.

Two readings the blog has already published both stood on this collection without reading it. [Geosophia](/geosophia/)
places the Orphic books at the substrate of the whole tradition it is reconstructing; its transmission sentence
runs the Orphic books, the Magical Papyri and the Byzantine Solomonic texts together as "the phases of a
continuous literary tradition."[^given] [The Chaldean Oracles](/the-chaldean-oracles/) inherited a theurgic
hymn-book as the authoritative revelation of the later Platonists — Majercik's "last important Sacred Book of
pagan antiquity" — and read the frame that came out of it.[^maj] And [two esoteric tarots](/two-esoteric-tarots/)
records Orphic hymns being *chanted*, in fifteenth-century Florence, by the men who were making the tarot: "they
met in Ficino's house in the hills that dominate Florence, and there they discussed the texts of Plato, shared
ideas, drank wine, played the lyre and chanted Orphic hymns."[^tet]

**What the blog sees now, having read them whole, is not a new fact about the tradition. It is that the practice
the other books theorise is here in its most direct form, and it has no claim to defend.** The Picatrix is a
manual for an operator: it tells you what to do, and the telling is itself an argument that the cosmos is
arranged the way the operations require. The Oracles are revelation: they tell you what is, and the telling is
addressed to a mind that must be prepared. **The Hymns do not tell anyone anything.** They address powers,
recite what the powers are, ask them to come, and burn something. There is no reader to persuade, no operator to
train, no doctrine to defend, and — this is the part the blog could not see from a quotation inside another
book — **no position on whether any of it is true.**

**The two structural differences from the readings immediately before.**

**The first is the operator.** All three of the last readings were read for what they say about the person at
the centre of the frame. The Picatrix is built around a practitioner, and runs Book One into the images and
petitions that practitioner works; [that reading](/picatrix/) found an office whose holder the text never
names. The Oracles station no one and the tradition built a seat around a man anyway. This collection has an
operator and says so in four words: "ever favoring the oxherd."[^oh1] He has a title, the scholars identify it
as "some official of the cult," and the title appears twice in eighty-seven poems. **There is no office here
and
no seat — and this time it is not because the frame forbids one. It is because the collection has no interest in
the question.** The oxherd is not a route, a mediator in the blog's sense, or a station on a ladder. He is the
person the hymn asks the goddess to favour.

**The second is the claim, and it is the sharpest thing in this reading.** Look at what the Hymns do *not* do.
They never argue that Hekate is real. They never warn that the material is dangerous if misused. They never
promise that the initiate who does this will be saved, or that a rival who does it differently will not. The
proem comes closest — "a mystical and most holy rite, / a prayer which surely / excels all others"[^proem] — and
even there the claim is a comparison of effectiveness, not a doctrine. **The collection asserts nothing about
the world.** It performs something in the world and leaves no theory behind.

**Which means the blog's most-used instrument has to be reconsidered.** Three readings ago the blog adopted a
test — does the frame name the endpoint, is it checkable by someone who does not benefit, does it leave a
seat — and [the frame is a variable](/the-frame-is-a-variable/) has carried the formulation that "a frame is a
variable and that the frame decides the collapse's meaning."[^fiv] The Orphic Hymns are a frame. They supply the
meaning of a ceremony, name a set of powers, coordinate a material to each, and are repeated. They are not
checkable by anyone who does not benefit, and they have no seat. **Under the blog's own criteria they are
simultaneously the most suspect kind of frame and the least dangerous, and the collection has done nothing to
earn the second description except refuse to claim anything.**

## 7. The correction: the marker is the claim, not the seat

The blog has been carrying an inference it never stated as one, and this collection breaks it.

**The inference is this:** that the marker of a dangerous frame is the **seat** — a place at the named endpoint
that someone can occupy as the route, which is what [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) identified and what
[the assumed seat](/the-assumed-seat/) and [the Chaldean Oracles](/the-chaldean-oracles/) have been testing
since. The test was built to catch a specific hazard: a frame with a seat can be entered, and a frame that can
be entered can be run by whoever gets there, and the running looks like cosmology. That finding stands. It was
earned against a dozen cases and it should not be withdrawn.

**But the Hymns are a case where the seat is not what marks the frame.** Four things are true of this
collection at once:

- it has **gods**, addressed by name, with epithets that state their powers;
- it has **mediators** — Mousaios is named in the frame, and the oxherd is named in a closing line and, on the
  scholars' inference, functioned as "the mediator who would help the initiates into a Dionysian
  transformation";[^intro]
- it has an **operator** — an association with a hierarchy, an officiant, and a commissioned text;
- and it makes **no claim whatsoever**, has no founding revelation, defends no cosmology, names no endpoint that
  must be reached, and builds no office out of its mediator.

**Every structural feature the blog has learned to read as a warning sign is present, and the thing the blog has
learned to warn about is not.** There is no route to seize, because the hymns do not describe a route. There is
no endpoint to occupy, because the closest the collection comes to an endpoint is a request for a noble old age.
There is nothing to be checkable *against*, because nothing has been asserted.

**The correction, then, is a narrowing rather than a reversal, and the blog should state it as one:**

> The marker the blog has been reading as the danger is not the seat itself. It is the **claim**. A frame that
> asserts something about the world can be contested, and the contest is done by whoever holds the seat it has
> provided — which is why [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) found that "a frame with a seat can be entered
> by anyone, and the seat makes whatever they do to occupy it look like cosmology." A frame that asserts nothing
> provides no such position, and the Orphic Hymns are the case that makes this visible, because they contain
> everything else: gods, mediators, an operator, a material correspondence, an organisation with a hierarchy.
> **The frame is entirely performative, and a performative frame has no seat to take — not because it is
> protected, but because it makes no claim that a seat could be used to hold.**

**And there is a second correction in the same finding, and it is the larger one.** The blog's frames posts have
read cosmologies as things one is **inside**: a cosmology is the largest frame, the thing so big it is invisible
as a frame, a set of contents one was born into. [The frame is a variable](/the-frame-is-a-variable/) put it in
those terms, and the metaphor has been load-bearing. **The Orphic Hymns suggest a frame one *does*, and that the
doing is what makes it real.** Nothing in the collection says that Ether exists. The hymn to Ether is sung, and
the singing is the only place the claim lives; the incense is burnt and the material is the link for as long as
it is burning. This is not a weaker form of the same thing. It is a different structural object: a frame whose
truth condition is its performance.

**The blog should hold this against its instincts and not overclaim it.** Hymns can be *sung in bad faith* as
easily as in good, and the collection's own tradition was fully capable of arguing — the same Greek world
produced the Oracles, whose fragments are nothing but assertion. What the Hymns establish is narrower and
sufficient: **argument is not what makes a frame work.** Here is a frame with no argument in it at all, used by
real associations for centuries, and the missing argument is not a defect anyone in the text appears to notice.

---

## 8. The counterweight: a liturgy without a doctrine

The gap in this reading is the mirror of the gap in every other. Those readings carried too much claim: a
system that could be examined, a revelation that could be dated, a doctrine that could be attributed. This one
carries too little to examine, and the honest position has three parts.

**First, what the Hymns say is almost nothing, and what is known about the Hymns is said by the scholars.** The
dating is a guess the scholars mark as one. The place is a mystery with a champion whose theory has "both
sympathizers and critics." The authorship is a fiction the scholars identify as a fiction. The composer is "a
religious antiquary" with a concordance. The cult is inferred from the collection's own addresses. **Every claim
of historical fact in this post belongs to the Introduction and Notes and is attributed there. The hymns
themselves claim nothing — not even that they were written by Orpheus, except by the title of a poem whose
belonging to the collection is itself in question.** A reader who wants the Orphic Hymns to be a document about
something will find that the document is what the book that prints them says about them, and the poems are a
liturgy rather than a record.

**Second, the unverifiability is structural, not accidental.** Ten lines of hymn 1 are an address to a
goddess, and there is no fact of the matter in the text that could be checked. This is different from the
Chaldean Oracles, where the fragments are damaged and the reconstruction contested but the claims still have
truth values a reader could argue about. Here the text's content is *invocation*, and invocation does not bear on
what is the case. The scholars say the poems "have neither plot nor action," and that is another way of saying
that they cannot be right or wrong. **A reading of this collection cannot test its cosmology, because the
collection does not put one forward; it can only describe what the poems do.**

**Third, the safeguard — and there is one, though it is not written into the hymns as an instruction.** The
blog has catalogued what its sources carry: [the Holy Daimon](/the-holy-daimon/) none, [the Holy
Heretics](/the-holy-heretics/) a dose, [Quareia](/quareia/) a clinical triage,
[Fromm](/the-anatomy-of-destructiveness/) a survey, [Black Abbot](/black-abbot-white-magic/) operational
cautions, [the Picatrix](/picatrix/) a designed obstacle. **What the Orphic Hymns carry is a felt hazard handled
by tone and by petition, and the scholars say so as a description of the collection rather than as advice.**
Their note on the collection's concern with madness is the clearest statement of it in the book:

> "Encountering the divine is always a risky endeavor; one can never be certain how one stands in the eyes of
> such beings. Contact with the more-than-human carries with it the danger of losing one's speech, one's senses,
> even one's sanity."[^note72]

And what the hymns do about it is formal, not instructional:

> "This concern is reflected throughout the collection in that many of the hymns either explicitly ask that
> madness be driven away or implicitly do so by requesting the divinity addressed to come with a peaceful
> disposition …"[^note72]

**That is the safeguard, and it is a clause in a prayer.** It appears as "ever with joyous heart"; as "may you come
with kindness / on your joyous face"; as the Dream hymn asking that the power come "in his beneficial, not
harmful, guise."[^note86] The collection's protection is not a rule, a warning, a referral, or a designed
obstacle. It is that the poem asks the power to arrive well-disposed — in "many of the hymns," as the scholars
put it.
**Written into every closing formula is the possibility that the power might arrive otherwise.**

**There is a second protective structure, and it is an absence.** The collection's Orphism carries a real
purificatory regime — the scholars describe it in the Introduction: "For the Orphics, purification from guilt and
pollution was a spiritual necessity"; "The things from which an Orphic had to abstain threatened the purity of
his soul with miasma, pollution"; and abstention from animal flesh follows from the belief in the transmigration
of souls.[^intro] Purity is treated as essential in the notes, where the god and the worshippers are said to
share the quality.[^note30] **And the hymns themselves contain no regime.** They contain epithets of purity —
"pure Persephone," "pure marshal of the gods" — and no rules. That is the same shape as the missing doctrine:
the collection performs the pure address and leaves the discipline to the institution, which is where the
scholars put it.

**The last gap is the one that cannot be closed by reading this book, and it should be stated rather than
managed.** Hymn 87 asks Death for a long life and calls it a "noble prize among men."[^oh87] The scholars
identify the collection's own hopes as ordinary and specific — "wealth, peace, health, and not infrequently for
a blameless end to a good life" — and they locate the whole point, on their reading of the text's own setting, in
something the hymns do not argue: "The initiates wanted to protect their lives and save their souls. And to this
end they needed the good will of every god and every daimon."[^intro] **The salvation is assumed by the
liturgy and never stated as a doctrine anywhere in it.** The blog's model has no place to stand on this. It can
report that a real association performed these poems asking for safety in childbirth, good harvests, a good end,
and the goodwill of "every god and every daimon," and it can report that the scholars infer a doctrine of the
soul's salvation from the wider Orphic literature rather than from the hymns. What it cannot do is check the
claim, because the collection does not make one. **Here the blog's own instrument — the demand that a frame be
examinable — meets a text that has arranged itself to be sung instead, and the honest result is an abstention,
not a verdict.**

**What this reading leaves the blog with is therefore a smaller and stranger object than the last three.**
The Picatrix could be argued with. The Oracles could be dated and their fragments sorted. This is eighty-seven
poems, twelve lines each, addressed to powers, with a substance named at the top of each, made by poets nobody
can name for an association nobody can locate in a city nobody can prove, under the name of a singer the
scholars say may never have existed, and used anyway — for centuries, and then by Florentines making a tarot
deck. **And the poems contain no thesis for the blog to correct, which is itself the correction.**

Hopkins University Press, 2013): the eighty-seven hymns and the unnumbered opening address; the incense line at
the head of every hymn but four, quoted at [^oh1b], [^oh26b] and [^oh87b]; and every verse quotation, checked
character for character and cited by hymn and line. **Attributed to the scholars, not to the hymns** — the dating ("probably
closer to the truth," "a date in the middle of the third century CE is as good a guess as any," the terminus post
quem of about 200 CE read from hymn 34's musical vocabulary); the place and Kern's Pergamon hypothesis; the
refusal of single authorship and the suspicion attaching to the opening address; the *thiasos*, the *mustai*, the
*boukolos*, and the commissioning inference; the identity of the fictitious attribution and what it was for; the
cultivation of epithets as the collection's characteristic device; the description of the poems as having
"neither plot nor action"; and the note that makes the collection's concern with madness explicit. **Inferred, as
this post's** — §1's reading of the absent assertion as a structural feature rather than a gap; §3's reading of
the second-person address as the collection's mode of existence for a cosmology; §4's reading of the incense as a
correspondence that is performed rather than asserted, with the scholars' finding of no systematic organisation
taken as evidence for that reading rather than against it; §6's account of what the blog could not see while
quoting this collection at second hand; §7's correction, that the marker is the claim rather than the seat, and
the accompanying reading of a frame one *does*; and §8's finding that the collection's safeguard is a formula in
a prayer and its discipline an absence. **The gap is real and is not resolved here:** this is a liturgy with no
doctrine, whose historical claims live in its editors' Introduction and not in its poems, whose authority is
inherited from a name those who wrote it did not have, and whose only protection is that its petitions ask the
power to arrive well-disposed. **The cosmology is not endorsed** — nothing here claims the gods, the daimones,
Ether, Protogonos or the correspondence of incense and divinity are real, and nothing here claims they are not —
no one is diagnosed, and nothing here validates, disqualifies or restricts anyone's practice.

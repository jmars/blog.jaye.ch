# Egregores

### Eighth of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the book the blog had already been quoting, read whole for the first time, and found to be arguing the opposite of the case it was borrowed for

*Eighth of the `readings`, and the first whose occasion is the blog's own use of it. [The western
column](/the-western-column/) quoted this book for one thing: a magical order destroyed by the very
structure it was built around, the predation account with the brainwashing line in it. [Ahead of the
story](/ahead-of-the-story/) quoted it for another: the Butler formulation of the shared thought-form,
read as a fact about groups. Both borrowings were accurate, and both took **a worst case and a
mechanism**. Neither post read the book. This one does — and finds that the claim underneath the worst
case is the opposite of a pathology, that the same structure is what a healthy group runs on, and that
the book's countermeasure, which the blog never cited, has a **pace** in it.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source. The quotations are verbatim from the digital text, checked
character for character against the file and cited in the notes by chapter and section; the reading of
them is an **inference**, offered as one. **The book's cosmology is not endorsed** — nothing here claims
that egregores are real entities, and nothing claims they are only metaphor; §2 is about why holding
that open is the point, and it is the book's own move rather than this post's. No living author is
diagnosed, no one's experience is validated or disqualified, and the book's sectarian and political
material is **reported as the book's content and not adopted** (§10). As everywhere: describe
structures, diagnose no one.*

---

## 1. The book, and why the borrowing makes the reading necessary

*Egregores: The Occult Entities That Watch Over Human Destiny* is by Mark Stavish, published by Inner
Traditions in Rochester, Vermont, in 2018 — paperback ISBN 978-1-62055-577-4, e-book
978-1-62055-578-1, LCCN 2017048793.[^egr] It is short. Seven chapters, a foreword by James Wasserman, a conclusion, and two appendices. The second
appendix is a 1929 narrative of a group's attempt to revive the egregore of the Roman Empire; the first —
**Appendix 1**, "Personal Accounts of
Disengaging from an Egregore" — is three first-person accounts by former members of three different
organisations, and that is where the blog's interest has been.[^app2][^app1]

Two corrections to the chapter list the brief carried: "A Modern Example of the War of the Egregores",
"The Power of the Egregore", "Watch Your Dreams" and "Language as a Control Mechanism" are **sections of
chapter 1**, and "The Golden Dawn Egregore" and "Walter Ernest Butler" are **sections of chapter 2** — a
small thing until one notices that the blog's first borrowing is not from a chapter at all.

Both borrowings are quoted in the posts that made them. [The western column](/the-western-column/) §5 has
the predation — the Secret Chiefs "relentlessly demanding of superhuman effort", the recruits whipped in
closets, "People simply burned out", "moderate to total brainwashing", and the closing "you are in charge
of your spiritual path, not spirits, not Chiefs, and not Imperators."[^wc][^app1] All of it is in
**Appendix 1**, in its third account, "Do Not Call Up That Which You Cannot Put Down!".
[Ahead of the story](/ahead-of-the-story/) §8 has the mechanism — "Each member of the group pours energy
into the collective thought-form but, equally, into each member there also passes the influence of the
group as a whole", and the warning that dependence made "mandatory upon all its members" is "treading a
dangerous and slippery path".[^wc][^ch2] That is in **chapter 2**, quoted by Stavish from W. E. Butler's
1970 article "The Egregore of a School". So the blog has had one piece of the book's
worst material and one piece of its mechanism, and has never had its claim.

## 2. The claim, and the bracket

This is an **entity claim**, made by an initiate of the Western esoteric tradition. The blog's discipline
is to describe the frame without adopting it, and that is harder here than usual, because the book is
written to persuade a practitioner and is full of instruction. Stavish gives the definition he treats as
common coin — "autonomous psychic entity composed of and influencing the thoughts of a group of people",
taken, he notes, from Wiktionary — and then a second, older one he prefers: an egregore is also "the home
or conduit for a specific psychic intelligence of a nonhuman nature".[^intro] He brings in Jean Dubuis,
who founded the Philosophers of Nature:

> "What is an egregore? **It is the psychic and astral entity of a group.** All members of a group, a
> family, a club, a political party, a religion, or even a country, psychically included in the egregore
> of the organization to which they belong. . . . Therefore, each individual who is involved in a group
> receives the influences of the egregores, that is the astral counterpart of the group, in his
> psyche."[^intro]

One textual note, reported rather than tidied: the digital text reads "or even a country, psychically
included in the egregore of the organization", with no verb before "psychically", and the clause is quoted
as the file has it.[^addr]

The scale is the point of that list. This is not a claim about cults, or about orders, or about any group
a reader would identify as dangerous. It is a claim about **every** group a person is in, up to and
including the country they live in — and Dubuis's next move is what makes the book interesting rather
than merely sweeping:

> "It is impossible to free oneself from certain egregores. . . . However, we should free ourselves from
> all egregores that are not essential."[^intro]

The target is not egregores. The target is **unnecessary** egregores. The book states its own scope in
three questions, unusually candid about what kind of inquiry this is:

> "What is the impact of an egregore on the individual, his destiny, and free will? Who or what manages
> the collective physical body of the group by way of the invisible? How is the management performed and
> to what ends?"[^intro]

The second and third are questions about **management** — who runs the collective body, and to what end —
and that is why the book is legible to a blog that has spent fifteen posts on how groups hold positions.
Then the bracket, which the book supplies itself:

**The group-mind is not the occult part.** Before the book's claim is weighed, it is worth separating
what in it is ordinary. That a group has a mental life of its own is a mainstream finding, stated in the
secular register for a century and a half. Gustave Le Bon, in 1895: "the fact that they have been
transformed into a crowd puts them in possession of a sort of collective mind which makes them feel,
think, and act in a manner quite different from that in which each individual of them would feel, think,
and act were he in a state of isolation."[^lebon] Durkheim found the same thing where he least expected an
analyst — in religion, whose ideas seem to be "born" precisely "in the midst of these effervescent social
environments and out of this effervescence itself."[^durkheim] So the ordinary claim is common ground: a
group is not a heap of persons.

What the occult register adds is not the collective mind but its **autonomy** — that the groupmind is an
*entity*, with its own existence, that can outlive its members and act on them. That is the claim to hold
open, and it is the whole distance between Le Bon and Stavish. The blog's method applies exactly: the
shared mind is a **structure**, and whether the structure is also a **being** is the part the blog does
not decide.

The book brackets the question itself — "it is functionally irrelevant, except for academic definition,
if an egregore is understood to exist only in the classical sense or if we can consider a thoughtform an
egregore"[^concl] — and this post holds that bracket open from both sides, as [the undreaming
wetiko](/undreaming-wetiko/) reading did. Nothing here claims an egregore is a being; nothing claims it is
a figure of speech for a shared mood. Where the book claims a **being**, this post says so and stops.

## 3. The word, which does not mean what the book says it means

**The book has a term with a history, and the history has the shape the blog keeps finding: an old name
carrying a new claim.** This is a small philological point with a large consequence, and it is the kind of check
the blog asks of every frame — what does the authority claim, and does it survive contact with the source?

Read Éliphas Lévi, the writer the modern usage is traced to, and his *Eggrégore* is a biblical giant:

> "Ces forces colossales ont parfois pris une figure et se sont présentées sous l'apparence de géants : Ce sont
> les Eggrégores du livre d'Hénoch ; créatures terribles pour qui nous sommes ce que sont pour nous les
> infusoires ou les insectes microscopiques qui pullulent entre nos dents et sur notre épiderme."[^levi]

> "Les véritables eggrégores, c'est-à-dire **les veilleurs de nuit**, auxquels nous aimons à croire, ce sont les
> astres du ciel avec leurs yeux toujours étincelants."[^levi]

The Watchers — *egrēgoroi*, the wakeful ones, the angels of the Book of Enoch — which is what the Greek word
means and where it actually lives. And Lévi, at the end of the passage, rejects *that* doctrine outright:

> "C'est pourquoi nous rejetons définitivement et absolument la mythologie des eggrégores."[^levi]

So far the word means *giants*, and the man using it disbelieves them. But it is the same Lévi, on René
Guénon's account, who gave the word its modern sense — and Guénon names him for it, in a passage the modern
usage never quotes, because it is the indictment of that usage:

> "Tout d'abord, nous devons faire remarquer que **nous n'avons jamais employé le mot « égrégore » pour désigner
> ce qu'on peut appeler proprement une « entité collective »** ; et la raison en est que, dans cette acception,
> c'est là un terme qui n'a rien de traditionnel et qui ne représente qu'une des nombreuses fantaisies du
> moderne langage occultiste. **Le premier qui l'ait employé ainsi est Éliphas Lévi**, et, si nos souvenirs sont
> exacts, c'est même lui qui, pour justifier ce sens, en a donné une étymologie latine invraisemblable, le
> faisant dériver de *grex*, « troupeau », alors que ce mot est purement grec et n'a jamais signifié en réalité
> autre chose que « veilleur »."[^guenon]

That is the whole of it, and it is exact. The **collective-entity sense — the sense this book depends on — is
Lévi's coinage**, not a tradition's; the **etymology he gave for it was false** (*grex*, a herd, where the word
is *egrēgoroi*, the watchers); and a scholar of the tradition rejected the sense as "one of the many fancies of
modern occultist language". The ancient name is real; the thing the name is now used for is a hundred and fifty
years old and was attached to the word by a man who got its root wrong.

**What that does to this reading, and what it does not.** It does not make Stavish's *phenomenon* wrong; a
group can share a mind whether or not the word for it is old. It makes the **claim of lineage** wrong — and
that is the blog's own subject arriving inside a single word. A **borrowed pedigree** is exactly the
"pre-certified frame" the blog names as a lever: the warrant issued by a host, before anyone checks the
issuer. The egregore's name is a warrant of that kind. The thing itself, if it exists, is new; the name it is
given is ancient; and the gap between them is where the authority is smuggled. Which is why the blog's
method applies here with unusual force: **read what the text says, not what its name implies.**

## 4. The neutrality — the centre the blog missed

Here is the finding of this reading, in one sentence. **The book's egregore is not a pathology.** It is
ordinary equipment, and the book says so in its most quoted definition, in the foreword, in the chapter
that is not quoted at all, and in its conclusion. The clearest statement is Butler's, quoted by Stavish in
chapter 2 as the standard British occult presentation of the idea:

> "the fundamental nature of the egregore consists of collective emotions and **that the thoughtform itself
> is amoral, taking its directions from those connected to it**."[^ch2]

Amoral is doing real work there. A group's shared mind is not good and it is not evil and it is not ill;
it is **directional**, and the direction comes from the members. Butler's expansion makes the loop
explicit: "Each member of the group pours energy into the collective thought-form but, equally, into each
member there also passes the influence of the group as a whole."[^ch2] Nothing in the structure chooses,
and everything in it has a bias: towards more of itself. Then Butler does the thing that makes this section
necessary, and it is the passage the blog has never quoted:

> "where the mental and psychic conditions justify such domination by the collective Group mind for a
> limited period, **such dependence upon the Egregore may even be remedial**. But any organization, which
> makes such dependence mandatory upon all its members, is treading a dangerous and slippery path and
> will, sooner or later, come to grief."[^ch2]

*Remedial.* Butler — "among one of the most important, and often little-known, occultists of the
twentieth century", in Stavish's words — writes that dependence on a group mind can be a **treatment**, for
a limited period, in the right conditions. What he forbids is not dependence but dependence made
**mandatory**.

The foreword says the same at the door of the book: "I believe egregores are both positive and negative.
**They can inspire or enslave.**"[^fore] And chapter 7, whose subject is escape, opens by conceding that
escape is mostly not the point — "So can egregores be avoided? **Yes, but only if human beings can be
avoided**"[^ch7] — and locates their origin in the ordinary pressure of association rather than in
anyone's design: they are "born out of necessity—one might even say accidentally", and mature "not unlike
other living organisms".[^ch7] What that looks like in the book's own examples is deliberately
unglamorous. Chapter 7 adds the school alumni association, "one of the stranger manifestations of a group
mind and also one of the most common"; chapter 1 adds the family egregore, where "families who achieve
renown or are even feared over several generations do so in part because of the egregore they have built
up around themselves".[^ch7][^ch1] The predation the blog borrowed is in this book. It is not what the
book is about.

Against [the environment](/the-environment/), which asked what a predator does **not** have to build
because the surrounding culture has already built it, the egregore is an unusually complete answer: a
shared language with ready-made definitions, an authority who can speak for the whole, and a membership
already habituated to taking its direction from the group. Against
[the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/), which found that a frame which "names an endpoint and makes the
connection operable" thereby has **a seat**, the egregore is a different kind of thing. A seat is a
**place**, at an endpoint, that a person occupies; every case study in this series has been a seat being
taken. An egregore has no endpoint — Butler's sentence is the proof, since it "takes its directions from
those connected to it" and so has no direction of its own to hand anyone. It is closer to [the
environment](/the-environment/) than to a seat: a **shared position no one occupies**, which is why it can
be amoral and remedial at once.

But in every account of harm in this book, a **seat appears inside the egregore**, and in the predation
account the fatal feature is stated in one clause by the writer: the Secret Chiefs were
"so-called because (1) they were the guiding forces legitimizing the school's existence and (2) **only the
Chief had contact with them**", and they "were aspects of the unconscious of the Chief, **whether or not
they had any objective existence**".[^app1] The egregore is the medium. The harm arrives when a single
person becomes its **reader** — the one who says what the group mind wants — and when that reading cannot
be checked. That is [the label post](/the-label/)'s rule arriving from the book's side: what a group with
an egregore does is not the question; whether a seat inside it has closed is.

## 5. Tulpa and tulku: the mechanism, and where it came from

The book's account of how a group mind becomes autonomous is a Western borrowing from Tibetan material,
and the book is explicit about the conduit. It is Alexandra David-Néel — French orientalist, Buddhist,
traveller — through whom "the idea of the tulpa —an animated thoughtform that takes on autonomous
existence—entered the vocabulary of Western esotericism".[^ch1] The claim, quoted by Stavish from *Magic
and Mystery in Tibet*:

> "Once the tulpa is endowed with enough vitality to be capable of playing the part of a real being, **it
> tends to free itself from its maker's control**. This, say Tibetan occultists, happens **nearly
> mechanically**, just as the child, when his body is completed and able to live apart, leaves its
> mother's womb."[^ch1]

The provenance is careful hearsay: David-Néel attributing a mechanism to "Tibetan occultists" on the
strength of training received in Sikkim, in a tradition she was also a reformer of. And the piece most
retellings drop is that she did not accept her own evidence — "I may have created my own
hallucination"[^ch1] — though others, she reported, saw the monk she had built.

The distinction between **tulpa** and **tulku** is the structural difference the whole book turns on: a
tulpa is "more or less ephemeral", while a tulku "is the incarnation of a lasting energy directed by an
individual with the object of continuing a given kind of activity after his death".[^ch1] The object of a
group's shared mind is not a good mood or a shared vocabulary. It is **activity continued past the person
who started it**. And the tradition, in David-Néel's record, knows what that costs. A lama's answer to her scepticism turns
her suggestion that the visions were self-generated into the book's best image of the mechanism — cut a
channel between a river and your house, dig a pond, and "fishes will come from the river" — and then into
its warning: "**It is only prudent to beware of opening channels without due consideration.**"[^ch1] The
line that gives the frame its price is in the same passage: "**One must know how to protect oneself
against the tigers to which one has given birth, as well as against those that have been begotten by
others.**"[^ch1] A group-made mind can be dangerous; the danger is stated by the tradition that says it is
also ordinary.

Then the book takes a **live** dispute inside Tibetan Buddhism — the worship of the protector deity Dorje
Shugden, banned by the Dalai Lama — and reads it as a war of egregores:

> "In Dorje Shugden we see a sort of **apotheosis, or divinization of the human being**—the source of the
> angry ghost often said to have been a condemned prisoner—who is then **employed to protect the sect and
> its adherents**."[^ch1]

Two counterweights belong in the body here. This is a **living tradition's internal dispute**, read by a
Western practitioner through a Western frame; that the frame reads every factional disagreement as a war
of group-minds is the frame's choice, not a finding about who is right in Tibet. And the book reports,
without flinching, that "some have seen the Chinese occupation and destruction of Tibet as punishment
for—or at least as a result of—the inadequate worship of Dorje Shugden".[^ch1] A frame that can assign a
national catastrophe to a deity's unappeased devotees has taken on a debt, and the debt is not paid in the
book.

## 6. Language as a control mechanism

The section of chapter 1 with that title is the purest blog territory in the book. Its argument is that
a tradition's **self-description** is part of how its egregore is maintained — that the frame is carried
in the wording, and that a membership taught to define itself only in its own terms has handed the
definition to the structure:

> "each school defines itself. Any comparison to anything other than Tibetan Buddhism, or Buddhism in
> general—as defined by the lamas—is rarely done and actively discouraged. Therefore, **the discussion is
> controlled, definitions are controlled, and, with this, the overarching egregore of the sect or school
> is maintained.**"[^ch1]

Stavish's illustration is a claim about his own readers, and a good one because it is falsifiable in a way
the rest of the chapter is not: few students of Tibetan Buddhism, he writes, will ever read his monograph,
because "though claiming to want enlightenment and the freedom it mandates, they have simply **traded one
egregore for another**."[^ch1] Read that against [the costume](/the-costume/): the state is a state, and
the frame decides what it is for — including the frame a person takes up when they leave one group for
another. Chapter 7 extends the argument to the environment, where "the purpose of media is, on its most
fundamental level, **to create an egregore**", and the media are "**extensions of various egregores**" to
be limited consciously.[^ch7] The clearest version of the mechanism is in chapter 7's closing section:

> "Controlling the narrative means to be in control of the story, of the story you want people to hear,
> believe, and act upon—**all three parts must be present**. It is not sufficient that they hear it; they
> must also believe it, and from that position of belief act in the manner that the narrative is
> directing them to."[^ch7]

Hear, believe, act. That is [the mechanism post](/meditation-harm/)'s install-the-frame step in the idiom
of a communications department, and it is a real addition to the blog's vocabulary, because it names the
**third** clause as the operative one: a narrative heard and believed but not acted on has not taken, and
the test of a frame is behaviour.

The counterweight goes here rather than at the end. **As stated, this argument cannot fail.** Any
tradition that maintains its own definitions exhibits the mechanism; any tradition that does not also
exhibits something, by neglect. A frame that explains both the control and the absence of control is not
making a prediction, and the blog's own rule applies — an instrument that cannot return "undecided" will
return something else. The salvageable version is narrower: the **definition** of the group's terms is a
resource, someone holds it, and who holds it is checkable.

## 7. Fiction into structure

The book's most testable claim, and this reading's most useful new material, is that a fiction can create
a real group-mind. It makes that claim three times, with three different degrees of evidence.

**AMORC, and a documented mechanism.** In 1930 the Ancient Mystical Order Rosae Crucis sent five
confidential manifestos to long-standing members — they "were to be read, signed, and returned" —
announcing an international council of Adepts whose list of signatories Stavish judges doubtful: "it is
doubtful that the mentioned 'signatories' existed in any uniform manner; if they did exist they were
probably more honorary than active."[^ch6] What he says they **did** do is the chapter's finding:

> "This document, and ones similar to it, are in fact **magical operations**. Here the semihistorical and
> mythological Rosicrucian fraternity, a worldwide body of Adepts, is affirmed, listed, and again
> announced to all the world… **That which is fiction slowly materializes into fact; the dream becomes
> reality, and history is justified.**"[^ch6]

That is a claim about a **ritual of assertion**: a group stating its own charter as fact, to its most
committed members, under signature, as a way of making the fact load-bearing — the same move [the
certified frame](/the-certified-frame/) documented in the secular register. The averment those members had
to sign, with the hour and date recorded, is why Stavish calls it a magical operation at all: a ritual
text with a **witnessed return**.[^ch6]

**Lovecraft, and a tradition of contrivance.** Chapter 5 argues that the *Necronomicon*'s fictionality is
not unusual in the tradition it entered: "a close examination shows that **nearly all of the famous
magical texts in Western occultism were in some way contrived**."[^ch5] It supports that with the
Solomonic corpora and the Padmasambhava "termas" — the same evidentiary move [Star.Ships](/star-ships/)
had to make — and then gives the mechanism by which a fiction becomes usable: the book is linked through
various fictions "to both historical people and locations as well as fictional ones, **thus bending the
framework of reality for the reader**".[^ch5] That is a frame producing its own evidentiary texture — the
same operation [the machine said so](/the-machine-said-so/) catalogued.

**Slender Man, and no evidence at all.** Chapter 4's account of the fictional internet figure is where the
claim goes from structural to strong, and it is the book's weakest link, so it gets quoted whole:

> "By using the magical method of the egregore, 'what I cannot do alone, many others can do for me,' and
> harnessing the psycho-sexual-emotional energy of millions of preadolescent and adolescent youths, a
> veritable **buffet of psychic energy** was made available for the taking."[^ch4]

This is **assertion**, and the book does not pretend otherwise. There is no measurement, no case, no
prediction, no control; there is a figure whose fictional elaboration was followed by real violence, and a
mechanism asserted to connect them. Reported as what it is — the book's testable claim, presented in its
least testable form — it is also the clearest example of the frame's reach, which is the subject of §10.

One more, and it is the hardest thing in the book to leave alone. Chapter 6's section on the "Third Mind",
named after Napoleon Hill and after Burroughs and Gysin, ends with Stavish attributing the death of Joan
Vollmer, shot by Burroughs in Mexico in 1951, to the structure the two of them had built: the shooting
"cannot be seen as an 'accident' in the true sense of the word… In short, **the magical being they had
engendered was taking its toll.**"[^ch6] That is the frame doing what frames do, and it is also the frame's
cost, stated plainly and not adopted here: a man fired the shot, and a reading that relocates the act into
a shared mind has removed the person who did it.

## 8. The countermeasure: what the book actually prescribes

This is the reading's second occasion. [The western column](/the-western-column/) used this book for the
diagnosis and never for the prescription; it quoted one closing imperative — "you are in charge of your
spiritual path" — and treated that as the book's countermeasure. It is a countermeasure, and it is one
sentence. **Chapter 7 is a procedure**, and the blog has never cited it.

**First, the honest scale of the problem.** Extraction is compared, without hedging, to leaving a
mind-control cult: it is "the same thing as demonstrated in **deprogramming from destructive mind-control
cults (DMCC)**".[^ch7] And then the sentence that keeps the comparison from becoming an accusation, which
is the most careful line in the book and the one this blog most needed:

> "It is important to note that **all egregores are cults in the classical sense of the word, but not all
> are cults in the modern pejorative sense.**"[^ch7]

*Cult* in the classical sense is cultus — a body of worship and practice shared by a group. Read with
Butler's "amoral" and "remedial" in hand, that sentence says every human association is a cultus and that
calling one a cult in the modern sense is a separate, later judgement. A reading that stopped at the DMCC
comparison would have endorsed an accusation the book refuses.

**Second, the support requirement** — the prescription's one outside check, easy to miss because it is a
subordinate clause:

> "the amount of emotional energy, social dynamics, shared goals and values, and 'family' identification
> and structure all combine to make it emotionally painful and difficult to extract oneself—**by oneself,
> without some form of support**—from an egregore."[^ch7]

The extraction is **not prescribed as a solo act**; the remedy assumes a second person.

**Third, the pace — and it is a real one, with numbers.** The procedure has a box, a duration, and a rate:

> "Place everything associated with your identified egregore in a box. Place the box out of sight in your
> closet or give it to someone to hold for the duration of this practice: **one month minimum, and six
> weeks ideally.**"[^ch7]

with the expected consequence named in advance — "a period of mourning is to be expected" — and a
benchmark that is arithmetic rather than inspirational:

> "the entire process of extraction, a comprehensive understanding of the related events, and the
> realization of a new direction **may take up to two years**. It is easy to say that **for every year
> one is involved with the egregore it can take a month for the process to work itself out.**"[^ch7]

The book flags its own number as "just a general figure", which is the correct register. But a **rate** is
what it is: one part recovery to twelve parts involvement, with a floor of one month and a ceiling of two
years. [The safeguards](/safeguards/) and [the western column](/the-western-column/) both found a metered
pace in their traditions, usually as a warning to go slowly. Here the pace is in the remedy, and it is
quantified.

**Fourth, the remedy has an explicit no-outcome.** The procedure ends by declining to conclude: "You may
find a way to balance the two, **or you may choose to go back**… whatever it is that you do it will be
from a position of **individual choice, self-expression, and freedom**."[^ch7] Three permitted endings,
one of which is return, and the test is not the outcome but whether the choice was made from inside or
outside — a safeguard no programme that wants an exit can manufacture.

**Fifth, the checking work done before the decision.** Chapter 7's instrument is a comparison between
promise and delivery: know what you joined for — "it is critical that we know what our goals are to begin
with" — and use that "as a beneficial checklist", because **"knowing what it is we desire to accomplish
and comparing what is being promised and what is really being delivered"** is what keeps a person moving
toward their own goals.[^ch7] That is [the label post](/the-label/)'s check, written for the member rather
than for the observer.

**Sixth, the remedy for the frame itself, with a manner-rule.** The chapter's named countermeasure is
"therapeutic blasphemy", and Stavish is careful about its provenance: the term is Joscelyn Godwin's, in
*The Theosophical Enlightenment*, borrowed from Urgyen Sangharakshita, and its content is the public and
deliberate desacralisation of the tradition one was raised in — "**we are to free ourselves publicly and
consciously from private and unconscious indoctrination.**"[^ch7] And then the rule that makes it a
therapy rather than an act:

> "If, however, it is done disrespectfully—out of cynicism, pride, or without appreciation for what
> positive lessons may have been learned—then **desacralization, or therapeutic blasphemy, loses its
> therapeutic component and simply becomes blasphemy.**"[^ch7]

A remedy with a condition on **how** it is done is a remedy with a safeguard inside it — and the
condition is about the state of the person doing it, not about the object.

**Seventh, the rule about material anchors.** The destructive section is unambiguous: "the objects
connected with the organization or movement must be destroyed. **Fire is the principal means of separating
the energetic link and destroying it.**"[^ch7] A letter Stavish prints from an anonymous practitioner does it at the scale of a person's own temple:
tools defaced with an ice pick on a Tuesday, "the day of Mars", each with a spoken thanks, the sword
hammered and bent, all "consigned to water and fire to eliminate any residual etheric links".[^ch7]
Whether or not one accepts the metaphysics, the structure is recognisable: a **rite of separation** with a
witness, a date, and a specific inventory, rather than a decision taken silently. The counterweight is in
the same section: the book's own examples of destroying an egregore's anchors are the French
revolutionaries of 1789, the Soviets in Germany in 1945, the Chinese Communists in Tibet in 1959, and "the
destruction of monuments, texts, and places of worship by various Islamic fundamentalist paramilitary
forces".[^ch7] The remedy and the iconoclasm are the same description; what separates them is who is
burning whose objects.

**Eighth, the standing defence.** Quoting Mouni Sadhu, the book sets out three points against coming under
a foreign influence: "**DO NOT SLEEP in the sense of being passive or distracted**", pray, keep defined
forms in consciousness, exercise the body.[^refuge] The second is the interesting one, because its content is
a **choice of association** rather than an avoidance: "**Join a certain good Egregor, which corresponds
to your contemplation, so that you will not be entangled with the Chain of a foreign, evil
Egregor.**"[^refuge] The remedy is not none. It is a better one.

**Ninth, and last, the outside test.** Chapter 2 carries the best check in the whole book, quoted by
Stavish from Butler and attributed by Butler to Dion Fortune:

> "the best way of judging the spiritual health of any organization was not to take its public teachings
> and statements as proof of its integrity but rather to **look closely at the type of person its
> discipline turned out**. . . . Here is the ancient touchstone we can always apply for it is eternally
> true: '**By their fruits shall ye know them.**'"[^ch2]

That is an outside check in the exact sense this series means: it cannot be performed from inside the
group's own terms, it looks at people rather than at claims, and it is checkable by anyone. Set beside the
"you are in charge of your spiritual path" line the blog already had, it is the stronger instrument — the
first tells the practitioner to trust their own authority, the second to look at the output.

So the answer to this reading's second question is: **yes, the book supplies a real countermeasure.** A
support requirement, a quantified pace, a pre-commitment checklist, a manner-rule on the remedy, a
material deposit with a date, a standing three-point practice, an outside test that reads people rather
than claims, and an explicit permission to go back. [The western column](/the-western-column/) cited none
of it. One more line is worth keeping, because it is [the machine has no
reading](/the-machine-has-no-reading/)'s claim from the other side: "a demon perceived, i.e., on whom the
light of consciousness is thrown, is already a demon rendered impotent."[^ch7]

## 9. The negative finding

**Stated as a search result, scoped to the file.** The digital text of *Egregores* contains **no clinical
referral**. Case-insensitive searches return **0** hits for "safeguard", "physician", "therapist",
"therapis", "psychiatry", "psychosis", "counsel" and "supervis". Everything clinical in the file is
incidental, and it is itemised at [^gap]: **one** hit for "psychiatr", in an appendix account, where a
Chief's "psychiatric medication kept him a bit more stable"; **three** hits for "doctor", all inside a
quoted 1920s dialogue by the French mystic Paul Sédir; and **one** hit for "clinic", inside "severe
clinical depression" as a reported symptom among historians of witchcraft. **No hit is a referral**, there
is no instruction anywhere in the book to take a state, a symptom or a crisis to anyone outside the
practice, and the word "safeguard" does not occur. A search shows only what it indexes, so the claim is
scoped: within this text, the referral is absent.

This matters more here than in the other readings, for two reasons. **First, because of what this book
contains.** It is the blog's most explicit Western account of groups that **damage people** — members
whipped in closets, a leader who "had the discipline and ethics of an eight-yearold child", a school
destroyed, a Chief whose involvement with the occult began "through a complete psychotic break" after
which "he was cataleptic for about a month and was briefly institutionalized."[^app1] In the second
appendix account, a man in the aftermath of a grand master's death reports chest pain he considered severe
enough for an emergency room.[^app1] The material is this close to a clinical boundary. The book never
names one.

**Second, because of the contrast inside the blog.** [The western column](/the-western-column/) §4
singled out the clinical referral as "the single most striking passage in the Western material" — and
credited it to Josephine McCarthy's Quareia curriculum: "Clean yourself up magically, and **if you are
still hearing them, go see a doctor.**"[^wc] The book it used for the predation account of the West
supplies no route from a casualty to a clinician, and does not distinguish between a problem the practice
can address and a problem it cannot. And the pace in §7 is not a substitute for the check: a **rate**
tells a person to be patient, and cannot return the verdict this book's own material most obviously
invites — that some of what the accounts describe is illness, that illness has a treatment, and that the
treatment is not in the book. [The differential](/the-differential/) drew that boundary. This tradition
wrote a whole chapter on extraction and did not write the sentence.

## 10. The correction the blog owes

Two corrections, and they are the same correction seen from two ends.

**One: the blog took the book's marked-as-dramatic appendix and left its thesis.** The quotation [the
western column](/the-western-column/) §5 uses — the closets, the whipping, the burnout, the brainwashing,
the spirits-and-Chiefs line — is in **Appendix 1**, and the appendix has a preamble that changes what the
material is. In full:

> "It is very clear that **many people have positive experiences with various organizations and their
> attendant egregores.** However, at some point this may change. For the sake of comparison we have
> provided several examples of individuals who extracted themselves from three very different spiritual
> organizations. **These examples are dramatic and have been chosen for this very quality.** It is up to
> each reader to compare the statements of the following people with their own personal experiences to
> see if there is anything that may be of value to them."[^app1]

The book says, in its own voice, that the accounts the blog used are **selected for being dramatic**, and
that "many people have positive experiences". Those three accounts — a former member of a Tibetan Buddhist
organisation, a former member of a worldwide initiatic fraternity, and the writer of the Secret Chiefs
account — are introduced as examples, "for the sake of comparison", with each writer supplying their own
recollection. The blog's note to that material describes it as "the account of the order"; the passage
is a contributed first-person account in an appendix, and the writer of it is not named.[^wc][^app1]

Set that beside the book's thesis, and the flattening is exact. The book's claim is that a group's shared
mind is **amoral**, **born of necessity, one might even say accidentally**, **possibly remedial**, able to
**inspire or enslave**, and that **there are egregores that are healthy for some people** — "one need only
look at the number of people who overcome addictions, health concerns, or emotional distress by
interacting with healthy support groups."[^ch7] The blog took the three accounts the book flags as its
dramatic cases, and left the ordinary case the book spends six chapters on. That is the same failure of
reading [Star.Ships](/star-ships/) caught in [the deep inheritance](/the-deep-inheritance/) — a book's
*strongest available* figure taken as the book's frame, when the book's own text is weaker and better.
It happened here to the blog's own most careful post about the West.

**Two: the blog took the diagnosis and left the prescription.** [The western column](/the-western-column/)
§5 closes on the "you are in charge of your spiritual path, not spirits, not Chiefs, and not Imperators"
line and calls it "the countermeasure". It is a countermeasure, and it is a **verdict** — an assertion of
authority against the structure. The book's **procedure** is chapter 7, which that post does not cite: the
support requirement, the box, the one-month floor and the six-week ideal, the one-month-per-year
benchmark, the two-year ceiling, the promise-and-delivery checklist, the manner-rule on therapeutic
blasphemy, the Tuesday destruction of the tools, the three-point standing defence, and Fortune's fruits
test. That is some twenty pages of programme, and it is the thing this series was arguing for when it said
the Western column writes safeguards **into the training**. The blog found the sentence. It missed the
chapter.

Both corrections run the same way as the reading's central finding: **the book is not a predation case
with a warning attached. It is a treatment of an ordinary structure that sometimes eats its host, and it
prescribes, at length, what to do when it does.** The blog published the worst page and the closing
sentence. Three things follow. **One** — the frame inventory gains a third kind of object: [a cosmos of
persons](/a-cosmos-of-persons/) has a reading and no seat, [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) has a
seat, and the book supplies a **shared position nobody occupies** — [the environment
post](/the-environment/) given a name and a metabolism, with the harm appearing inside it as an
**appointment** rather than as a property of the thing itself. **Two** — the blog's argument about the
ordinary case acquires its opposite from the same source: a tradition's shared mind is what a tradition
runs on, and the disaster is the structure with a closed reader, not the structure. **Three** — the
positive programme gains a **rate**, which the blog has never had. The book's own conclusion offers the
sentence the whole argument rests on, reported here without adopting it:

> "there is no such thing as **good or evil, only ideas and their consequences**. . . . Recognizing this
> reality is the first step in defeating it."[^concl]

Frames are not ranked by their content, and the question that has an answer is what a frame does and who
can check it. This blog will not say there is no good or evil. It will say what it has said from the
beginning, and what this book, read whole, supports — **a group-made mind is ordinary, it is directional,
and the harm is in the seat**. The Western tradition that knew the most about it wrote down both the
disaster and the treatment, in one book, with the treatment longer, and this is the first time the blog
has read them together.

## 11. Counterweights, in the body

**The 80 per cent, without a denominator.** The second appendix account reports that "**upward of 80
percent of the officers of the order died before reaching the age of sixty-five, and from unusual and
debilitating blood diseases**", and that with the general membership the figure was "still a staggering 20
percent".[^app1] There is no denominator, no comparison population, no base rate for officers of small
esoteric orders, and no control — and the reporter is a member with an interest in the explanation he goes
on to give. A residual the same size as the scan step is measuring the scan. Reported as what it is: one
person's recollection of a small group, offered as evidence of a shared mind's toll.

**And the same account gives itself away, which is worth more than the statistic.** After the list of
everyone else's misfortunes — a lost job, a transferred post, blood cancers, an embezzlement charge — the
writer explains his own survival naturally and the others' suffering structurally: "I have been spared
through no grace or skill of my own. **I attribute it to constant prayer, a loving wife who steered me
back to sane choices, and the love and grace of God.**"[^app1] The frame receives a mundane cause for the
self and a supernatural one for the others, in the same paragraph, by the same hand. That asymmetry is the
most useful thing in the appendix, and it is a reason to read the accounts as testimony rather than as
data.

**The frame's reach.** The book's own sentence is the sharpest statement of the problem: "So can egregores
be avoided? Yes, but only if human beings can be avoided."[^ch7] A frame that covers every association
between persons — a family, a club, a nation, a sports team, a news network, a marriage — does not
discriminate between a group mind that is load-bearing and one that is eating someone. Butler's "amoral"
carries the same load from the other direction: if the structure takes its direction from the members,
then "the egregore did it" describes no mechanism at all until someone names the reader. What makes the
book usable is exactly the part this post has kept: the seat inside the structure, and the test that reads
people rather than claims.

**The book's political material, which the blog reports and does not adopt.** The foreword extends the
frame further than the chapters do, and one passage of it is political invective by any standard: it names
the *New York Times*'s motto as "a lie whose object is incitement", describes Berkeley protestors as
"leftist-inspired censorship storm troopers and jackbooted, mask-wearing street gangs", and characterises
the six-pointed star and its doctrines as "another resilient egregore that could well be improved
upon".[^fore] Chapter 1 also relays, without endorsing, the claim that the destruction of Tibet was a
consequence of inadequate worship of a protector deity.[^ch1] The blog quotes the book's material; it does
not adopt its politics and does not repeat its invective, and this paragraph is here so that a reader who
takes the book up knows what is in it.

**And the remedy's own presupposition.** The book's countermeasure is addressed to someone who accepts its
cosmology: "join a certain good Egregor" is not an instruction this blog can give anyone, and the rite of
destruction is described in terms — "the energetic link", "residual etheric links" — that the blog
brackets. But the **shape** of the prescription survives the bracketing: a support requirement, a rate, a
checklist, a manner-rule, a documented separation with a date, and an explicit permission to return. Those
are the parts a reader who does not accept the metaphysics can use, and the parts the blog should have had all along.

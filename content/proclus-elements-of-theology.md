# Proclus, The Elements of Theology

### Twenty-first of the `readings`, one book at its own scale — the last reading's frame put into proof, two hundred and eleven propositions from a first principle to a soul that comes down whole, and a system with nothing in it that performs anything

*Twenty-first of the `readings`, and the fourth case of the same system. [The Orphic
Hymns](/the-orphic-hymns/) perform a cosmology and assert nothing. [The Chaldean
Oracles](/the-chaldean-oracles/) assert one and station no one. [Proclus' Theology of
Plato](/proclus-theology-of-plato/) argues one at length, in prose, in a ranked hierarchy of
mediation, and leaves an office at the top of it. This is the same frame in its last available
form: **not argued, proved.** Two hundred and eleven propositions, each stated once and then
demonstrated out of the propositions before it, from a first principle that is not being to a
last proposition about what happens to a soul when it comes down. Nothing in this series has
claimed so much, nothing in this series has shown so much of its working, and nothing in this
series has offered so little to do.*

*Status.* This post reads **two** editions of one work, and names the edition each time. The
primary text is Thomas Taylor's 1816 English translation of *Proclus' Elements of Theology*,
printed in the same volume as his translation of the *Theology of Plato* and the treatises
that follow it; the second is E. R. Dodds' *Proclus: The Elements of Theology*, a revised text
with translation, introduction and commentary (Oxford, Clarendon, 1963), Greek and English
facing. **Taylor's translation is cited by Proposition and roman numeral** as his volume
prints them — "Proposition VII", "Proposition CCXI" — and **Dodds' translation as "Prop." with
an arabic numeral** — "Prop. 7", "Prop. 211" — with Dodds' Introduction cited by its section.
The two numberings are the same work's two hundred and eleven propositions, and are never the
chapters of the *Theology of Plato*; the sibling reading of that work is a different text in a
different part of a different edition. Every quotation below is checked character for character
against a copy of the edition named, and every one carries its Proposition address. Because
both editions are nineteenth- and twentieth-century printings reproduced by machine reading,
each does damage to its own text; **nothing is repaired, and nothing is spliced in from
anywhere else.** Taylor's line-break hyphenation is undone and its spacing collapsed where a
quotation crosses a line; its typographic quotation marks are rendered here as straight ASCII,
and its curly apostrophes, dashes and ellipses are normalised. Dodds' are treated the same
way. Where either printing is corrupt, the quotation stops rather than being mended, and where a
sentence is damaged partway through, only its clean part is quoted.
Taylor's Greek and his Latin notes are not quoted, and neither is Dodds' Greek, Latin or
French. Elisions are marked with a bare ellipsis. The reading of this material through the
blog's model is an **inference**, offered as one. **Nothing here claims the One exists and
nothing here claims it does not; nothing here claims the henads, the gods, the orders of
intelligence, or the descent of the soul are real, and nothing here claims they are not.**
Proclus' claims are reported as his, Taylor's and Dodds' as theirs. No living or dead person is
diagnosed. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no one.

## 1. What kind of book this is

Where the *Theology of Plato* argues, discursively and at length, that reality is a graded
hierarchy of mediation, the *Elements* takes the same hierarchy and **derives** it: two hundred
and eleven propositions, each with an enunciation and a demonstration, in the order the
demonstration requires rather than the order a reader might want. This reading begins inside the
last one's frame, because this is the other half of the same system, and the half the series had
only ever seen from outside.

Dodds opens his study of the book by saying what makes it worth reading at all, and his
justification is a fair statement of the object: he credits the *Elements* with being "the one
genuinely systematic exposition of Neoplatonic metaphysic which has come down to us."[^i1] The
*Enneads* are occasional pieces written for a school that already shared the doctrine; the
manuals of Sallustius and Porphyry are for a general readership; neither is a structure. The
*Elements* is the structure, stated as a structure, and the only one of its kind to survive.
Dodds says elsewhere that it is "a purely academic and theoretical work, containing
little or nothing that appears to be directed either to spiritual edification" — which is not a
compliment, and which is also the finding this post will spend its length on.[^i1]

The book's form is the thing to describe first, because the form is the claim. Dodds describes
it as a method of pure a priori deduction, "known to the ancient mathematicians as synthesis and
familiar to us from Euclid and Spinoza", and calls it "the Platonic method of
hypothesis".[^i1] Proclus, he adds, found a model for it in the hypothetical
argumentation put into Parmenides' mouth in the dialogue of that name — where the first
hypothesis denies everything of the one and the later ones unfold what follows. So the
*Elements* is not a treatise that happens to be arranged as theorems. It is an attempt to put a
whole metaphysics in the form of a demonstration, so that its authority rests on the derivation
rather than on the teacher.

That attempt has a price and Dodds names it at once. "Ingenious as Proclus is, too often his
'demonstration', though formally correct, in fact merely repeats the 'enunciation' at greater
length" — that is the reader's complaint about a proof-form in the hands of a schoolman.[^i1]
But the form also buys something. Because each proposition is stated before it is proved, a
reader can locate every step; because the steps run in one direction, a reader can name the
point at which the system is not derived from anything. A rite does not offer that. A revealed
text does not offer that. A discursive argument offers it only intermittently, because a
discursive argument can change the subject. **A chain of two hundred and eleven propositions
cannot change the subject, and that is the first thing the form does for a reader: it makes the
system's weakest joint findable.**

The two editions make the findability a comparison. Taylor's 1816 volume prints the *Elements* among
the works that follow the *Theology of Plato* and begins immediately with the first proposition: no
preface, no statement of
method, no editorial note. The translation runs each enunciation and its proof together into one
continuous archaic paragraph, so that the scaffolding is visible only in the recurring connectives
with which each proof opens. Dodds' 1963 edition does the opposite: it prints the Greek with an
apparatus, faces it with a translation, and adds the two things Taylor's volume does not have —
an Introduction that places the work among Proclus' other writings and reconstructs the text's
history, and a section-by-section Commentary. **That asymmetry is the whole reason this reading
uses two editions: for the propositions Taylor will do, and for their place, their method, the
state of the text they arrive in, and the practice missing from them, only Dodds has anything to
say.**

## 2. The first principle, and the form that shows you the joint

The *Elements* begins with the smallest possible object and does not begin with God. Its first
proposition is about a manifold and the one, and Taylor renders it:

> "All multitude participates in a certain respect of the one."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition I.[^t1] That is the first of the two hundred and eleven, and
the whole system is downstream of it: everything that follows is either an elaboration of what
"participates in a certain respect" can mean or an application of it to a new order of being.
The proof that follows is the shape every later proof repeats. If the manifold participated in
no respect of the one, Taylor writes, "neither will the whole be one whole, nor each of the many
of which the multitude consists" — each part would again be a manifold, and "this will be the
case to infinity", and an infinite regress of parts is impossible.[^t1] The system's first move
is therefore an **exclusion**: the alternative to unity is an infinite divisibility that cannot
be stated, and so unity is admitted by default. Dodds makes the same move visible from the
other side, in his translation:

> "Every manifold in some way participates unity."

— Dodds' translation, Prop. 1.[^d1] And in the Commentary he says exactly what kind of argument
that is: "This prop. is placed at the head of Pr.'s system in order to exclude the assumption of
a world of pure quantitative plurality" — an alternative that the argument rules out before
anything has been proved about the One.[^c1] He adds that the proof "does not occur in Plato,
nor, I think, in Plotinus": the cut against pure plurality is Proclus' own, and it is made by
demonstration rather than by citation.[^c1]

Then the system lays the first principle down, and it does so in four lines, in Taylor:

> "All multitude is posterior to the one." (Proposition V)
> "Every thing which becomes one, becomes so through the participation of the one…" (Proposition III)
> "Every thing productive of another is more excellent than the nature of the thing produced." (Proposition VII)
> "All beings proceed from one first cause." (Proposition XI)

— Taylor's translation, Propositions V, III, VII and XI.[^t5][^t3][^t7][^t11] These four, taken
together, are the axioms of the whole book, and they are not derived from anything before them.
Proposition V rules out the priority of the many; Proposition VII establishes the ranking
principle — the producer is better than the produced — by which every later order will be
graded; Proposition XI ties the whole series to a single first cause, on the ground that the
alternatives (no cause, a circle of causes, an infinite ascent) are all impossible. **By
Proposition XI the system has its shape: one first cause, everything else posterior to it,
ranked by the principle that what makes is more excellent than what is made.** The *Elements*
has stated its premises and has done so in plain sight, in the first eleven propositions.

That is the honesty the form affords, and Dodds gives it in one sentence: "Although no
authorities are directly quoted in the Elements of Theology, its pages are haunted by the ghosts
of authorities."[^i1] What is being excluded — infinite divisibility, a circle of causes, an
infinite regress — is the *check*, and what takes its place is the *postulate*. **The first
principle is not proved. It is named, and the cost of naming it is that everything after it is
proved from it.**

And the name is not a neutral one, which is the second thing the form lets a reader see. The
first principle is not "being", because being is what participates in it. Taylor's Proposition
VI shows the ladder's bottom rung — "Every multitude consists either of things united, or of
unities" — and its heading is two words: *Concerning Unity.*[^t6] Proposition XX then puts the
grades in a single ascending sentence:

> "The essence of soul is beyond all bodies, the intellectual nature is beyond all souls, and
> the one is beyond all intellectual hypostases."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition XX.[^t20] Body, soul, intellect, the one: the sentence is the
whole cosmos in one line, and it is a hierarchy of *transcendence*, which is to say of
successive subtraction. Each term is "beyond" the one below it, and the last term is beyond all
of them, so the top of the ladder is named by being set outside the world it tops. Dodds makes
the same point structurally in his Introduction, dividing the book: "The book falls into two
main sections. The first of these (props. 1 to 112)", he says, "introduces successively the
general metaphysical antitheses with which Neoplatonism operated"; "The remaining part (props.
113 to 211) expounds in the light of these antitheses the relations obtaining within each of the
three great orders of spiritual substance, gods or henads, intelligences, and souls ; and the
relations connecting each of these orders with the lower grades of reality."[^i1]
**The first half is pure structure, stated once and, in principle, checkable; the second half is
the structure being populated — with gods, with intelligences, with souls.** The axioms carry
the whole thing, and the axioms are where a reader can see them.

## 3. The ladder, and the closing proposition

Read as a demonstration, the *Elements* is a machine for assigning rank, and the assignment runs
the same way at every level. The propositions of the second half do not argue that there are gods
and souls; they argue where each thing sits once the axioms are granted. Taylor's Proposition CIII
states the rule for how the grades relate:

> "All things are in all, but appropriately in each."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CIII.[^t103] The formula is the system's answer to the
problem of hierarchy: the lower orders are not impoverished copies but *contain everything the
higher orders contain, in their own mode* — being, life and intellect all present at every
level, but in being the mode is being, in life it is life, in intellect it is intellect. It is
also the system's insurance. Nothing is lost in the descent, because nothing left the top.

Then the series closes over the top with two propositions about the gods, and their language
shows the system doing what the blog's frame-instrument has been tracking since
[the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/):

> "Every divine number is unical." (Proposition CXIII)

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CXIII.[^t113] Prop. 113 in Dodds renders the same thesis in
the plural — the gods' number "has the character of unity" — and the difference between the two
renderings is the whole matter of §4.[^d113] The point here is what Taylor's word does: the
number of the gods is *unical* — one, whatever its count — and so a multitude of gods is
asserted without multiplicity being admitted at the top. Proposition CXIX then states what a god
is, and it is a statement by subtraction:

> "Every God subsists according to superessential goodness, and is good neither according to
> participation, nor according to essence, but superessentially…"

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CXIX.[^t119] A god is not good by participation and not good
by essence; it is good *superessentially*, which is a mode of being one grade above the
distinction the sentence has just drawn. **The ladder grades everything by moving the top term
outside the distinction it is used to grade with.** And Proposition CXXIX runs the same
operation down the grades, in a sentence that is a summary of the whole second half:

> "Every divine body is divine through a deified soul. But every soul is divine through a
> divine intellect. And every intellect is divine through the participation of a divine unity.
> And unity indeed is of itself a God; intellect is most divine; soul is divine; but body is
> deiform."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CXXIX.[^t129] Body is *deiform* — god-shaped — not divine;
soul is divine; intellect is "most divine"; and union with the one is a god. Four grades, each
naming the grade above as the cause of its divinity, and the top term as itself a god.

What this series of propositions is *for* is stated, and it answers the blog's own instrument.
In the *Theology of Plato* the same system stations a hierarchy of mediation and an office at its
head; in the *Elements* the highest propositions are about what the divine orders are, and their
practical interest has been put in a separate group entirely. Dodds says of the propositions on
material substances and their properties that their purpose "is to provide a philosophical
basis for the practice of theurgy."[^c145] **The system grounds a practice in one part of itself
and performs no practice in any part of it.** That is the finding of §5, and it is the reason
this is the most useful book in the series for a reader who wants to see a frame with everything
left in it except the operator.

But before the operator is removed, the ladder is climbed. The last propositions of the book
handle the soul, and they handle it going both ways. Taylor's Proposition CCVI states the round
trip:

> "Every partial soul is able to descend infinitely into generation, and to ascend from
> generation to real being."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CCVI.[^t206] Descent and ascent are both possible, both
unlimited, and both the soul's own — the *Elements* ends with mobility, not with surrender or
with a technique. And the final proposition, Proposition CCXI, states the one thing about the
descent that a reader who has followed the climb would not have predicted:

> "Every partial soul descending into generation descends wholly; nor does one part of it remain
> on high, and another part descend."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CCXI.[^t211] The proof runs the same structure as
Proposition I: assume the opposite — that some part of the soul remains in the intelligible —
and the assumption generates either an intelligence rather than a soul, or a single substance
with contradictory properties, and is therefore impossible. Taylor's proof closes by restating
the enunciation, and the restatement is the thing to notice:

> "Every partial soul, therefore, wholly descends."

— Taylor's translation, Proposition CCXI.[^t211] That is the form's signature move, and Dodds
makes it explicit: "The final proposition is directed consciously", he writes in the Commentary,
"against the well-known theory of Plotinus that a part of the human soul remains 'above'".[^c211]
And Dodds' own translation of the same proposition says "Every particular soul, when it descends
into temporal process, descends entire", which is the same claim in a vocabulary less archaic and
a shade more philosophical.[^d211] **So the book closes not by opening a route upward but by
closing a route that Plotinus had left open: there is no hidden summit of the soul that never
came down.** The ascent of Proposition CCVI is real, but it is an ascent out of a total descent.

## 4. The terms are the metaphysics, and the two translations differ measurably

This section is a measured comparison, and it makes no claim beyond the loci it checks.

Proposition I is the cleanest case, because both editions render the same Greek and the
difference is total. Taylor: "All multitude participates in a certain respect of the one."[^t1]
Dodds: "Every manifold in some way participates unity."[^d1] **Three substitutions, and each is
a doctrine.** Taylor's *multitude* is a substantive — the name of a stuff, a collection of
things — and Dodds' *manifold* is an adjective used as a noun, a quality of things; Taylor's
*the one* is a substantive that can be participated *of*, and Dodds' *unity* is a quality that
can be participated; and Taylor's "participates of" keeps the Greek genitive — participation
*of* something — where Dodds' bare "participates" makes unity a predicate a manifold can
simply have. The three substitutions move the whole opening proposition from a claim about two
kinds of entity to a claim about a quality every entity has. **That is not a stylistic
difference. It is the difference between a cosmology of substances and a description of
structure, and it is on the first line of the book.**

Proposition VI shows the same slide on a proposition about composition. Taylor: "Every multitude
consists either of things united, or of unities."[^t6] Dodds: "Every manifold is composed either
of unified groups or of henads (units)."[^d6] Taylor's "things united" is a phrase about things;
Dodds' "unified groups" is a phrase about aggregates; and on the second term Taylor's plain
"unities" is done over by Dodds into "henads (units)", carrying the technical word in front of
the plain one. **Here the two translations are not merely saying the same thing differently;
Dodds has added a term of art and Taylor has not.** A reader who has only Taylor never learns
that the *Elements* has a doctrine of henads at this point, because Taylor has translated the
Greek word instead of importing it. A reader who has only Dodds never sees how plainly the claim
can be stated.

Proposition XXI is a third locus, and it moves the other way. Taylor: "Every order beginning from
a monad, proceeds into a multitude co-ordinate to the monad, and the multitude of every order is
referred to one monad."[^t21] Dodds: "Every order has its beginning in a monad and proceeds to a
manifold co-ordinate therewith; and the manifold in any order may be carried back to a single
monad."[^d21] Both are close to the Greek here, and the divergence is small — "multitude" against
"manifold", "one monad" against "a single monad" — but the comparison is worth having because it
shows the pattern is not an artefact of proposition I. **Where the Greek offers a substantive,
Taylor takes the substantive and Dodds takes the adjective; where the Greek offers an adjective,
they agree.** The rendering follows the ontology, and Taylor's ontology is the more thing-like
of the two.

There is a documented reason for the divergence, and citing it is part of the measurement. In
Dodds' list of the work's translations, the entry for Taylor names what it was made from:
"English, T. Taylor 1816 (based on Patrizzi)".[^tr] Patrizzi's Latin was itself, Dodds says,
translated from "renaissance copies of the second family" of manuscripts — a derivative of a
derivative.[^tr]
And Taylor's own notes to the *Elements* confirm the direction of travel from the volume's own
side: at Proposition CLVII he writes, "The words within the brackets are wanting in the original,
which I have supplied from the version of Patricius", and at the final proposition he notes,
"From the version of Patricius, it is here necessary to supply the words" — supplying a gap in
the Greek out of a Latin translation of a later manuscript tradition.[^t157][^t211n] **So
Taylor's English is a reading of a Latin reading of a Greek text, and where he needed the Greek
he reached for the Latin.** That does not make the translation wrong; it makes the words
*multitude*, *unities* and *the one* a layer further from the Greek than the reader of an
English *Elements* would assume, and it explains why the series has been quoting a Taylor whose
vocabulary is broader, flatter and more concrete than the philosophical text behind it.

The last part of the comparison is not a word but a division, and it belongs in the body because
it is a finding about the text's shape. Taylor's *Elements* has headings. Some propositions
carry a title — Proposition VI is "Concerning Unity", Proposition VII "Concerning producing
causes and things produced", Proposition CLXXXIV "Concerning Soul" — and those titles group the
two hundred and eleven propositions into named topics, which is how a reader of Taylor's volume
finds their way around.[^t6][^t7] Dodds' translation carries no such titles: it numbers the
propositions and prefixes group headings of its own — one for the One and the Many, one for
Causes — and in a note he says why. "I have thought it convenient to indicate
in my translation by means of headings the natural grouping of the propositions. In doing so I
have followed no manuscript authority."[^c187] The titles Taylor prints, Dodds says, "are
relegated to the apparatus criticus", because "they are inserted quite arbitrarily and
sporadically" — and he lists the propositions they precede, a list that covers the propositions
Taylor's volume titles, adding that some of them "clearly betray the hand of a medieval
reader".[^c187] **So the
topical architecture a reader takes from Taylor's volume — this proposition is About Unity, that
one is About Soul — is not in the text. It is an editor's or a scribe's convenience laid over the
axiomatic sequence, and Dodds' translation is an argument against it.** The measured comparison
therefore ends not with a word but with a frame: one edition presents the *Elements* as an
ordered handbook with subjects, the other as a bare sequence of numbered steps.

## 5. The theurgy is gone

This is the reading the series has been heading towards since it took the Chaldean Oracles
whole, and it is delivered by the editor in one sentence. Dodds, in the Introduction's second
section, at the end of a paragraph about the work's relation to the rest of Proclus, writes:

> "Finally, all direct reference either to personal mysticism or to theurgy is absent from the
> Elements."

— Dodds, Introduction §2.[^i2] The sentence is not an aside; it is part of his argument about the
book's date and character, and it comes with the qualification that makes it usable: "that
Proclus in fact believed in theurgy when he wrote it can hardly be doubted" — and he refers the
reader to the Commentary on Props. 39 and 145 for the evidence.[^i2] What is absent from the
*Elements* is not a belief. It is the *reference*: the ritual, the operator, the act.

The measurement supports the sentence, and the blog ran it. **In the text printed under the
heading of the *Elements* — the two hundred and eleven propositions and their proofs, together
with Taylor's own notes to them — the word "theurgy" and its derivatives do not occur at all.
Neither does "ritual", "initiation", "priest", "sacrifice", "prayer", "worship", "divination" or
"telestic".** The word occurs sixteen times elsewhere in the same volume, in the *Theology of
Plato* and the treatises that follow the *Elements* — "theurgic power, which is more excellent
than all human wisdom", "the theurgic art through certain symbols calls forth… goodness of the
Gods" — and the ritual material survives once inside the *Elements*, in a
note to Proposition CXL, where Taylor quotes Plotinus on the ancient wise men "who wished to
procure the presence of the Gods, by fabricating statues and performing sacred rites" and calls what
they practised "the magic cultivated by the ancient philosophers".[^t140]
**That note is the exception that proves the rule: the ritual material the propositions lack
survives in the translator's footnotes, and even there the word theurgy is not used.**

What the *Elements* has instead is the *theory* the theurgy needs, and Dodds shows where it sits.
The propositions on material sympathy and the virtues of stones and plants — propositions about
the lowest grades of being — exist, he says, "to provide a philosophical basis for the practice of
theurgy", and their "purgative" and other virtues "are not medical but magical".[^c145] So a
system that performs nothing still carries, in its propositions, the warrant for a practice
performed elsewhere — by a school, with a manual, in the same author's other books.
**The absence is an absence of *reference*, not an absence of *use*: the frame was built to
underwrite a rite and it does not mention the rite.**

What that does to the blog's instrument is a sharpening, not a confirmation. The blog's marker,
from [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/) and [the cosmos that
refuses](/the-cosmos-that-refuses/) onward, is **the seat**: does the frame name an endpoint and
station someone at it. The *Elements* stations no one and does nothing — no mediator who acts,
no practitioner, no rite, no route that can be pointed to, not even an addressee. It is the
frame at its most complete and its emptiest: an entire cosmos derived, and nothing in it
performing, teaching, or admitting anyone in particular.

And that is the wrong reading, as Dodds' apparatus makes clear. The seat is not absent; it is
**elsewhere in the same system**: the author who derives the cosmos without mentioning theurgy
is the author of the theurgic theology, and the school that read the *Elements* as its textbook
also practised the rites its propositions underwrite. The *Elements*
is what the frame looks like when the operator has been taken out of the text and left standing
beside it — and the reason the operator is beside the text rather than in it is stated by Dodds
in the next sentence of the same paragraph: the reference may have been omitted as out of keeping
with the book's rationalist character, or as an infringement of its a priori method of argument —
a suggestion he records without settling it.[^i2] **The operator was removed so that the proof
would look like a proof.** That is the honest description, and it is not an accusation: a school
that holds a rite and writes a
geometry is not hiding the rite — it is showing the geometry, and the geometry is the part the
ritual could not supply. But a reader who takes the *Elements* as the whole of the frame, which is
exactly what a reader of this book is invited to do, will look for a seat inside a proof and will
not find one, because the seat was never a proposition.

The ritual-versus-frame axis the series has been running since [the Chaldean
Oracles](/the-chaldean-oracles/) therefore closes inside a single author, and Dodds states it in
the shortest form, in §3 of the Introduction, quoting Iamblichus against the rationalist method:

> "that links the theurgist to the gods: else what should hinder the theoretical philosopher from
> enjoying theurgic union with them? The case is not so. Theurgic union is attained only by the
> perfective operation of the unspeakable acts correctly performed, acts which are beyond all
> understanding ; and by the power of the unutterable symbols which are intelligible only to the
> gods."

— Iamblichus, *De mysteriis* II.11, as the modern critical edition prints it, quoted in Dodds' Introduction
§3.[^i3] Read beside the
*Elements* it is an argument against it: the theoretical philosopher — the man whose method is
*theoria*, demonstration, reason — does **not** attain union by thought, and what attains it is
an act "beyond all understanding", performed correctly, on unutterable symbols. That is the
ritual pole of the series stated by a Neoplatonist, and a straight denial that a deductive frame
reaches the top. Dodds adds the other half of the contrast in the same section: Plotinus, he
says, "left to his successors a dialectical instrument of matchless power and delicacy and a
vivid tradition of personal mysticism in the proper sense of that term, as the actual experience
of the merging of the self at certain moments into some larger life" — and within two generations
that had "become a pious formula on the lips of professors".[^i3]
**So the *Elements* sits at the junction the whole series has been reading: the doctrine of the
theurgists, written in the form of the philosophers, by a man who practised the one and
published the other, in separate books, with the joint between them left unstated.**

## 6. The correction the blog owes

The last reading said that the frame which argues is the largest claim the series had met, and
it said something the blog has been carrying since: the marker of a frame is **the claim** rather
than the seat, and a claim about the whole is a claim about every being's place. The blog took
from that a working rule for reading frames: an asserted cosmology tells you what is the case,
and the more complete the assertion, the more of the world it has already answered for.

The *Elements* adds the thing that rule was missing, and it is the correction this post owes.
**A frame in the form of a proof does not assert less than a frame in the form of a revelation,
and it does not make its claim more checkable. It moves the uncheckable part to the front and
then proves everything from it.** The axioms are the revelation. The difference is that they are
labelled as propositions instead of as scripture, and that the label is what makes them look like
something a reader may inspect.

The evidence is Dodds', and it is the sharpest thing in the whole apparatus. In the footnote to
the sentence about the method of hypothesis, he writes:

> "It may be doubted, however, whether Pr. fully realized the hypothetical character of his
> postulates, to which centuries of unquestioned tradition had given the appearance of
> self-evidence."

— Dodds, Introduction §1, note.[^i1n] That is the editor of the text saying, in a single sentence,
that the axioms of the *Elements* are hypothetical — and that the author did not treat them as
hypotheses, because the tradition had given them "the appearance of self-evidence". The form
presents the premises; the author did not experience them as premises. **The axiomatic form
relocates the uncheckable part to the axioms; it does not remove it, and it does not label it —
it labels it "proposition" and lets two centuries of school doctrine supply the missing
"self-evident".** A reader who takes the deduction at face value is reading a demonstration whose
foundation is a consensus, and the consensus is not on the page.

Dodds reinforces the point from two more directions in the same Introduction. First, the method:
he notes that "no authorities are directly quoted in the Elements of Theology", and that "its
pages are haunted by the ghosts of authorities" — the acknowledgment that the derivation is full
of things arrived at elsewhere and never cited.[^i1] Second, the honesty of the derivation
itself: "Ingenious
as Proclus is, too often his 'demonstration', though formally correct, in fact merely repeats the
'enunciation' at greater length" — and worse, "A more serious fault is Proclus' trick of
confusing the accidental with the essential", by which doctrines "which owe their form, and even
sometimes their being" to a phrase met elsewhere enter the chain "in the guise of" deduction.[^i1]
And Dodds' closing verdict on the form is a verdict on all formal systems: "the coherence of a
body of philosophical thought cannot be fully expressed in a chain of logically flawless
syllogisms."[^i1]

So the correction, stated plainly:

> The blog has been treating a frame that states its premises as safer than one that reveals
> them, and the *Elements* shows the mistake from inside the strongest available example. Its
> premises are stated *before* anything is proved, so a reader can put a finger on the joint —
> and they are not demonstrated, because centuries of tradition had given them "the appearance of
> self-evidence". **The formal frame has the same uncheckable part as the revealed one, and it is
> at the front, wearing the label of the thing that proved everything else.**

And the consequence lands on the blog's test, which is the same three questions it has been
asking since [the owned cosmos](/the-owned-cosmos/). The blog can now add a fourth, because the
*Elements* names it: **does the frame present its first principle as a postulate, or as a
self-evident fact?** [The Orphic Hymns](/the-orphic-hymns/) had no premises to inspect, which was
its own answer. The *Elements* has its premises in plain sight and does not inspect them, which
is a different answer, and a harder one, because the plain-sight premises are exactly what makes
the rest of the book look like knowledge. **The advantage of the axiomatic form is not that it
checks anything. It is that it shows a reader where to check — and Proclus' book shows it, and
does not do it.**

## 7. The counterweight: the proof is a scholar's text

The gap in this reading is not in Proclus. It is in the text the blog could read, and it is
larger here than in the previous reading, which had one translator's reconstruction to account
for and this one has an editor's as well.

Dodds' edition is not the *Elements*; it is a text *established* for it. He collated the work
against some forty-five manuscript copies, arranged them into three families, and concluded that
four sources — two families' archetypes, one manuscript of the second family, and the medieval
Latin version of William of Morbecca — are mutually independent witnesses to a lost archetype he
calls [X].[^ms] His account of his own work is exact:

> "The chief part of my work has been in removing corruptions of late origin, attempting the
> reconstruction of [X], and endeavouring to introduce a system of punctuation which shall not
> needlessly obscure the author's thought."

— Dodds, Introduction §3.[^ed] And he is honest about the limit of the whole enterprise: "The
stemma codicum facing this page makes no claim to complete accuracy".[^ed] One of his chief
manuscripts, he records, "has been much tampered with, and many of the original readings have
been wholly or partially erased; but most of these can be recovered with greater or less
certainty" — *recovered*, by inference from the copies made before correction.[^ms] A third
family's readings are, he says, often "due to deliberate and reckless 'correction' of the
tradition".[^ms]

**So the text this post calls "Dodds' translation" is a hypothesis about a text.** Every
punctuation mark in the facing Greek, every choice between admittedly erased readings, every
decision that a passage is to be read with a comma or without one, is the editor's — and the
editor says as much. The reading a modern reader has of the *Elements* is mediated by a
scholar's judgement at every line, and the scholar is the first to put that in print.

Then the headings, which is the sharpest form of the same point. As §4 recorded, Dodds'
translation prints group headings for the propositions and says in a note that they are his:
"In doing so I have followed no manuscript authority."[^c187] The topical titles a reader of
Taylor's volume sees — *Concerning Unity*, *Concerning producing causes and things produced* —
are, Dodds says, insertions into "certain MSS. and in the printed texts of Portus and Creuzer",
made "quite arbitrarily and sporadically", some of them clearly the work of "a medieval
reader".[^c187] **So the division of the axiomatic sequence into named subjects is a frame laid over the frame,
and the two editions lay different ones.** Both are printings of the same propositions, and
neither is the sequence as the author left it — because nobody has that.

Taylor's side of the gap is the one the previous reading described and this one inherits. His
translation was made, as Dodds records, from Patrizzi's Latin, out of a manuscript tradition
further from the Greek than the one Dodds collated, and he supplies the text's gaps from the same
Latin — his notes to Propositions CLVII and CCXI say so in his own words, and a note to
Proposition CL simply records, "There is a chasm here in the original."[^t157][^t211n][^t150]
So Taylor's *Elements* is a text with chasms mended from a translation of a translation, and
Dodds' is a text with no chasms and a punctuation its editor invented. **Neither is "the text".
Both are readings of a work that survives in some forty-five manuscript copies, in three
families, with the archetype lost, and which every edition since 1618 has reconstructed by
judgement.** There is no such reader's text to want: the *Elements* reaches a modern reader
already argued about, at the level of its own punctuation.

The second half of the gap is structural, and it is the honest counterweight to §6, so it
belongs in the body rather than in a note. **The axiomatic form's failure to label its premises
as premises is real, and it is not the same failure as a rite's.** A rite also hides its premises
— it performs them, and a performer need never say "the gods exist". A revealed text hides them
at the top, in a voice that is not the author's. The *Elements* states them, in the first eleven
propositions, in the author's own voice, and then derives everything from them. **Nothing in it
is unstated; everything in it is under-labelled.** That is a smaller failure than the
alternatives, and the blog's instrument should hold both facts at once: this is the frame in
which a reader can most nearly find the joint, and also the frame in which the joint is presented
as self-evidence. The form is not a safeguard against uncheckable premises; it is a *disclosure*
of them, and that is the most a formal frame can honestly offer.

## 8. What this reading claims

**One.** The *Elements of Theology* is the same system as the *Theology of Plato* in its other
form: not the frame that argues, but the frame that proves. Two hundred and eleven propositions,
each stated and then derived from the ones before, from a first principle that is not being to a
last proposition about the descent of a soul. It is the most complete frame this series has read
and the first whose authority is its deduction rather than its revelation or its rite.

**Two.** The form does what it appears to do and it does not do what the blog half-suspected. It
shows a reader where the system's first principle enters and how everything after it is derived.
It does not demonstrate the first principle, and its editor records that the author did not hold
it as a hypothesis at all: "centuries of unquestioned tradition had given the appearance of
self-evidence". So the correction: **the axiomatic form relocates the uncheckable part to the
axioms and places it in plain sight without labelling it.** The blog's test gains a fourth
question — does the frame present its first principle as a postulate or as a fact? — and the
*Elements* answers it wrongly while being the book that makes the question askable.

**Three.** The theurgy is gone, and the absence is measurable rather than interpretive. The word
and its derivatives do not occur in the *Elements* at all; nor do ritual, initiation, priest,
sacrifice or prayer. The editor says it outright: "all direct reference either to personal
mysticism or to theurgy is absent from the Elements." What survives is the theory the rite needs,
in the propositions on material sympathy, which exist "to provide a philosophical basis for the
practice of theurgy". **So the seat is not absent from the system; it is absent from the text,
and it is standing beside it in the same author's other books and the same school's practice.**
The operator was taken out of the proof so that the proof would read as a proof.

**Four.** The terms carry the ontology, and the two editions differ measurably. Taylor's
*multitude*, *unities* and *the one* are substantives; Dodds' *manifold*, *unified groups* and
*unity* are qualities; and the difference is on the first line of the book. It is explained in
part by provenance: Taylor's English is based on Patrizzi's Latin, itself made from a later
manuscript family, and Taylor's own notes supply the Greek's gaps from the Latin. And it is
explained in part by editing: the topical headings Taylor's volume carries are not in the text,
and Dodds' translation is built to reject them.

**Five.** The counterweights, in the body, and there are two. There is no reader's text of the
*Elements*: both editions are frames laid over the frame — Dodds' with a punctuation its editor
invented and a stemma that "makes no claim to complete accuracy", Taylor's with chasms mended
from a translation of a translation. And the form's honesty is real but partial: the book states
its premises and does not call them premises. Neither counterweight is a verdict on anyone.

What the blog takes from the twenty-first reading is the completion of an arc it began when it
took the Orphic Hymns whole. The Hymns perform a cosmology and state nothing. The Oracles state
one and station no one. The *Theology of Plato* argues one and gives every being its place. The
*Elements* proves one, and proves it with nothing in the proof that does anything — no rite, no
operator, no mediator who acts, no route a reader could be shown, and an editor who can say
where the rites went. **The frame at its most complete has no seat and no practice in it, and the
reason is the form: a proof cannot perform, and a school that wants a practice to go with its
proof writes the practice somewhere else.** And the book that says so most clearly is not
Proclus'. It is the sentence a twentieth-century editor wrote to describe what is missing from
it.

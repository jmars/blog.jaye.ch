# The Anatomy of Destructiveness

### Twelfth of the `readings` — the one psychology in the library that refuses both instinct and conditioning, and answers with a character structure that is formed, real, and the hardest thing this blog has had to place

*Twelfth of the `readings`. [The follower post](/the-follower/) found the follower to be a position
rather than a personality, and warned that the instrument which would sort people is the instrument
that fails; [the Lucifer Effect post](/the-lucifer-effect/) read the social psychology the blog had
been running unnamed and found the study underneath it broken; [Eichmann in Jerusalem](/eichmann-in-jerusalem/)
took the blog's most-quoted phrase back to its source and found a faculty where the blog had assumed
a structure. This is the third book the tree's own planning note files in that group, and the one the
blog has never cited at all — a psychology that refuses the position-versus-personality split from the
other side, argues that something **inside the person** is also real and describable, and would put it
on a scale and count it.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source. The quotations are verbatim from the digital text, checked
character for character against the file and cited in the notes **by chapter and section**, because the
file has no usable page flow; the reading of them is an **inference**, offered as one. **The book's
thesis is not endorsed**, and §8 and §9 are about why a reading of it cannot endorse it. The case
material — Stalin, Himmler, Hitler — is **reported as the book's content and not adjudicated here**,
and no living person is diagnosed. Nothing here validates or disqualifies anyone's experience, and
nothing here is a checklist for recognising anybody. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no
one.*

## 1. The book, and the debt it names

*The Anatomy of Human Destructiveness* is by Erich Fromm. The file's copyright page gives it exactly:
first published by Holt, Rinehart and Winston, New York, **1973**; the text follows the revised edition
as Owl Book (Henry Holt and Company, New York) **1992**; (c) 1973 by Erich Fromm, (c) 1992 by the Estate
of Erich Fromm; this edition published in 2013 by Open Road Integrated Media; ISBN
978-1-4804-0193-8.[^ed] The structure the Contents page gives is: Preface; Terminology; Introduction:
Instincts and Human Passions; then **Part I — Instinctivism, Behaviorism, Psychoanalysis** (chs. 1-4);
**Part II — The Evidence Against the Instinctivist Thesis** (chs. 5-8, on neurophysiology, animal
behaviour, paleontology and anthropology); **Part III — The Varieties of Aggression and Destructiveness
and Their Respective Conditions** (chs. 9-13, ending with a chapter each on cruelty and destructiveness,
on necrophilia, and on Adolf Hitler); an **Appendix** on Freud's theory of aggressiveness; a
Bibliography; an Index; and a closing **Epilogue: On the Ambiguity of Hope**.[^file]

The digital text — `~/thework/work-text/The_Anatomy_of_Human_Destructiveness_-_Erich_Fromm.txt`,
1,386,860 bytes as stored — carries **no line breaks anywhere in it**. A repeated watermark,
"OceanofPDF.com", stands where some section breaks were, the sections are shuffled out of book order —
the file opens in the middle of chapter 13 and puts the Contents page at offset 1,262,176, near the
end — and the running heads are unreliable. That is why the citations below are by chapter and section
with the character offset of each quotation, the way the other readings have handled damaged scans.[^file]

Two facts belong before the reading, and they are the reason it exists.

**The blog has never cited him.** A case-insensitive search for "Fromm" across `content/` returns
**nothing** — not one line, in any post, in either spelling of the name.[^cite] He appears in the tree
only in the planning note that files him with Arendt and Zimbardo as unmined, and in the build plumbing
for this post. He is, on that count, the largest uncited source the blog has.

**And he is the hardest of them to place.** The blog's method has two standing forms, and both point
away from him. The first is *describe structures, diagnose no one* — which forbids the diagnosis of a
person. The second is stronger: the blog has read predation as a **position**, and said so in three
separate registers — the levers are "positions rather than beliefs",[^costume] the follower is "a
position, not a personality",[^follower] and the machine's reading "describes a position, not a
type".[^machine] On that account the person is *dissolved into the position*: what makes the operation
work is not what is in anybody's head but where everybody stands.

Fromm is the serious psychological answer from the opposite side, and it is not a naive one. He is not
saying people are born bad; he is saying that whatever is in the person is **formed by conditions and
by the person's own response to them**, that it is organised, that it persists, and that it can be
described. The thing he names is neither an instinct nor a circumstance. It is a **character
structure** — and the whole question of this reading is what the blog does with that.

## 2. What the book is arguing against, in its own words

Fromm begins by naming two opponents and refusing both.

The first is the instinctivists — Lorenz's *On Aggression* (1966), Robert Ardrey, Desmond Morris,
Eibl-Eibesfeldt. Their common thesis, in Fromm's summary:

> "man's aggressive behavior as manifested in war, crime, personal quarrels, and all kinds of
> destructive and sadistic behavior is due to a phylogenetically programmed, innate instinct which
> seeks for discharge and waits for the proper occasion to be expressed."[^intro]

His reading of why that theory sells is not flattering, and it is the sentence the blog's own material
would recognise:

> "This theory of an innate aggressiveness easily becomes an ideology that helps to soothe the fear of
> what is to happen and to rationalize the sense of impotence."[^intro]

The second opponent is behaviourism — Skinner's neo-behaviourism specifically. Fromm's complaint about
it is a complaint about a subject matter, and he puts it in one line: "the subject matter of the
dominant school in psychology became behavior, not the behaving man", and "the 'science of the psyche'
was transformed into the science of the engineering of animal and human conduct."[^intro] The two
schools he says the present alternative offers are both closed:

> "Both positions are 'monoexplanatory,' depending on dogmatic preconceptions, and investigators are
> required to fit data in one or the other explanation."[^intro]

And then he asks the question the book exists to answer: "But are we really confronted with the
alternative of accepting either the instinctivist or the behaviorist theory? Are we forced to choose
between Lorenz and Skinner; are there no other options?" — followed immediately by the book's own
sentence of scope: "This book affirms that there is another option, and examines the question of what
it is."[^intro]

What the two have in common is the point, and it is the point at which Fromm becomes interesting to
this blog rather than merely useful. Chapter 3, "A Common Ground", states the shared premise flatly:
instinctivism and behaviourism both assume **"that man has no psyche with its own structure and its own
laws"**, and both "exclude the person, the behaving man, from their field of vision".[^ch3] Then the
image, in a sentence that is easy to mistake for rhetoric and is not:

> "Whether man is the product of conditioning, or the product of animal evolution, he is exclusively
> determined by conditions outside himself; he has no part in his own life, no responsibility, and not
> even a trace of freedom. Man is a puppet, controlled by strings — instinct or conditioning."[^ch3]

The contrast he draws between the two types is the same move from the other side — "The man of the
instinctivists lives the past of the species, as the man of the behaviorists lives the present of his
social system" — and it is a symmetry, not a preference.[^ch3]

**The part of this that the blog owes is not the thesis; it is the reflexivity.** Fromm does not leave
his opponents' theories as theories. Chapter 3, "The Political and Social Background of Both Theories",
reads eighteen-century environmentalism as the revolutionary middle class's theory of itself, and
nineteenth-century instinctivism as "the basic assumption of nineteenth-century capitalism" — a natural
order proved by nature — with the twentieth century's turn away from it explained by a change in the
mode of control. Then he notes the asymmetry in the critics: he quotes the psychoanalyst Robert Waelder
exposing the bias of the anti-instinctivists, and observes that "it is nevertheless noteworthy that he
only sees the bias of the anti-instinctivists and not of those who share his own position."[^ch3]

That is the blog's own kind of move — a frame that allocates authority is described as a frame — and it
is worth registering that Fromm makes it *against both sides including his own*, because the rest of
this post will press on the one place where he does not.

On Freud he is neither a disciple nor a prosecutor, and the distinction he draws matters for §4. He
keeps the Freud who found the passions and drops the Freud who explained them: "I had been deeply
impressed by his concept that the striving for life and the striving for destruction were the two most
fundamental forces within man; but I could not reconcile myself to Freud's theoretical explanation."[^ch12]
What he shifts is the ground of explanation — "from Freud's physiological to a sociobiological and
historical principle"[^intro] — and he says so as a programme, not an aside. The Appendix at the back of
the book, an eighty-page study of the evolution of Freud's concept of aggressiveness, is the fullest
statement of what he keeps and what he drops.

## 3. The two aggressions, and a word that is doing two jobs

The book's first distinction is the one everything else rests on. In the Introduction, in two
paragraphs:

> "We must distinguish in man two entirely different kinds of aggression. The first, which he shares
> with all animals, is a phylogenetically programmed impulse to attack (or to flee) when vital
> interests are threatened. This defensive, 'benign' aggression is in the service of the survival of
> the individual and the species, is biologically adaptive, and ceases when the threat has ceased to
> exist. The other type, 'malignant' aggression, i.e., cruelty and destructiveness, is specific to the
> human species and virtually absent in most mammals; it is not phylogenetically programmed and not
> biologically adaptive; it has no purpose, and its satisfaction is lustful."[^intro]

Three of those clauses are load-bearing and each one costs him something. *Virtually absent in most
mammals* is what obliges the four empirical chapters of Part II. *Not phylogenetically programmed* is
the direct denial of Lorenz. And **"it has no purpose, and its satisfaction is lustful"** is the one
that should stop a reader of this blog, because the blog's own account of predation is entirely
instrumental — a lever works whether or not anyone enjoys it, and the-position-that-exploits is
described precisely so that motive never has to be invoked. Fromm's malignant aggression is the
opposite: purposeless by his own definition, and therefore sensible only as an explanation of a person.

The distinction is followed by the claim about species, stated without hedging:

> "man differs from the animal by the fact that he is a killer; he is the only primate that kills and
> tortures members of his own species without any reason, either biological or economic, and who feels
> satisfaction in doing so."[^intro]

and then the empirical thesis, which is where the book is genuinely a history rather than a psychology:
human groups "differ so fundamentally in the respective degree of destructiveness" that innateness
could hardly explain it, destructiveness correlates with social structure, "and (3) the degree of
destructiveness increases with the increased development of civilization, rather than the opposite."[^intro]

**And the disciplinary move, which is the one this blog has already inherited without knowing from
whom.** The book opens with a short section called **Terminology**, and it is a refusal to keep using a
word that is doing two jobs. Fromm's complaint is that "aggression" is applied to a man defending his
life, to a robber killing for money, and to a sadist torturing a prisoner, and that once one word covers
all three, "there is no hope of understanding their 'cause'; they have no common cause because they are
entirely different phenomena."[^term] He then sets out Lorenz's argument as a syllogism and lets it
stand:

> "The word 'aggression' serves conveniently as a bridge to connect biologically adaptive aggression
> (which is not evil) with human destructiveness which indeed is evil. The core of this kind of
> 'reasoning' is: Biologically adaptive aggression = innate
> Destructiveness and cruelty = aggression
> Ergo: Destructiveness and cruelty = innate. Q.E.D."[^term]

His own terminological rule follows: "aggression" is reserved for defensive, reactive aggression, and
"destructiveness and cruelty" are the words for "the specifically human propensity to destroy and to
crave for absolute control".[^term] The blog's own post on the word "cult" makes exactly this argument —
*a category that does harm, so name the mechanism instead* — and it should be said plainly that the blog
did not invent that discipline and did not arrive at it alone; Fromm is running it here, five decades
earlier, on a word that was doing more damage than "cult".[^label]

**One collision of vocabulary needs flagging, because it will mislead anyone who reads this book
alongside the blog.** Fromm uses *predation* for a biological feeding mode, in a section of chapter 5
literally headed "Predation and Aggression": predatory animals "comprise the families of cats, hyenas,
wolves, and bears"; predatory aggression has its own neurophysiological substrate, distinct from
defensive aggression; "The predatory instinct is not one of defense, common to all animals, but of
food-finding"; and his conclusion, which is the opposite of Ardrey's famous title, is that "man is
phylogenetically a nonpredatory animal."[^ch5] The blog's *predation* is not that. It is the name for a
position in a structure — an operator and the frame they stand over — and it is explicitly not a claim
about appetite, species, or biology.[^costume] The words are the same and share nothing. A reading that
lets them be confused would end up arguing that the blog had called its operators cats.

## 4. Character as the third term — and the norm that comes with it

Between instinct and conditioning Fromm inserts a third thing, and it is the book's actual subject. The
Introduction states it as a pair of distinctions:

> "The distinction between benign-defensive and malignant-destructive aggression calls for a further
> and more fundamental distinction, that between instinct and character" — the file's own footnote
> digit falls here — "or more precisely, between drives rooted in man's physiological needs (organic
> drives) and those specifically human passions rooted in his character ('character-rooted, or human
> passions')."[^intro]

He then says what character is *for*, and this is the hinge of the whole book: it is "man's 'second
nature,' the substitute for his poorly developed instincts", and the character-rooted passions "are
answers to 'existential needs,' which in turn are rooted in the very conditions of human
existence."[^intro] Instincts answer physiological needs; character answers needs that arise from being
the kind of creature that has to make sense of having been born. And he is careful that this is a
category of explanation, not a metaphor: "Instincts are a purely natural category, while the
character-rooted passions are a sociobiological, historical category."[^intro]

Chapter 10's section "Character Structure" gives the fullest statement of what the term means:

> "Character is the specific structure in which human energy is organized in the pursuit of man's
> goals; it motivates behavior according to its dominant goals: a person acts 'instinctively,' we say,
> in accordance with his character. To use Heraclitus's phrase, character is man's fate."[^ch10]

Read the examples that follow, because they are chosen to be unremarkable: "The miser does not ponder
whether he should save or spend; he is driven to save and to hoard".[^ch10] The structure is not
something a person consults. It is what makes their conduct look to them like nature.

**The existential account is where the book is most itself.** Man's needs — for a frame of orientation
and devotion, for rootedness, for unity, for effectiveness, for excitation — are the same needs in
everyone, and each can be answered two ways. "The need for effectiveness can be answered by love,
productive work — or by sadism and destructiveness." And then the sentence that explains why anyone
would choose the second: "Man seeks for drama and excitement; when he cannot get satisfaction on a
higher level, he creates for himself the drama of destruction."[^intro] Destructiveness here is not an
overflow. It is a solution to a problem, and a cheap one — "for destruction all that is necessary is
strong arms, or a knife, or a gun."[^ch12]

**The formation story is the part that does the work against innatism**, and Fromm tells it as a sliding
scale rather than a threshold. In chapter 13, on method:

> "the individual begins life with certain qualities that dispose him to go in certain directions, but
> his personality is still malleable enough to allow the character to develop in many different
> directions within the given framework. Every step in life narrows down the number of possible future
> outcomes. The more the character is fixed, the greater must be the impact of new factors if they are
> to produce fundamental changes in the direction of the further evolution of the system. Eventually,
> the freedom to change becomes so minimal that only a miracle would seem capable of effecting a
> change."[^ch13]

That paragraph is the book's answer to both opponents at once. Against the instinctivists: what starts
early is a set of *dispositions to develop*, not a programme. Against the behaviourists: there is
something there, and it accumulates — which is why later events have to be more violent than earlier
ones to move it. He also names the social half of the same mechanism, and coins a term for it: "social
character", the process by which "each form of society (or social class) needs to use human energy in
the specific manner necessary for the functioning of that particular society" and its members "must
want to do what they have to do if the society is to function properly."[^ch10]

**And here is the thing the blog has to mark, because it is where a structure becomes a verdict.** The
same chapter that describes character as formed also carries a **norm**, and Fromm states it as a
premise rather than a finding:

> "The position taken here assumes that man has an immanent goal, that man's biological constitution is
> the source of norms for living. He has the possibility for full development and growth, provided the
> external conditions that are given are conducive to this aim."[^ch10]

From that premise fall the two syndromes — the "life-furthering" and the "life-thwarting" — the
vocabulary of "a crippled, stunted man", and the claim that necrophilia is "the outcome of unlived
life, of the failure to arrive at a certain stage beyond narcissism and indifference."[^ch12] He knows
how this will be heard and says so: "It is truly astonishing that this view should be considered
'idealistic' or 'unscientific' by so many who would not dream of questioning the relation between
constitution and norms in regard to physical development and health."[^ch10]

The blog cannot run that premise. Not because it is falsifiable — because it is a *norm*, and a norm is
what turns a description into a ranking. That is the risk the brief named, and it is real, and it is in
the book rather than in its reception. What keeps it from being fatal is that Fromm spends the rest of
the chapter qualifying it, and §9 is where those qualifications are collected.

## 5. Necrophilia, at the book's own scale

Chapter 12 is the famous one, and the blog's job here is to report it as the book states it — including
where the book goes too far.

**Provenance first, because it is not Freud's.** Fromm says he took the word from a speech. In 1936, at
the University of Salamanca, the Nationalist general Millan Astray's motto — "Viva la Muerte!", "Long
live death!" — was shouted from the hall, and the rector, Miguel de Unamuno, answered him. Fromm quotes
the reply at length, and takes the coinage:

> "The term 'necrophilous,' to denote a character trait rather than a perverse act in the traditional
> sense, was used by the Spanish philosopher Miguel de Unamuno in 1936"[^ch12]

and then, in a footnote in the text's own voice: "I adopted the use of the term from Unamuno and have
been studying the phenomenon of character-rooted necrophilia since about 1961."[^ch12] The move from
the older, clinical sense — acts with a corpse — to a character trait is his, and he says so. He also
says where his data came from, without dressing it up: "My theoretical concepts were gained mainly by
observation of persons in analysis", with historical personalities and questionnaire studies as
additional material.[^ch12]

**The definition is one sentence, and it is the sentence to argue with:**

> "Necrophilia in the characterological sense can be described as the passionate attraction to all that
> is dead, decayed, putrid, sickly; it is the passion to transform that which is alive into something
> unalive; to destroy for the sake of destruction; the exclusive interest in all that is purely
> mechanical. It is the passion to tear apart living structures."[^ch12]

Note what is in it and what is not. There is no violence in the definition. *Order*, *control* and
*the mechanical* — the three things a reader of this blog would expect to find in a description of a
predatory frame — are there, and they are there as the **opposite of life**, not as methods. And the
last clause is the one that gives the whole book its shape: the passion is to **tear apart living
structures**. His biophilia, when it arrives, will be "structures rather than summations" (§6). The
axis is not good against bad. It is living organisation against its dismemberment.

**What the character does**, in the book's catalogue, is a list rather than a syndrome, and its
ordinariness is deliberate: the conviction "that the only way to solve a problem or a conflict is by
force and violence", with force — "as Simone Weil said, 'the power to transform a man into a corpse'" —
as "the first and the last solution for everything"; the queen's answer in *Alice in Wonderland*,
"off with their heads!"; a marked interest in sickness and death, the obituaries read first, the
funeral parlours and the cemeteries; a particular kind of lifelessness in conversation — "The
necrophilous person is a wet blanket and a joy killer in a group; he is boring rather than animating";
and the relation to time and property, which Fromm compresses into three clauses: "Briefly, things rule
man; having rules being; the dead rule the living."[^ch12] There is a section on colour and one on
smell, and the physical descriptions extend to "unspontaneous gestures of a poor actor."[^ch12]

**The language section is the one the blog's own material sits closest to.** Fromm's finding is that
"the language of the necrophilous person is characterized by the predominant use of words referring to
destruction and to feces and toilets", and his evidence is a questionnaire answer: asked about women's
use of lipstick and make-up, a majority answered in the prevailing ideology — "It is bourgeois",
"unnatural", "not hygienic" — while a minority gave answers like "It is poisonous" or "It makes
women look like whores", and "almost invariably, the respondents who used these words showed a
destructive trend in most other answers."[^ch12] Whether a questionnaire about cosmetics can do that
work is a question this reading cannot settle; what is worth noting is that it is a finding about
**diction as a symptom**, which is the same surface the blog's own posts on loaded vocabulary work on,
reached from underneath rather than from the outside.

**Chapter 12's most interesting structure is a derivation, and it is also its weakest joint.** Fromm
sets out a clinical sequence — "normal anal character ———> sadistic character ———> necrophilous
character" — "determined by the increase of narcissism, unrelatedness, and destructiveness", and
concludes that "necrophilia can be described as the malignant form of the anal character."[^ch12] He
then immediately hedges that the neatness is a schematism — "But the connections are by no means so
neat" — and then, more interestingly, notices that the type he built the derivation from is
disappearing:

> "the anal character that was typical of the nineteenth-century middle class is becoming increasingly
> less frequent among the sector of the population that is fully integrated into the economically most
> advanced forms of production."[^ch12]

so that "the character of the new type of man does not seem to fit into any of the older categories",
and he proposes a **marketing character** for whom "everything is transformed into a commodity — not
only things, but the person himself, his physical energy, his skills, his knowledge, his opinions, his
feelings, even his smiles."[^ch12]

**And then the sentence the blog has no analogue for, in the section on necrophilia and the worship of
technique.** Writing about what mechanised killing does to the killer, Fromm says the technicalisation
of destruction removes "the full affective recognition of what one is doing", and then:

> "Once this process has been fully established there is no limit to destructiveness because nobody
> destroys: one only serves the machine for programmed — hence, apparently rational — purposes."[^ch12]

*Nobody destroys: one only serves the machine.* That is the blog's own description-of-a-position,
written by an author who does not believe positions are enough. Read in that light the sentence is
ambiguous in a productive way: it can mean the person has been replaced by a function, or it can mean
the person's passion has been routed through a function so that it no longer has to be felt. Fromm
notices the ambiguity himself, and raises it against his own thesis — whether contemporary technical
man "is not motivated by a passion for destruction, but would be more properly described as a totally
alienated man whose dominant orientation is cerebral … who has become, in a characterological sense,
an automaton, but not a destroyer?"[^ch12] His answer, which the next section reaches, includes the
book's most quoted image of modern necrophilia, and it is not a corpse: "Its symbols are now clean,
shining machines; men are not attracted to smelly toilets, but to structures of aluminum and glass."[^ch12]

## 6. Biophilia, and the alternative

The opposite orientation arrives late and briefly, at the end of chapter 12, and it is the part a
reader is most likely to skip and least likely to find elsewhere:

> "Biophilia is the passionate love of life and of all that is alive; it is the wish to further growth,
> whether in a person, a plant, an idea, or a social group."[^ch12]

What follows is a list of orientations, and two clauses in it are gifts to a blog whose subject is
structures. The biophilous person "wants to be more rather than to have more … He loves the adventure
of living more than he does certainty", and then: **"He sees the whole rather than only the parts,
structures rather than summations."[^ch12]** The phrase is Fromm's, in 1973, and it says in nine words
what this blog has been doing with the words *frame* and *environment* — that a structure is not the
sum of its parts, and that seeing it is a capacity rather than a method.

The book then declares an ethics in the same voice, and is honest that that is what it is. "Biophilic
ethics have their own principle of good and evil. Good is all that serves life; evil is all that serves
death."[^ch12] That is a definition, not a measurement, and Fromm presents it as an ethics — which is
the least a reader can ask of a norm. It is still a norm, and §9 returns to it.

**The relation to Freud is the technical point and it is worth stating exactly.** Fromm does not say
the two orientations are equal forces, and it is on this that he departs from the death-instinct
theory: "in Freud's concept both tendencies have equal rank, as it were, both being biologically given.
Biophilia, on the other hand, is understood to refer to a biologically normal impulse, while
necrophilia is understood as a psychopathological phenomenon."[^ch12] So the asymmetry is his own
contribution: one is what the organism is for, the other is what happens when it is stunted. Hence the
sentence that gives the book its moral shape — a *capacity* is given, and a *possibility* is chosen
badly:

> "Destructiveness is not parallel to, but the alternative to biophilia. Love of life or love of the
> dead is the fundamental alternative that confronts every human being. … Man is biologically endowed
> with the capacity for biophilia, but psychologically he has the potential for necrophilia as an
> alternative solution."[^ch12]

And then the mechanism by which the alternative gets chosen, which is the closest the book comes to
the blog's own account of why a frame is so easy to enter:

> "If man cannot create anything or move anybody, if he cannot break out of the prison of his total
> narcissism and isolation, he can escape the unbearable sense of vital impotence and nothingness only
> by affirming himself in the act of destruction of the life that he is unable to create."[^ch12]

**The counterweight belongs here rather than at the end**, because this is the passage from which the
book's whole normative apparatus is built: an alternative that is available to every human being, a
capacity that is given and a pathology that is a failure to arrive. It is a complete moral psychology,
and it is the strongest thing in the book — and the blog will not take it, because taking it means
holding a specification of what a person is for, and the first thing the blog's discipline forbids is a
specification against which a person can be measured. What the blog can take is the *structure* of the
claim: one orientation is not the negation of the other but its **substitute**, chosen when the first
is unavailable, and cheap — "for destruction all that is necessary is strong arms, or a knife, or a
gun."[^ch12]

## 7. The three cases, and what a case is for here

Part III contains three clinical case studies of named historical figures — Stalin ("a Clinical Case of
Nonsexual Sadism"), Himmler ("a Clinical Case of Anal-Hoarding Sadism"), and Hitler ("a Clinical Case
of Necrophilia"). The Stalin chapter says what the form is meant to be: "His behavior is a textbook
description of nonsexual, as de Sade's novels are of sexual sadism."[^ch11] The Hitler chapter is the
central exhibit, and it is the one this reading has to describe structurally, because it is where the
book's method and the blog's rule diverge most sharply.

**The method is stated in three moves.** First, the two questions an "analytic psychobiographical study"
asks: "What are the driving forces that motivate a person, the passions that impel or incline him to
behave as he does?" and "What are the conditions — internal and external — responsible for the
development of these specific passions (character traits)?"[^ch13] Second, the evidence base, which is
deliberately not behavioural: "the analysis of dreams, unintended behavior, gestures, language, and
behavior that is rationally not fully explainable permits one to form a picture of the essential and
mostly unconscious passions ('X-ray approach')."[^ch13] Third, the point of the exercise, and it is the
one the blog must not lose:

> "The question raised in this book is not concerned with behavior, but with character. … the question
> is not whether Hitler behaved destructively, but whether he was motivated by an intense passion to
> destroy, a passion for destruction that was part of his character, this has to be proven, not taken
> for granted."[^ch13]

and the premise underneath it: **"destructive actions are not necessarily manifestations of a
destructive, necrophilous character."**[^ch13] He argues the point by counter-example — Napoleon, and
the many commanders who ordered destruction — and that argument is genuinely strong. A general who
sacrifices his soldiers for ambition is not thereby in love with death.

**This is the load-bearing collision with the blog, and it is worth naming precisely.** The blog's rule
is that you read the position and never the motive, because the motive is exactly what cannot be read
and exactly what a description becomes when it is misused. Fromm's method is the exact inverse: conduct
tells you almost nothing, "the question raised in this book is not concerned with behavior", and the
work is to reconstruct the motive from dreams, slips, gestures, diction and the pattern of a life. Both
positions accept that destructiveness is real. They disagree about what a description is *of* — a place
somebody stands, or a structure somebody has. Nothing in this reading resolves that; §8 and §9 are
about how far it can be carried.

**What he does with the evidence is best seen in the section on repression, because it is a real
argument rather than an assertion.** Hitler, he says, repressed the awareness of his destructiveness by
rationalising every order as defensive, and then by reaction formation — the mechanism, familiar from
clinical work, in which a person denies a drive "by developing traits that are exactly the opposite".[^ch13]
His named examples are vegetarianism, begun after the suicide of Geli Raubal, and a phobia of corpses
that kept Hitler from the front: "In my opinion this phobic reaction to seeing corpses is a defense
reaction against the awareness of his own destructiveness. As long as he only gave and signed orders,
he had only spoken and written."[^ch13] *He had only spoken and written* — the sentence is doing the
same work the blog's posts do about the operator who never touches anybody, arrived at from the
opposite direction, through a psychology of motive.

He is also scrupulous about his sources in a way that is worth recording, because a reading that
reported only the vivid material would misrepresent the chapter's own standard. The early-life material
leans on B. F. Smith and W. Maser — with the verdict that "Maser, though a historian, is often
unreliable in the use of his sources" and "Smith is by far the most objective and reliable source for
Hitler's youth"[^ch13] — and Speer's recollections, which recur as "A. Speer, personal communication."
He refuses the madness verdict twice over: "in conventional terms Hitler was not a psychotic man, in
dynamic, interpersonal terms he was a very sick man",[^ch13] and the labelling question is answered
with a passage his own profession has to sit under: "the questionable value of psychiatric labels …
in the last analysis what we are dealing with are interpersonal processes that defy such labels."[^ch13]

**And then the thing the blog has to record, because it is the book's own sentence and not a reading
imposed on it.** The Hitler chapter closes by stating its purpose:

> "I had still another aim: that of pointing to the main fallacy which prevents people from recognizing
> potential Hitlers before they have shown their true faces. This fallacy lies in the belief that a
> thoroughly destructive and evil man must be a devil — and look his part … as long as one believes
> that the evil man wears horns, one will not discover an evil man."[^ch13]

followed by an estimate offered as an estimate — "even if one would estimate that such persons formed
10 percent of our population, there are enough of them to be very dangerous if they attain influence
and power" — and the line "there are probably hundreds of Hitlers among us who would come forth if
their historical hour arrived."[^ch13]

That is a detection programme. It is offered in good faith, and it is exactly the move the blog's rule
exists to prevent: the step from *this is how a structure works* to *here is how to recognise a
person*.[^label] The book's counterweight is in the same paragraph — "But even the most evil man is
human and calls for our compassion", and "clinical analysis must not be used to obscure the moral
problem of evil"[^ch13] — but it is a counterweight and not a refusal. The blog will take the first
half of Fromm's closing pages and leave the second.

## 8. Structure or disposition? The question this reading exists for

Here is the question, stated plainly. The blog says **describe structures, not dispositions**. Fromm
says the destructive thing is a **character structure**. Is that a structure, which the blog may
describe, or a disposition, which the blog's discipline refuses to sort by?

The answer this reading reaches is that **it is both, and the blog's rule was never about the first
half of that sentence.** The argument runs in four steps.

**First: in form, it is a structure, and Fromm's term is not loose.** A character structure is
organised ("the specific structure in which human energy is organized in the pursuit of man's
goals"),[^ch10] enduring ("character is man's fate"),[^ch10] formed rather than given ("the malignant
forms of aggression, on the other hand — sadism and necrophilia — are not innate"),[^epi] continuous
rather than typological, and it is not the person's essence — the phrase "the destructive character"
names a dominance, not a membership. Anyone who reads Fromm as a typologist has not read chapter 12's
methodological section, which says in terms that "only a relatively small minority are completely
necrophilous" and that "there is no fixed border between the necrophilous and the biophilous
orientation."[^ch12] The blog's structures are outside the person — a frame, an environment, a position
— and Fromm supplies the case for a structure *inside* one, at the same level of description.

**Second: in behaviour, it is unmistakably a disposition, and Fromm says so with the old vocabulary.**
The sadistic character "looks as if he fitted the hydraulic model of a dammed-up instinct",[^ch3] and
the reason it looks that way is that a character *is* a propensity to act when the occasion comes:

> "In a person with a sadistic character, a sadistic impulse is constantly active, waiting only for a
> proper situation and a fitting rationalization to be acted out."[^ch10]

and, in the sentence that decides it:

> "But only people with a sadistic character wait for the opportunity to behave sadistically, just as
> people with a loving character wait for the opportunity to express their love."[^ch3]

That is a disposition. It is the thing that makes a pattern of occasions intelligible, and it is
precisely the kind of claim the blog's phrase warns against. So the blog's rule is either wrong or it
was never about this.

**Third: it was never about this, and the blog's own best material already proves it.** [The follower
post](/the-follower/) runs the blogger's most careful piece of psychology, and its finding is a
two-term model: "**A latent disposition, plus a condition, produces the behaviour**", and "**The
environment does not create the follower. It decides whether the predisposition is expressed**."[^follower]
That is Fromm's model with different words, arrived at through Stenner's research rather than through
psychoanalysis. The blog has therefore never held that nothing in a person is real; it has held that a
description of a person is not a *sorting* device — that a type becomes harm at the step from "this is
what coercion looks like" to "therefore this person cannot choose". The rule's force is about what
a description may be **used for**, and about which level of description survives contact with the
evidence. It is not a claim that persons are empty.

**Fourth: and this is the correction, and it goes from the blog towards Fromm's side of the ledger.**
The blog has spent a series arguing that the person is dissolved into the position, and that a lever
works whether or not anyone enjoys it. Fromm supplies the mechanism the blog has been missing, and it
runs one level down and in both directions:

> "the power through which one group exploits and keeps down another tends to generate sadism in the
> controlling group, even though there will be many individual exceptions."[^ch11]

Read that as the blog would read it, and it is a structural claim, not a moral one: **the position forms
the person who occupies it.** The blog's account of a predatory frame describes a place and the levers
it supplies; Fromm's account adds that the place is inhabited long enough to leave a shape, and that the
shape is what makes the operation repeatable — a discovered frame can be rebuilt by the next occupant,
an operator who has *become* exploitative cannot easily be talked out of it. And his prediction is
testable in a way that the blog's model already predicts:

> "A person whose character is sadistic will be essentially harmless in an antisadistic society; he
> will be considered to be suffering from an illness."[^ch11]

Take away the condition and the disposition does not express — which is [the follower post](/the-follower/)
again, with a clinical case behind it. And note the harder clause in the same section of the book, which
is where the blog's own hope about what text can do gets its ceiling: "Unless this happens he can be
domesticated, but he cannot be cured."[^intro]

**What the blog therefore owes, stated as the correction it is.** The blog has described predation as a
position and dissolved the person into it; Fromm's case is that **the position also forms the person**,
and that the formed character — not the position alone — is what makes an exploitative structure
durable, repeatable and transferable. The blog may describe that, because it is a structure and not a
verdict, provided it keeps three conditions the book itself supplies: formed and not innate; continuous
and not binary; motive-level and never act-level (a destructive act does not imply a destructive
character). **And it must refuse a fourth step that the book itself takes** — the step from describing
the structure to identifying the person, which is the recognition programme of §7. Fromm, not this blog,
is the one who said "Even the most sadistic and destructive man is human, as human as the saint" and
"he can also be called a man who took the wrong way in search of his salvation."[^intro] Those two
sentences are the blog's rule in Fromm's register. They are also the reason his framework can be taken
without the programme.

## 9. Counterweights, in the body

Five, and each is a thing this reading would be wrong to leave out.

**First, the book's own hedges are substantial, and they are the reason it is not a typology.** Chapter
12's "Clinical-Methodological Principles" opens with the rule that any diagnostic use must start from:
"The presence of one or two traits is insufficient for the diagnosis of a necrophilous character", and
continues that it is "not necessary to find all characteristically necrophilous features together",
that "only a relatively small minority are completely necrophilous", and that most people are the
blend.[^ch12] Chapter 11 says the same thing in the moral register: "it would be a dangerous
oversimplification if I were to classify people into only two groups: the sadist devils and the
nonsadistic saints", and "What matters is the intensity of the sadistic passion within the character
structure of a given person."[^ch11] A framework with those caveats built into its method section is not
the thing a reader fears it is.

**Second, the instruments are named and not validated, and the numbers are judgements.** The clinical
evidence is "observation of persons in analysis"; the additional instruments are a fixed questionnaire
designed with Michael Maccoby, applied to "six very different populations (as to class, race, and
education)", and "projective tests like the Rorschach inkblot test", which "Maccoby has used … with
satisfactory results".[^ch12] The population figure in the book is quoted from Maccoby's paper, and its
own verb is "we judge": "we judge that about 10 to 15 per cent of the samples interviewed would be
dominantly necrophilous."[^ch12] The 10 per cent in the Hitler chapter is introduced as an estimate —
"even if one would estimate".[^ch13] This matters because the blog's standing requirement of its own
mechanism claims is that somebody else can run them. Nothing here can be run by a reader: there is no
published instrument with a false-positive rate in this file, no inter-rater figure, and a central type
whose diagnosis rests on the interpretation of dreams and diction. That does not make it false. It
makes it a *clinical inference*, and a reading must say so.

**Third, the place where the book argues with itself is real and it does not fully close.** The objection
Fromm raises at the end of the technique section — that modern technical man may be "an automaton, but
not a destroyer" — is answered by extending the theory (the marketing character, the shift from smelly
toilets to structures of aluminium and glass), not by testing it.[^ch12] A reader who wants to weigh the
book's central claim about contemporary society should register that its own author posed the strongest
counter-example available and answered it with a new category. That is legitimate theoretical work and
it is not evidence.

**Fourth, the norm. This is the gap the blog cannot paper over.** "Good is all that serves life; evil is
all that serves death" is *declared* as the principle of a biophilic ethics, and "man's biological
constitution is the source of norms for living" is *assumed* as a premise.[^ch12][^ch10] Fromm is
straightforward about this — he presents them as ethics and as premises rather than as findings — and
that candour is why the framework can be read without being adopted. But a norm at the centre of a
character theory is exactly the mechanism by which a description becomes a verdict: once "a man who
took the wrong way in search of his salvation" is a category, some descriptions of people acquire the
force of a judgement about how well they have been a person. The blog describes structures; it has no
standing to say what a life is for, and where a source's account of destructiveness rests on it, the
source is reported and the norm is left where it is.

**Fifth, and this is the counterweight the blog owes itself rather than the book: what actually works here
is the weakest part of the book.** The book's prescription for malignant aggression is social, not
clinical — "they can be substantially reduced when the socioeconomic conditions are replaced by
conditions that are favorable to the full development of man's genuine needs and capacities" — and its
closing pages argue for a "humanist radicalism" that goes "to the roots".[^epi] There is no clinical
route in the book for the person already inside a collapse, no account of what to do tomorrow, and no
placement of the check anywhere a reader can reach it. The blog's own safety material — the traditions'
codes read against the mechanism, and the clinical convergence with them — is doing work this book does
not attempt.[^safe] A reading that called Fromm's remedy sufficient would be trading a description for a
promise.

## 10. The negative finding: safeguard, metered pace, and no clinical referral

The series reports the presence or absence of a **safeguard**, a **metered pace** and a **clinical
referral** as search results, scoped to the file. This book's results are unusual: it is written by a
clinician, in the first person, out of forty years of practice, and it offers its reader no clinical
destination at all.[^abs]

**"Safeguard" occurs zero times.** The only near hit in the whole text is the word "safeguarding", once,
inside a passage from another author's neurophysiology, about food, family and territory. There is no
instruction to a reader anywhere in the book, no stopping rule, no fence.

**"Pace" occurs zero times as a word.** Thirty of the letters appear, and every one of them is the
tail of "space" (the file's dashes are spaced, which is what made the count worth doing by hand). There
is no metered practice in this book to have a pace, which is the same finding as the other readings in a
different register: the only thing the book asks a reader to do is read a study.

**There is no clinical referral at all.** "Referral" zero; "informed consent" zero; "clinic" and
"clinician" zero. The clinical vocabulary is present and enormous — "clinical" 45 times, almost always
as the book's own adjective for its own method — and the rest of it is practitioner vocabulary with the
reader left outside it: "therapy" 12, "psychotherapy" 2, "therapist" 2 (both inside a reported case
history), "patient" 24. "Psychiatry" 8, "psychiatrist(s)" 8, "psychiatric" 5 — and most of those are in
the Bibliography, in footnotes thanking colleagues, or in the book's own argument about the value of
labels. "Doctor" 3: twice the physician who treated Hitler's mother, once Himmler admonishing the
physician-in-chief of the SS. "Hospital" 4: a place a defendant might be sent instead of prison, and one
case history.

**What the book has instead is the reverse of a safeguard, and this is the finding.** Chapter 12's
methodological section closes by asking "would it not be of great social and political significance to
know what percentage of the population can be considered to be predominantly necrophilous or
predominantly biophilous", and calls for an adequate sample studied by a method that
would "permit us to recognize the driving and largely unconscious forces behind manifest behavior and
opinions".[^ch12] The same section names the dangerous class: "severely necrophilous persons are very
dangerous. They are the haters, the racists, those in favor of war, bloodshed, and destruction", and
they "become the executioners, terrorists, torturers", while the less intense ones "are necessary for
the existence of a terror regime because they form a solid basis, although not necessarily a majority,
for it to gain and hold power."[^ch12]

**It is not simply a gap, and the difference is worth stating exactly.** This is the first subject in
the series that offers a **check on its own instrument**: one or two traits are insufficient, not all
features are required, only a small minority are complete cases, there is no fixed border, "there are as
many combinations as there are individuals."[^ch12] That is a real check, and no previous reading's
source has supplied one at this level of care. What the book does not have is any check on a *reader* —
and what it reaches for instead of one is a survey and a list of who is dangerous, which is the thing
the blog's discipline exists to refuse.

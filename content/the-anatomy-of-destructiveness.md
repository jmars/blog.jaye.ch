# The Anatomy of Destructiveness

### Twelfth of the `readings` — the one psychology in the library that refuses both instinct and conditioning, and answers with a character structure that is formed, real, and the hardest thing this blog has had to place

*Twelfth of the `readings`. [The follower post](/the-follower/) found the follower to be a position
rather than a personality, and warned that the instrument which would sort people is the instrument
that fails; [the Lucifer Effect post](/the-lucifer-effect/) read the social psychology the blog had
been running unnamed and found the study underneath it broken; [Eichmann in Jerusalem](/eichmann-in-jerusalem/)
took the blog's most-quoted phrase back to its source and found a faculty where the blog had assumed a
structure. This is the third book the tree's own planning note files in that group, and the one the
blog has never cited at all — a psychology that refuses the position-versus-personality split from the
other side, argues that something **inside the person** is also real and describable, and would put it
on a scale and count it.*

*Status.* This post reads **one** source. The quotations are verbatim from a complete digital text of that
edition, checked character for character against it and cited in the notes **by chapter and section**,
because the text has no usable page flow; the reading of them is an **inference**, offered as one. **The book's
thesis is not endorsed**, and §8 and §9 are about why a reading of it cannot endorse it. The case
material — Stalin, Himmler, Hitler — is **reported as the book's content and not adjudicated here**, and
no living person is diagnosed. Nothing here validates or disqualifies anyone's experience, and nothing
here is a checklist for recognising anybody. As everywhere: describe structures, diagnose no one.*

---

## 1. The book, and the debt it names

*The Anatomy of Human Destructiveness* is by Erich Fromm. The copyright page gives it exactly: first
published by Holt, Rinehart and Winston, New York, **1973**; the text follows the revised edition as Owl
Book (Henry Holt and Company, New York) **1992**; (c) 1973 by Erich Fromm, (c) 1992 by the Estate of
Erich Fromm; this edition published in 2013 by Open Road Integrated Media; ISBN 978-1-4804-0193-8.[^ed]
The Contents gives the structure: Preface, Terminology, Introduction; **Part I — Instinctivism,
Behaviorism, Psychoanalysis** (chs. 1-4); **Part II — The Evidence Against the Instinctivist Thesis**
(chs. 5-8); **Part III — The Varieties of Aggression and Destructiveness and Their Respective
Conditions** (chs. 9-13, ending with a chapter each on cruelty, on necrophilia, and on Hitler); an
**Appendix** on Freud; and a closing **Epilogue: On the Ambiguity of Hope**.[^file]

The text's sections stand out of book order and its running heads are unreliable. That is why the
citations below are by chapter and section, the way the other readings have handled damaged texts.[^file]

Two facts belong before the reading. **The blog has never cited him**: a case-insensitive search for
"Fromm" across `content/` returns nothing, not one line, in any post.[^cite] He appears in the tree only
in the planning note that files him with Arendt and Zimbardo as unmined, and in the build plumbing for
this post.

**And he is the hardest of them to place.** The blog's method has two standing forms. The first is
*describe structures, diagnose no one*, which forbids the diagnosis of a person. The second is stronger:
the blog has read predation as a **position**, in three registers — the levers are "positions rather than
beliefs",[^costume] the follower is "a position, not a personality",[^follower] the machine's reading
"describes a position, not a type".[^machine] On that account the person is *dissolved into the
position*: what makes the operation work is not what is in anybody's head but where everybody stands.

Fromm is the serious psychological answer from the opposite side, and it is not naive. He is not saying
people are born bad; he is saying that whatever is in the person is **formed by conditions and by the
person's own response to them**, that it is organised, that it persists, and that it can be described.
What he names is neither an instinct nor a circumstance. It is a **character structure** — and the
question of this reading is what the blog does with that.

## 2. What the book is arguing against, in its own words

Fromm names two opponents and refuses both. The first is the instinctivists — Lorenz's *On Aggression*
(1966), Ardrey, Morris, Eibl-Eibesfeldt. Their thesis:

> "man's aggressive behavior as manifested in war, crime, personal quarrels, and all kinds of destructive
> and sadistic behavior is due to a phylogenetically programmed, innate instinct which seeks for
> discharge and waits for the proper occasion to be expressed."[^intro]

and his reading of why it sells is not flattering: **"This theory of an innate aggressiveness easily
becomes an ideology that helps to soothe the fear of what is to happen and to rationalize the sense of
impotence."[^intro]**

The second is behaviourism — Skinner's specifically. Fromm's complaint is about a subject matter: "the
subject matter of the dominant school in psychology became behavior, not the behaving man."[^intro] The
two schools, he says, are both closed: "Both positions are 'monoexplanatory,' depending on dogmatic
preconceptions."[^intro] Then the book's sentence of scope: **"This book affirms that there is another
option, and examines the question of what it is."[^intro]**

What they have in common is the point, and it is where Fromm becomes interesting to this blog rather than
merely useful. Chapter 3, "A Common Ground", states the shared premise: both assume **"that man has no
psyche with its own structure and its own laws"**, and both "exclude the person, the behaving man, from
their field of vision."[^ch3] Then the image, which is easy to mistake for rhetoric and is not:

> "Whether man is the product of conditioning, or the product of animal evolution, he is exclusively
> determined by conditions outside himself; he has no part in his own life, no responsibility, and not
> even a trace of freedom. Man is a puppet, controlled by strings — instinct or conditioning."[^ch3]

**The part of this the blog owes is not the thesis; it is the reflexivity.** Fromm does not leave his
opponents' theories as theories. He reads eighteenth-century environmentalism as the revolutionary middle
class's theory of itself and nineteenth-century instinctivism as "the basic assumption of
nineteenth-century capitalism" — a natural order proved by nature — with the twentieth century's turn
away from it explained by a change in the mode of control. Then he notes the asymmetry in the critics: he
quotes the psychoanalyst Robert Waelder exposing the bias of the anti-instinctivists, and observes that
Waelder "only sees the bias of the anti-instinctivists and not of those who share his own position."[^ch3]

That is the blog's own kind of move, and Fromm makes it against both sides *including his own* — which is
worth registering, because the rest of this post presses on the one place where he does not.

On Freud he is neither disciple nor prosecutor. He keeps the Freud who found the passions and drops the
Freud who explained them: "I had been deeply impressed by his concept that the striving for life and the
striving for destruction were the two most fundamental forces within man; but I could not reconcile
myself to Freud's theoretical explanation."[^ch12] What he shifts is the ground of explanation — "from
Freud's physiological to a sociobiological and historical principle"[^intro] — and the Appendix at the
back of the book is the fullest statement of what he keeps and drops.

## 3. The two aggressions, and a word that is doing two jobs

The book's first distinction is the one everything else rests on:

> "We must distinguish in man two entirely different kinds of aggression. The first, which he shares
> with all animals, is a phylogenetically programmed impulse to attack (or to flee) when vital interests
> are threatened. This defensive, 'benign' aggression is in the service of the survival of the
> individual and the species, is biologically adaptive, and ceases when the threat has ceased to exist.
> The other type, 'malignant' aggression, i.e., cruelty and destructiveness, is specific to the human
> species and virtually absent in most mammals; it is not phylogenetically programmed and not
> biologically adaptive; it has no purpose, and its satisfaction is lustful."[^intro]

Three clauses are load-bearing. *Virtually absent in most mammals* obliges the four empirical chapters of
Part II. *Not phylogenetically programmed* is the direct denial of Lorenz. And **"it has no purpose, and
its satisfaction is lustful"** should stop a reader of this blog, because the blog's account of
predation is entirely instrumental — a lever works whether or not anyone enjoys it, and the position
that exploits is described precisely so that motive never has to be invoked. Fromm's malignant aggression
is the opposite: purposeless by his own definition, and therefore sensible only as a claim about a
person.

The species claim follows unhedged — "he is the only primate that kills and tortures members of his own
species without any reason, either biological or economic, and who feels satisfaction in doing
so"[^intro] — and then the empirical thesis: human groups differ too much for innateness to explain it,
destructiveness correlates with social structure, "and (3) the degree of destructiveness increases with
the increased development of civilization, rather than the opposite."[^intro]

**And the disciplinary move, which the blog already runs without knowing whose it is.** The book opens
with a short section called **Terminology**, and it is a refusal to keep using a word that is doing two
jobs. Once "aggression" covers a man defending his life, a robber killing for money and a sadist
torturing a prisoner, "there is no hope of understanding their 'cause'; they have no common cause because
they are entirely different phenomena."[^term] He then sets out Lorenz's argument as a syllogism and lets
it stand:

> "The word 'aggression' serves conveniently as a bridge to connect biologically adaptive aggression
> (which is not evil) with human destructiveness which indeed is evil. The core of this kind of
> 'reasoning' is: Biologically adaptive aggression = innate
> Destructiveness and cruelty = aggression
> Ergo: Destructiveness and cruelty = innate. Q.E.D."[^term]

His own rule follows: "aggression" is reserved for defensive, reactive aggression, and "destructiveness
and cruelty" for "the specifically human propensity to destroy and to crave for absolute control".[^term]
The blog's own post on the word "cult" makes exactly this argument, and it should be said plainly that
the blog did not invent that discipline and did not arrive at it alone; Fromm is running it here, five
decades earlier, on a word that was doing more damage.[^label]

**One collision of vocabulary needs flagging.** Fromm uses *predation* for a biological feeding mode, in
a section headed "Predation and Aggression": predatory animals "comprise the families of cats, hyenas,
wolves, and bears"; "The predatory instinct is not one of defense, common to all animals, but of
food-finding"; and his conclusion, the opposite of Ardrey's famous title, is that **"man is
phylogenetically a nonpredatory animal."[^ch5]** The blog's *predation* is not that. It is the name for a
position in a structure, and it is explicitly not a claim about appetite, species or biology.[^costume]
The words are the same and share nothing; a reading that let them be confused would end up arguing that
the blog had called its operators cats.

## 4. Character as the third term — and the norm that comes with it

Between instinct and conditioning Fromm inserts a third thing, and it is the book's actual subject:

> "The distinction between benign-defensive and malignant-destructive aggression calls for a further
> and more fundamental distinction, that between instinct and character" — the text's own footnote
> digit falls here — "or more precisely, between drives rooted in man's physiological needs (organic
> drives) and those specifically human passions rooted in his character ('character-rooted, or human
> passions')."[^intro]

He says what character is *for*: it is "man's 'second nature,' the substitute for his poorly developed
instincts", and the character-rooted passions "are answers to 'existential needs,' which in turn are
rooted in the very conditions of human existence."[^intro] Instincts answer physiological needs;
character answers needs that arise from being the kind of creature that has to make sense of having been
born. It is a category of explanation, not a metaphor: "Instincts are a purely natural category, while
the character-rooted passions are a sociobiological, historical category."[^intro]

Chapter 10's section "Character Structure" gives the fullest statement, and then an example chosen to be
unremarkable:

> "Character is the specific structure in which human energy is organized in the pursuit of man's goals;
> it motivates behavior according to its dominant goals: a person acts 'instinctively,' we say, in
> accordance with his character. To use Heraclitus's phrase, character is man's fate."[^ch10]

"The miser does not ponder whether he should save or spend; he is driven to save and to hoard".[^ch10]
The structure is not something a person consults; it is what makes their conduct look to them like
nature. The existential account explains why anyone would choose the destructive answer: "The need for
effectiveness can be answered by love, productive work — or by sadism and destructiveness", and "Man
seeks for drama and excitement; when he cannot get satisfaction on a higher level, he creates for himself
the drama of destruction."[^intro] Destructiveness here is a solution to a problem, and a cheap one —
"for destruction all that is necessary is strong arms, or a knife, or a gun."[^ch12]

**The formation story is what does the work against innatism**, and Fromm tells it as a sliding scale:

> "the individual begins life with certain qualities that dispose him to go in certain directions, but
> his personality is still malleable enough to allow the character to develop in many different
> directions within the given framework. Every step in life narrows down the number of possible future
> outcomes. … Eventually, the freedom to change becomes so minimal that only a miracle would seem
> capable of effecting a change."[^ch13]

That answers both opponents at once. Against the instinctivists: what starts early is a set of
*dispositions to develop*, not a programme. Against the behaviourists: there is something there, and it
accumulates — which is why later events must be more violent than earlier ones to move it. He names the
social half of the mechanism too, and coins a term for it: "social character", the process by which "each
form of society (or social class) needs to use human energy in the specific manner necessary for the
functioning of that particular society."[^ch10]

**And here is the thing the blog has to mark, because it is where a structure becomes a verdict.** The
same chapter that describes character as formed also carries a **norm**, stated as a premise:

> "The position taken here assumes that man has an immanent goal, that man's biological constitution is
> the source of norms for living. He has the possibility for full development and growth, provided the
> external conditions that are given are conducive to this aim."[^ch10]

From that premise fall the two syndromes — the "life-furthering" and the "life-thwarting" — the
vocabulary of "a crippled, stunted man", and the claim that necrophilia is "the outcome of unlived life,
of the failure to arrive at a certain stage beyond narcissism and indifference."[^ch12] He knows how this
will be heard: "It is truly astonishing that this view should be considered 'idealistic' or
'unscientific' by so many who would not dream of questioning the relation between constitution and norms
in regard to physical development and health."[^ch10]

The blog cannot run that premise — not because it is falsifiable, but because it is a norm, and a norm is
what turns a description into a ranking. That is the risk the brief named, and it is in the book rather
than in its reception. What keeps it from being fatal is that Fromm spends the rest of the chapter
qualifying it; §9 collects those qualifications.

## 5. Necrophilia, at the book's own scale

Chapter 12 is the famous one, and the blog's job is to report it as the book states it — including where
the book goes too far.

**Provenance first, because it is not Freud's.** Fromm took the word from a speech. In 1936, at the
University of Salamanca, the Nationalist general Millan Astray's motto — "Long live death!" — was shouted
from the hall, and the rector, Miguel de Unamuno, answered him, naming the thing:

> "The term 'necrophilous,' to denote a character trait rather than a perverse act in the traditional
> sense, was used by the Spanish philosopher Miguel de Unamuno in 1936"[^ch12]

and then, in a footnote in the book's own voice: "I adopted the use of the term from Unamuno and have
been studying the phenomenon of character-rooted necrophilia since about 1961."[^ch12] The move from the
older, clinical sense — acts with a corpse — to a character trait is his, and he says so. He says where
his data came from too, without dressing it up: "My theoretical concepts were gained mainly by
observation of persons in analysis."[^ch12]

**The definition is one sentence, and it is the sentence to argue with:**

> "Necrophilia in the characterological sense can be described as the passionate attraction to all that
> is dead, decayed, putrid, sickly; it is the passion to transform that which is alive into something
> unalive; to destroy for the sake of destruction; the exclusive interest in all that is purely
> mechanical. It is the passion to tear apart living structures."[^ch12]

Note what is in it and what is not. There is no violence in the definition. *Order*, *control* and *the
mechanical* — the three things a reader of this blog would expect in a description of a predatory frame —
are there, and they are there as the **opposite of life**, not as methods. And the last clause gives the
book its shape: the passion is to **tear apart living structures**. His biophilia will be "structures
rather than summations" (§6). The axis is not good against bad. It is living organisation against its
dismemberment.

**What the character does**, in the book's catalogue, is a list rather than a syndrome, and its
ordinariness is deliberate: the conviction "that the only way to solve a problem or a conflict is by
force and violence", with force — "as Simone Weil said, 'the power to transform a man into a corpse'" —
as "the first and the last solution for everything", and the queen's answer in *Alice in Wonderland*,
"off with their heads!";[^ch12] a marked interest in sickness and death, the obituaries read first; and
the relation to time and property, compressed into three clauses: **"Briefly, things rule man; having
rules being; the dead rule the living."[^ch12]**

**The language section is where the blog's own material sits closest.** Fromm's finding is that "the
language of the necrophilous person is characterized by the predominant use of words referring to
destruction and to feces and toilets", and his evidence is a questionnaire answer: asked about women's
use of lipstick and make-up, a majority answered in the prevailing ideology — "It is bourgeois",
"unnatural", "not hygienic" — while a minority answered "It is poisonous" or "It makes women look like
whores", and "almost invariably, the respondents who used these words showed a destructive trend in most
other answers."[^ch12] Whether a questionnaire about cosmetics can carry that is not something this
reading can settle; what is worth noting is that it is a finding about **diction as a symptom** — the
same surface the blog's posts on loaded vocabulary work on, reached from underneath rather than from
outside.

**Chapter 12's most interesting structure is a derivation, and also its weakest joint.** Fromm derives
necrophilia from the anal character — "necrophilia can be described as the malignant form of the anal
character"[^ch12] — and then hedges the neatness himself: "But the connections are by no means so neat."
He goes further and notices that the type he built it from is disappearing, so that "the character of the
new type of man does not seem to fit into any of the older categories", and proposes a **marketing
character** for whom everything, even his smiles, is a commodity.[^ch12]

**And then the sentence the blog has no analogue for.** Writing about what mechanised killing does to the
killer, Fromm says the technicalisation of destruction removes "the full affective recognition of what
one is doing", and then:

> "Once this process has been fully established there is no limit to destructiveness because nobody
> destroys: one only serves the machine for programmed — hence, apparently rational — purposes."[^ch12]

*Nobody destroys: one only serves the machine.* That is the blog's own description of a position, written
by an author who does not believe positions are enough. It is ambiguous in a productive way: it can mean
the person has been replaced by a function, or that the person's passion has been routed through a
function so that it no longer has to be felt. Fromm notices the ambiguity and raises it against his own
thesis — whether contemporary technical man "is not motivated by a passion for destruction, but would be
more properly described as a totally alienated man whose dominant orientation is cerebral … who has
become, in a characterological sense, an automaton, but not a destroyer?"[^ch12] His answer includes the
book's most quoted image of modern necrophilia, and it is not a corpse: **"Its symbols are now clean,
shining machines; men are not attracted to smelly toilets, but to structures of aluminum and glass."[^ch12]**

## 6. Biophilia, and the alternative

The opposite orientation arrives late and briefly, at the end of chapter 12:

> "Biophilia is the passionate love of life and of all that is alive; it is the wish to further growth,
> whether in a person, a plant, an idea, or a social group."[^ch12]

Two clauses in the list that follows are gifts to a blog whose subject is structures. The biophilous
person "wants to be more rather than to have more … He loves the adventure of living more than he does
certainty", and then: **"He sees the whole rather than only the parts, structures rather than
summations."[^ch12]** That is Fromm, in 1973, in nine words: a structure is not the sum of its parts, and
seeing it is a capacity rather than a method.

The book then declares an ethics in the same voice, and is honest that it is one. "Biophilic ethics have
their own principle of good and evil. Good is all that serves life; evil is all that serves death."[^ch12]
That is a definition, not a measurement — which is the least a reader can ask of a norm, and it is still
a norm; §9 returns to it.

**The relation to Freud is the technical point.** Fromm does not say the two orientations are equal
forces, and it is on this that he departs from the death-instinct theory: "in Freud's concept both
tendencies have equal rank, as it were, both being biologically given. Biophilia, on the other hand, is
understood to refer to a biologically normal impulse, while necrophilia is understood as a
psychopathological phenomenon."[^ch12] So the asymmetry is his contribution: one is what the organism is
for, the other is what happens when it is stunted. Hence the sentence that gives the book its moral
shape:

> "Destructiveness is not parallel to, but the alternative to biophilia. Love of life or love of the dead
> is the fundamental alternative that confronts every human being. … Man is biologically endowed with
> the capacity for biophilia, but psychologically he has the potential for necrophilia as an alternative
> solution."[^ch12]

and the mechanism by which the alternative gets chosen, which is the closest the book comes to the blog's
account of how easy a frame is to enter: "If man cannot create anything or move anybody, if he cannot
break out of the prison of his total narcissism and isolation, he can escape the unbearable sense of
vital impotence and nothingness only by affirming himself in the act of destruction of the life that he
is unable to create."[^ch12]

**The counterweight belongs here rather than at the end**, because this is the passage the book's whole
normative apparatus is built on: an alternative available to every human being, a capacity that is given
and a pathology that is a failure to arrive. It is a complete moral psychology — the strongest thing in
the book — and the blog will not take it, because taking it means holding a specification of what a
person is for, and the first thing the blog's discipline forbids is a specification against which a
person can be measured. What the blog can take is the *structure* of the claim: one orientation is not
the negation of the other but its **substitute**, chosen when the first is unavailable, and cheap.

## 7. The three cases, and what a case is for here

Part III contains four clinical case studies of named historical figures — Stalin ("a Clinical Case of
Nonsexual Sadism"), Himmler ("a Clinical Case of Anal-Hoarding Sadism"), and Hitler ("a Clinical Case of
Necrophilia"), the central exhibit.

**The method is stated in three moves.** An "analytic psychobiographical study" asks two questions: the
driving forces that motivate a person, and "the conditions — internal and external — responsible for the
development of these specific passions (character traits)."[^ch13] The evidence base is deliberately not
behavioural: "the analysis of dreams, unintended behavior, gestures, language, and behavior that is
rationally not fully explainable permits one to form a picture of the essential and mostly unconscious
passions ('X-ray approach')."[^ch13] And the point of the exercise is the one the blog must not lose:

> "The question raised in this book is not concerned with behavior, but with character. … the question is
> not whether Hitler behaved destructively, but whether he was motivated by an intense passion to
> destroy, a passion for destruction that was part of his character, this has to be proven, not taken for
> granted."[^ch13]

with the premise underneath it: **"destructive actions are not necessarily manifestations of a
destructive, necrophilous character."[^ch13]** He argues it by counter-example — Napoleon, and the many
commanders who ordered destruction — and the argument is genuinely strong. A general who sacrifices his
soldiers for ambition is not thereby in love with death.

**This is the load-bearing collision with the blog.** The blog's rule is that you read the position and
never the motive, because the motive is what cannot be read and what a description becomes when it is
misused. Fromm's method is the exact inverse: conduct tells you almost nothing, "the question raised in
this book is not concerned with behavior", and the work is to reconstruct the motive from dreams, slips,
gestures and the pattern of a life. Both accept that destructiveness is real; they disagree about what a
description is *of* — a place somebody stands, or a structure somebody has. Nothing here resolves that;
§8 and §9 are about how far it can be carried.

**What he does with the evidence is best seen in the section on repression, because it is a real
argument.** Hitler, he says, repressed the awareness of his destructiveness by rationalising every order
as defensive, and then by reaction formation — the mechanism in which a person denies a drive "by
developing traits that are exactly the opposite."[^ch13] His examples are vegetarianism, begun after the
suicide of Geli Raubal, and a phobia of corpses that kept Hitler from the front: "In my opinion this
phobic reaction to seeing corpses is a defense reaction against the awareness of his own destructiveness.
As long as he only gave and signed orders, he had only spoken and written."[^ch13] *He had only spoken
and written* — the same observation the blog's posts make about the operator who never touches anybody,
arrived at from the opposite direction, through a psychology of motive.

He is scrupulous about his sources, too: "Maser, though a historian, is often unreliable in the use of
his sources", "Smith is by far the most objective and reliable source for Hitler's youth",[^ch13] and
Speer's recollections recur as "A. Speer, personal communication." He refuses the madness verdict:
"in conventional terms Hitler was not a psychotic man, in dynamic, interpersonal terms he was a very sick
man",[^ch13] and the labelling question is answered with a passage his own profession has to sit under —
"the questionable value of psychiatric labels … in the last analysis what we are dealing with are
interpersonal processes that defy such labels."[^ch13]

**And then the thing the blog has to record, because it is the book's own sentence and not a reading
imposed on it.** The chapter closes by stating its purpose:

> "I had still another aim: that of pointing to the main fallacy which prevents people from recognizing
> potential Hitlers before they have shown their true faces. This fallacy lies in the belief that a
> thoroughly destructive and evil man must be a devil — and look his part … as long as one believes
> that the evil man wears horns, one will not discover an evil man."[^ch13]

followed by an estimate offered as an estimate — "even if one would estimate that such persons formed 10
percent of our population, there are enough of them to be very dangerous if they attain influence and
power" — and the line "there are probably hundreds of Hitlers among us who would come forth if their
historical hour arrived."[^ch13]

That is a detection programme. It is offered in good faith, and it is exactly the move the blog's rule
exists to prevent: the step from *this is how a structure works* to *here is how to recognise a
person*.[^label] The book's counterweight sits in the paragraph just before it — "But even the most evil man is
human and calls for our compassion", and "clinical analysis must not be used to obscure the moral problem
of evil"[^ch13] — but it is a counterweight, not a refusal. The blog will take the first half of Fromm's
closing pages and leave the second.

## 8. Structure or disposition? The question this reading exists for

The question, plainly. The blog says **describe structures, not dispositions**. Fromm says the
destructive thing is a **character structure**. Is that a structure, which the blog may describe, or a
disposition, which the blog's discipline refuses to sort by?

The answer this reading reaches is that **it is both, and the blog's rule was never about the first half
of that sentence.** Four steps.

**One: in form it is a structure, and the term is not loose.** It is organised ("the specific structure
in which human energy is organized in the pursuit of man's goals"),[^ch10] enduring ("character is man's
fate"),[^ch10] formed rather than given ("the malignant forms of aggression, on the other hand — sadism
and necrophilia — are not innate"),[^epi] and continuous rather than typological: "only a relatively
small minority are completely necrophilous" and "there is no fixed border between the necrophilous and
the biophilous orientation."[^ch12] "The destructive character" names a dominance, not a membership. The
blog's structures are outside the person — a frame, an environment, a position — and Fromm supplies the
case for a structure *inside* one, at the same level of description.

**Two: in behaviour it is unmistakably a disposition, and Fromm says so with the old vocabulary.** The
sadistic character "looks as if he fitted the hydraulic model of a dammed-up instinct",[^ch3] and the
reason it looks that way is that a character *is* a propensity to act when the occasion comes: "In a
person with a sadistic character, a sadistic impulse is constantly active, waiting only for a proper
situation and a fitting rationalization to be acted out."[^ch10] Then the sentence that decides it:

> "But only people with a sadistic character wait for the opportunity to behave sadistically, just as
> people with a loving character wait for the opportunity to express their love."[^ch3]

That is a disposition — the thing that makes a pattern of occasions intelligible, and precisely the kind
of claim the blog's phrase warns against. So the rule is either wrong, or it was never about this.

**Three: it was never about this, and the blog's own best material already proves it.** [The follower
post](/the-follower/) runs the blog's most careful piece of psychology, and its finding is a two-term
model: "**A latent disposition, plus a condition, produces the behaviour**", and "**The environment does
not create the follower. It decides whether the predisposition is expressed**."[^follower] That is
Fromm's model in different words, arrived at through Stenner's research rather than psychoanalysis. The
blog has therefore never held that nothing in a person is real; it has held that a description of a
person is not a *sorting* device — that a type becomes harm at the step from "this is what coercion looks
like" to "therefore this person cannot choose".[^label] The rule's force is about what a description may
be **used for**, and about which level of description survives contact with the evidence. It is not a
claim that persons are empty.

**Four: and this is the correction, and it runs from the blog towards Fromm's side of the ledger.** The
blog has argued that the person is dissolved into the position, and that a lever works whether or not
anyone enjoys it. Fromm supplies the mechanism the blog has been missing, and it runs one level down:

> "the power through which one group exploits and keeps down another tends to generate sadism in the
> controlling group, even though there will be many individual exceptions."[^ch11]

Read as the blog would read it, that is a structural claim: **the position forms the person who occupies
it.** The blog's account of a predatory frame describes a place and the levers it supplies; Fromm's adds
that the place is inhabited long enough to leave a shape, and that the shape is what makes the operation
repeatable — a discovered frame can be rebuilt by the next occupant, an operator who has *become*
exploitative cannot easily be talked out of it. And his prediction is one the blog's model already makes:

> "A person whose character is sadistic will be essentially harmless in an antisadistic society; he will
> be considered to be suffering from an illness."[^ch11]

Take away the condition and the disposition does not express — which is [the follower post](/the-follower/)
again, with a clinical case behind it. And note the harder clause, in the Introduction, which is where the
blog's hope about what text can do gets its ceiling: "Unless this happens he can be domesticated, but he
cannot be cured."[^intro]

**What the blog therefore owes, stated as the correction it is.** The blog has described predation as a
position and dissolved the person into it; Fromm's case is that **the position also forms the person**,
and that the formed character — not the position alone — is what makes an exploitative structure durable,
repeatable and transferable. The blog may describe that, because it is a structure and not a verdict,
provided it keeps three conditions the book itself supplies: formed and not innate; continuous and not
binary; and formed by life-conditions rather than given. **And it must refuse a fourth step that the book itself takes**
— from describing the structure to identifying the person, which is the recognition programme of §7.
Fromm, not this blog, is the one who wrote that "Even the most sadistic and destructive man is human, as
human as the saint" and that he "can also be called a man who took the wrong way in search of his
salvation."[^intro] Those two sentences are the blog's rule in Fromm's register, and they are the reason
his framework can be taken without the programme.

## 9. Counterweights, in the body

Five, and each is a thing this reading would be wrong to leave out.

**First, the book's own hedges are substantial, and they are why it is not a typology.** Chapter 12's
methodological section opens with the rule any diagnostic use must start from: "The presence of one or
two traits is insufficient for the diagnosis of a necrophilous character", and continues that it is "not
necessary to find all characteristically necrophilous features together", that most people are the blend,
and that "there are as many combinations as there are individuals."[^ch12] Chapter 11 says it in the
moral register: "it would be a dangerous oversimplification if I were to classify people into only two
groups: the sadist devils and the nonsadistic saints", and "What matters is the intensity of the sadistic
passion within the character structure of a given person."[^ch11]

**Second, the instruments are named and not validated, and the numbers are judgements.** The clinical
evidence is "observation of persons in analysis"; the other instruments are a fixed questionnaire
designed with Michael Maccoby, applied to "six very different populations (as to class, race, and
education)", and "projective tests like the Rorschach inkblot test", which "Maccoby has used … with
satisfactory results."[^ch12] The population figure is quoted from Maccoby's paper and its own verb is
"we judge": "we judge that about 10 to 15 per cent of the samples interviewed would be dominantly
necrophilous."[^ch12] The 10 per cent in the Hitler chapter is introduced as an estimate — "even if one
would estimate".[^ch13] The blog's standing requirement of its own mechanism claims is that somebody else
can run them. Nothing here can be run by a reader: no published instrument with a false-positive rate in
this file, no inter-rater figure, and a central type diagnosed by the interpretation of dreams and
diction. That does not make it false; it makes it a clinical inference, and a reading must say so.

**Third, the place where the book argues with itself is real and does not fully close.** The objection
Fromm raises at the end of the technique section — that modern technical man may be "an automaton, but not
a destroyer" — is answered by extending the theory, not by testing it.[^ch12] Its own author posed the
strongest counter-example available and answered it with a new category. That is legitimate theoretical
work and it is not evidence.

**Fourth, the norm, and this is the gap the blog cannot paper over.** "Good is all that serves life; evil
is all that serves death" is *declared* as the principle of a biophilic ethics, and "man's biological
constitution is the source of norms for living" is *assumed* as a premise.[^ch12][^ch10] Fromm is
straightforward that these are ethics and premises rather than findings, and that candour is why the
framework can be read without being adopted. But a norm at the centre of a character theory is exactly
how a description becomes a verdict: once "a man who took the wrong way in search of his salvation" is a
category, some descriptions of people acquire the force of a judgement about how well they have been a
person. The blog describes structures; it has no standing to say what a life is for, and where a source's
account of destructiveness rests on that, the source is reported and the norm is left where it is.

**A fifth counterweight, and it is the one the blog owes itself rather than the book: what actually works
is the weakest part of the book.** Its prescription for malignant aggression is social, not clinical —
the malignant forms "are not innate; hence, they can be substantially reduced when the socioeconomic
conditions are replaced by conditions that are favorable to the full development of man's genuine needs
and capacities"[^epi] — and its closing pages argue for a "humanist radicalism" that goes "to the
roots".[^epi] There is no clinical route in the book for the person already inside a collapse, no account
of what to do tomorrow, and no placement of the check anywhere a reader can reach it. The blog's own
safety material is doing work this book does not attempt, and a reading that called Fromm's remedy
sufficient would be trading a description for a promise.[^safe]

## 10. The negative finding: safeguard, metered pace, and no clinical referral

The series reports the presence or absence of a **safeguard**, a **metered pace** and a **clinical
referral** as search results, scoped to the text of this edition. This book's results are unusual: it is written by a
clinician, in the first person, out of decades of practice, and it offers its reader no clinical
destination at all.[^abs]

**"Safeguard" occurs zero times.** The only near hit is "safeguarding", once, inside a passage from
another author's neurophysiology about food, family and territory. There is no instruction to a reader
anywhere in the book, no stopping rule, no fence.

**"Pace" occurs zero times as a word.** Thirty occurrences of the letters, every one of them the tail of
"space". There is no metered practice here to have a pace, which is the same finding as the other
readings in a different register: the only thing the book asks a reader to do is read a study.

**There is no clinical referral at all.** "Referral" zero; "informed consent" zero; "clinic" and
"clinician" zero. The clinical vocabulary is present and enormous — "clinical" 45 times, almost always
the book's own adjective for its own method — and the rest is practitioner vocabulary with the reader
left outside it: "therapy" 12, "psychotherapy" 2, "therapist" 2, both inside a reported case history,
"patient" 24. "Psychiatry" 8, "psychiatrist(s)" 8, "psychiatric" 5, and most of those are in the
Bibliography, in footnotes thanking colleagues, or in the book's own argument about the value of labels.
"Doctor" 3: twice the physician who treated Hitler's mother, once Himmler admonishing the SS
physician-in-chief. "Hospital" 4: a place a defendant might be sent instead of prison, sampling ("hospital inmates"), the Stalin passage, and one case
history.

**What the book has instead is the reverse of a safeguard, and this is the finding.** Chapter 12's
methodological section asks, as the sixth of its numbered questions, "would it not be of great social and political significance to
know what percentage of the population can be considered to be predominantly necrophilous or
predominantly biophilous", and calls for an adequate sample studied by a method that would "permit us to
recognize the driving and largely unconscious forces behind manifest behavior and opinions."[^ch12] The
same section names the dangerous class: "severely necrophilous persons are very dangerous. They are the
haters, the racists, those in favor of war, bloodshed, and destruction", and they "become the
executioners, terrorists, torturers", while the less intense ones "are necessary for the existence of a
terror regime because they form a solid basis, although not necessarily a majority, for it to gain and
hold power."[^ch12]

**It is not simply a gap, and the difference is worth stating exactly.** This is the first subject in the
series that offers a **check on its own instrument**: one or two traits are insufficient, not all
features are required, only a small minority are complete cases, there is no fixed border. That is a real
check, and no earlier reading's source has supplied one at this level of care. What the book does not
have is any check on a *reader* — and what it reaches for instead is a survey and a list of who is
dangerous, which is the thing the blog's discipline exists to refuse.
